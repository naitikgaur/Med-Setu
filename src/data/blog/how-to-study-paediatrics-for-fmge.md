**Paediatrics for FMGE** is a compact, fact-dense subject: it carries **15 questions out of 300** in the examination conducted by NBEMS. The paper has two parts of 150 MCQs, no negative marking, and a pass mark of 150. What makes paediatrics different from the adult subjects is that so much of it is anchored to numbers that change with age — milestones, vaccine schedules, growth parameters, drug-relevant physiology. Students who build clean tables for these score steadily; students who read passively forget them within weeks. This guide lays out the areas that matter and a method for keeping the facts alive.

## The four pillars of paediatrics for FMGE

Almost every paediatrics question falls into one of four groups:

- **Growth and development** — developmental milestones by age, growth charts, head circumference, and red-flag delays
- **Immunisation** — the National Immunisation Schedule, vaccine types, contraindications and cold chain basics
- **Neonatology** — the newborn examination, APGAR score, common problems of the newborn and their first management steps
- **Common childhood diseases** — presentations and management of the conditions every undergraduate syllabus emphasises

If your time is short, work through the pillars in that order. Milestones and immunisation are nearly pure recall, and recall questions are the fastest marks in the paper.

## Development milestones: learn them as a timeline

Do not memorise milestones as isolated flashcards. Lay them out as a single timeline at 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 24 months across the four domains — gross motor, fine motor, language and social. Learn the anchor milestones first: social smile, head holding, sitting without support, walking, first words, and the pincer grasp. Then attach the neighbouring milestones to each anchor. Examiners favour the boundary questions — the child who is *not* doing something by a given age — so for every milestone also note the age by which its absence is a red flag.

## Immunisation and preventive paediatrics

The **National Immunisation Schedule** (under the Universal Immunisation Programme) is a guaranteed area. Learn it as a table by age: birth, 6, 10 and 14 weeks, 9 months, 16–24 months, and the school-age boosters. For each vaccine know the type (live, killed, toxoid), the route and one classic contraindication or caution. Live vaccines — BCG, OPV, measles/MR, rotavirus — attract questions about immunocompromised children. Add the basics of the cold chain and vaccine vial monitor, which appear regularly in the community-health overlap.

## Neonatology and common childhood illness

In neonatology, concentrate on the APGAR score and its components, neonatal resuscitation priorities, physiological versus pathological jaundice, and common problems such as respiratory distress, sepsis risk factors and feeding difficulties. For older children, the repeatedly examined conditions include:

- **Protein-energy malnutrition** — kwashiorkor versus marasmus, and the classification systems
- **Acute respiratory infections and diarrhoea** — severity classification and the role of ORS and zinc
- **Congenital heart disease** — the cyanotic versus acyanotic split, with tetralogy of Fallot as the classic cyanotic lesion
- **Nephrotic syndrome, rheumatic fever and common infections** such as measles, mumps and pertussis

Management questions in paediatrics usually test the *first* step or the *most appropriate next* step, so learn each condition as a short sequence rather than a paragraph.

## Books and study method

**OP Ghai's Essential Pediatrics** is the standard text; for FMGE, use it to build your milestone, immunisation and growth tables and to clarify anything your revision notes leave vague. The winning method for this subject is spaced repetition of tables. Write the milestone timeline and vaccine schedule once, neatly, and revise them on a fixed cycle — after one day, one week, then before every mock. Mix in questions from the start: paediatric stems are short and age-specific, and practising them teaches you to extract the age clue first, which is usually the key to the answer.

## The numbers you must know cold

Paediatrics is unusually number-heavy, and a small set of values answers a surprising share of questions. Keep one page for: normal birth weight and the low-birth-weight cut-off, expected weight gain in the first year, the ages for head circumference growth, anterior fontanelle closure, and the standard anthropometric indices used to classify malnutrition. Add the dehydration assessment signs — the single most practical classification in the subject — and the danger signs in a sick child that mandate urgent referral. These are pure-recall facts; unlike concepts, they cannot be reasoned out in the hall, so they belong on your last-week revision page.

## How paediatrics questions try to trap you

The classic trap is the **age in the stem**. A presentation that is normal at two months is pathological at two years, and the options are built to catch students who skimmed past the age. Train yourself to read the age before the symptoms. The second trap is the **adult answer in a child's question**: drug choices, doses in principle, and even investigations differ in children, and one option usually imports the adult protocol as a distractor. Third, growth questions mix **weight, height and head circumference** curves; know which parameter is affected first in acute versus chronic malnutrition — weight falters first, height later, head circumference last and most seriously. Naming these traps before the exam is most of the defence against them.
