Here is a fact many FMGE aspirants realise too late: **community medicine in FMGE** carries **30 questions** — equal to obstetrics and gynaecology, and more than anatomy, physiology and biochemistry individually. Yet students routinely give it a fraction of the time they give the "big" clinical subjects. That mismatch is an opportunity. Community medicine (PSM) questions are factual, programme- and concept-based, and highly repetitive in style; a disciplined student can secure a large share of these 30 marks with far less effort than the same marks cost in medicine. This guide covers the weightage, the areas that dominate the paper, how to use Park properly, and a revision system for a subject full of numbers, programmes and definitions.

## Community medicine FMGE: weightage and question style

The FMGE by **NBEMS** has 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, **no negative marking**, and a pass mark of **150 out of 300**. Community medicine contributes 30 questions, drawn from:

- **National health programmes** — objectives, components, recent targets and the population norms attached to health infrastructure
- **Epidemiology and biostatistics** — study designs, screening, measures of association, tests of significance, sampling
- **Communicable and non-communicable diseases** — epidemiology, prevention levels, control measures for the major diseases of India
- **Nutrition, environment and occupational health** — deficiency disorders, water and sanitation standards, occupational hazards and their prevention
- **Family planning and demography** — contraceptive methods and their indices, demographic cycle, fertility indicators
- **Health systems and management** — health planning, indicators of health, international health agencies

The style is factual with light application: definitions used precisely, programme facts, and epidemiology concepts applied to short scenarios.

## High-yield areas

- **Epidemiology** — study designs and their strengths, sensitivity/specificity and predictive values, odds ratio vs relative risk, bias types, levels of prevention
- **Biostatistics** — measures of central tendency and dispersion, normal distribution, p-value and significance, sampling methods, charts and diagrams
- **National programmes** — tuberculosis, malaria, HIV, immunisation (UIP schedule is essential), maternal and child health programmes, NPCDCS and other major national programmes with their key facts
- **Communicable diseases** — epidemiology and control of TB, malaria, dengue, cholera, typhoid, hepatitis, rabies, influenza — agent, transmission, incubation period patterns
- **Nutrition** — protein-energy malnutrition assessment, vitamin deficiencies, food fortification, mid-day meal and ICDS basics
- **Environment and occupation** — water purification, air pollution indices basics, occupational diseases (pneumoconioses) and the Factories Act/ESI essentials
- **Demography and family planning** — contraceptive efficacy indices (Pearl index), eligibility and classic one-liners on each method

## How to use Park — and what else you need

- **K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine** — the undisputed standard; the paper's programme facts, definitions and indicators are drawn from its framework. Use the latest edition you can access, because programme details and schedules change between editions
- **Your own fact sheets** — Park is huge and narrative; extract (1) a programme sheet, (2) an indicators/values sheet, (3) a vaccines sheet, (4) a biostatistics formula-and-concept sheet. These four sheets become your revision core
- **Update-sensitive topics** — immunisation schedule, programme names and targets change over time; prefer the newest source available and be alert to recently updated guidelines

A warning from experience: reading Park passively produces a feeling of familiarity, not recall. Every chapter must end with extracted facts and attempted questions.

## Study method for a fact-heavy subject

- **Concept first in epidemiology and biostatistics** — these are the only "understanding" parts; learn them properly (with worked examples) and they will also help you read journal-style questions elsewhere
- **Everything else as tables and lists** — programme components, incubation periods, indicators with their formulas, waste categories, water quality standards
- **Attach numbers carefully** — community medicine asks values: rates, ratios, cut-offs, norms. Keep a single "numbers notebook" and revise it weekly; mixing values between programmes is the classic error
- **Revise in short frequent rounds** — facts here are volatile; three 40-minute rounds across a month beat one 8-hour sitting
- **Drill questions early and often** — topic-wise sets from a [question bank](/qbank/) teach you the exact phrasing examiners use for definitions and programme facts

## Revision plan

- **First pass:** epidemiology and biostatistics concepts; then chapter-wise Park reading with fact-sheet extraction; topic-wise questions after each block
- **Second pass:** fact sheets and numbers notebook only; a timed community medicine test from the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/)
- **Final phase:** programme and vaccine sheets revised twice in the last fortnight; full mocks from [previous-year papers](/papers/) and the [grand test series](/tests/) to place PSM inside whole-paper stamina

## Common mistakes that cost community medicine marks

- **Under-allocating time because "PSM is boring"** — thirty questions do not care about your interest level. On a marks-per-hour basis, community medicine is one of the most efficient subjects in the paper, and neglecting it is one of the most expensive habits an aspirant can have
- **Reading an outdated edition** — programme names, targets and immunisation details change; answers keyed to an old schedule are simply wrong. Check the edition of your source before you trust its programme chapters
- **Learning values without labels** — a number remembered without the programme, population and year it belongs to is a trap; examiners deliberately place similar values from different programmes in the same options
- **Treating biostatistics as formulae to memorise** — the questions test concepts (which test, which measure, what a p-value means) far more than arithmetic; understand first, memorise second
