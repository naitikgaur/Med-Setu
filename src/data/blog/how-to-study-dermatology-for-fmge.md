**Dermatology for FMGE** carries **5 questions out of 300** in the NBEMS paper of two parts of 150 MCQs, with no negative marking and a pass mark of 150. Small as it is, dermatology has an unusually high hit rate for prepared students: the questions are mostly photographs or one-line descriptions of classic conditions, and the list of conditions examiners rotate through is short. The catch is that the subject is visual — if you have only ever read descriptions without seeing lesions, the photographs in the paper will not resolve into diagnoses. This guide covers the vocabulary and the condition list that those 5 questions come from.

## Dermatology for FMGE: first, learn the language of lesions

Dermatology questions are written in a precise descriptive vocabulary, and half the battle is knowing what each word looks like:

- **Primary lesions** — macule, papule, plaque, vesicle, bulla, pustule, nodule, wheal
- **Secondary changes** — scale, crust, erosion, ulcer, lichenification, atrophy
- **Configurations** — annular, linear, grouped, dermatomal

When a stem says "annular plaque with a raised scaly border and central clearing", it is naming ringworm in code. Build a one-page visual glossary — term, one-line definition, one image — and the stems of this subject become readable.

## The conditions that fill the five questions

- **Infections** — tinea and other superficial fungal infections, scabies with its burrows and itch distribution, impetigo, herpes simplex and zoster, warts and molluscum contagiosum
- **Leprosy** — a persistent favourite in the Indian context: the spectrum from tuberculoid to lepromatous, hypopigmented anaesthetic patches, nerve thickening, and the reactions
- **Psoriasis and lichen planus** — the silvery-scaled extensor plaque with Auspitz's sign; the purple, polygonal, pruritic papules with Wickham's striae
- **Sexually transmitted infections** — the painless chancre of primary syphilis versus the painful ulcer of chancroid, and the ulcer differential generally
- **Bullous disorders and drug reactions** — pemphigus versus bullous pemphigoid at the level of blister depth and Nikolsky's sign, plus Stevens–Johnson syndrome as the emergency
- **Pigmentary and common conditions** — vitiligo, melasma, acne vulgaris and alopecia areata in outline

Notice how often the question reduces to one sign: Auspitz, Nikolsky, Koebner phenomenon, Darier's sign. Collect these named signs into a single revision list. Add to that list the target or iris lesion of erythema multiforme and the herald patch of pityriasis rosea — two more one-feature diagnoses that examiners reach for when they need a reliable dermatology question.

## Dermatology's overlap with other subjects

Skin is where several systemic subjects surface. Cutaneous markers of diabetes, the rash of secondary syphilis, leprosy's nerve involvement (shared with neurology teaching), and STD management (shared with OBG and Medicine) all cross borders. This is another reason the short dermatology list earns more than its 5 questions suggest.

## Study method: images first

Use a concise dermatology text or your undergraduate notes, but study **image-first**: look at the photograph, commit to a diagnosis, then read. Two short passes are enough — one to build the glossary and condition list, one before the exam for the named signs and the leprosy spectrum. Do not expand into rare genodermatoses or detailed treatment protocols; at FMGE level, management questions stay at first-line principles, and the bulk of the marks are in recognition.

## Hair, nails and mucosal clues

Examiners round out the subject with appendage questions. For **hair**: alopecia areata with its exclamation-mark hairs, telogen effluvium after illness or childbirth, and androgenetic alopecia in outline; scarring versus non-scarring alopecia is the organising split. For **nails**: koilonychia with iron deficiency, clubbing's systemic associations (shared with Medicine), pitting in psoriasis, and the nail changes of lichen planus. For **mucosa**: oral lichen planus, aphthous ulcers, and the white patches that must not be scraped off versus candidiasis that can. These are one-line associations — collect them on a single page and they convert directly into marks.

## Management principles without the detail

Treatment questions in dermatology stay at the level of principle: topical therapy first for localised disease, the role of topical steroids and their potencies in outline, emollients as the base of eczema care, antifungals for tinea (and the trap of steroid–antifungal combinations masking it as tinea incognito), permethrin and contact treatment in scabies, and systemic therapy reserved for severe or widespread disease. Add the public-health frame for leprosy — multidrug therapy as the standard programme treatment — and you have the management layer the paper expects. Anything deeper belongs to postgraduate study, not to your FMGE hours.

## The last-week page

Condense the whole subject onto one page for the final week: the lesion glossary in six lines, the leprosy spectrum as a two-pole table, psoriasis and lichen planus in four lines each, the genital ulcer differential, the named signs list, and the five management principles. If a dermatology fact is not on that page, it is unlikely to be worth a question slot — and if it is on the page, you will meet it fresh. Build the page during your first pass, not at the end; deciding what earns a line is itself the most valuable revision exercise this subject offers, because it forces you to rank conditions the way the examiner's short list does.
