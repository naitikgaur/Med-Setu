Examiners rarely meet a candidate who failed for one dramatic reason; they meet thousands who failed by accumulation, and the accumulation is almost always made of ordinary **fmge preparation mistakes** repeated for months. That is good news. Ability is hard to change in a season; habits are not. The FMGE asks for **150 out of 300** across **19 subjects**, in two parts of 150 questions, with **no negative marking** — a defined, knowable target. The fifteen mistakes below are the ones that most reliably keep prepared, intelligent students below that line. Read the list as an audit: find the ones that are yours, fix them in order of cost, and your next mock will usually show the difference before your knowledge has changed at all.

## FMGE Preparation Mistakes of Planning

**1. Studying without the blueprint.** Students revise what they enjoy or what their college emphasised, while the exam weights subjects by its own published pattern. Fix: print the subject weightage, plan from it, and review your plan against it monthly. The full table is on the [resources](/resources/) page.

**2. Starting mocks "when I am ready".** Readiness is produced by mocks, not required for them. Students who postpone testing discover their pacing and subject gaps weeks before the exam, when repairs are most expensive. Fix: take a diagnostic full paper in your first week, however uncomfortable the score.

**3. No written plan — or a fantasy one.** A plan that assumes fourteen focused hours daily is a guilt generator, not a plan. Fix: build the plan around your real available hours, with mock dates and revision cycles written in, and judge yourself weekly against it.

**4. Ignoring small subjects.** Dermatology, Psychiatry, Radiology, Orthopaedics and the other smaller-weightage subjects are individually minor and collectively decisive — together they carry a large share of the paper, and their questions are often more factual and learnable than the big subjects' vignettes. Fix: schedule them explicitly, in short repeated cycles.

## Mistakes of Method

**5. Passive preparation.** Watching videos end to end and reading notes cover to cover feels productive and tests nothing. Fix: attach questions to every topic the same day you study it, using the [question bank](/qbank/) in Practice mode, so retrieval — not recognition — is what you train.

**6. Memorising answers instead of concepts.** Previous-year questions repeated by rote collapse the moment the exam twists the stem. Fix: for every practised question, be able to say why the correct option is right and why each other option is wrong.

**7. Reviewing only the score.** A mock reviewed as "I got 132" teaches nothing. Fix: classify every error — did not know, misread, chose wrong between two, ran out of time — and write down three concrete repairs. MedSetu's **8-part analysis** is built to make this classification quick.

**8. One revision, or none.** A topic read once in month one is a rumour by month four. Fix: build spaced revision into the plan from the start — short revisits of earlier subjects every week, not a single heroic revision at the end.

**9. Collecting materials instead of finishing them.** Five half-finished sources teach less than one finished source plus questions. Fix: choose your core material per subject, finish it, and let additional sources answer specific doubts only.

## Mistakes of Exam Craft

**10. Leaving questions blank.** With no negative marking, a blank is a certain zero and an attempt is a chance. Students carry blank-habits from negative-marking exams into an exam where they are pure loss. Fix: train the rule — the last minutes of every part belong to unattempted questions — in every mock on the [grand test series](/tests/).

**11. Marrying a hard question.** Ten minutes spent wrestling one vignette is nine minutes taken from questions you could have answered. Fix: use the two-pass method — mark, move, return — and practise it until it is automatic.

**12. Never practising in full sittings.** Ninety-minute study sessions do not train a two-part, 300-question day. Fatigue errors in Part B are real and preventable. Fix: rehearse with full papers — the [previous-year papers](/papers/) in exam mode — at the real time of day, with the real break pattern.

**13. Misreading under speed.** "EXCEPT", "not", "first", "most appropriate" — the expensive words are small ones. Fix: slow down on the stem's demand, not on the whole paper; underline the operative word mentally before looking at options.

## Mistakes of Mind and Body

**14. Comparing your preparation to other people's.** Batchmates' hours, scores and confidence are unreliable data about a race that, in a fixed-pass-mark exam, is not even against them. Fix: compete with your own mock trend. It is the only comparison that predicts your result.

**15. Sacrificing sleep to buy hours.** A tired brain converts known answers into wrong ones and reads stems badly — the two most expensive error types in this exam. Fix: protect sleep as part of preparation, especially in the final fortnight, and taper mocks in the last days rather than cramming through them.

## Turning the Audit into Action

Do not try to fix fifteen habits in a week. Take your most recent mock, walk down this list, and mark the three mistakes that are demonstrably costing you marks — the analysis will usually point at them. Repair those three over the next fortnight, re-test, and repeat the audit. Preparation improves the way the mistakes leave: one verified fix at a time. Q: What is the most costly FMGE preparation mistake?
A: For most students it is postponing mock tests. Without mocks you cannot see your subject gaps, pacing problems or unattempted questions until too late.
Q: Why is leaving questions blank a mistake in FMGE?
A: The FMGE has no negative marking, so a blank scores a certain zero while any attempt has a chance of being correct. Attempting all 300 questions is the logical strategy.
Q: How many mistakes should I try to fix at once?
A: Three at a time. Use your latest mock analysis to identify the mistakes costing you the most marks, repair them over the next fortnight, then re-audit.
Q: Do small subjects really matter in FMGE?
A: Yes. Individually small subjects together carry a large share of the 300 questions, and their questions are often factual and learnable, so neglecting them leaks marks.
