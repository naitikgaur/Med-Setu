A prescription is a legal document, a clinical instruction and a safety-critical message in one page — and these **prescription writing basics** decide whether that message survives the journey from your pen to the patient's bedside. Studies of medication errors repeatedly trace harm back to illegible handwriting, ambiguous abbreviations and incomplete instructions rather than lack of knowledge. Students are rarely taught prescribing formally, yet from internship onwards you will write prescriptions daily. Learn the anatomy of a complete prescription and the safety habits now, before the workload makes shortcuts tempting. The examples in this article deliberately use placeholders such as "Tablet X" — the skill being taught is format, not the dose of any real drug.

## Prescription Writing Basics: The Parts of a Prescription

A complete prescription has a fixed structure. Missing any element creates work for the pharmacist and risk for the patient.

- **Prescriber details:** your name, qualifications, registration number, address and contact, usually pre-printed.
- **Date:** the day the prescription is written — essential for validity and for any later review.
- **Patient details:** full name, age, sex, address and weight (essential in children, where doses are weight-based). This is your main defence against a prescription being dispensed to the wrong person.
- **The symbol Rx** (superscription): traditionally begins the body of the prescription.
- **Inscription:** the medicines themselves — name, strength and dosage form.
- **Subscription:** dispensing instructions to the pharmacist, such as the quantity to supply.
- **Signatura (directions):** how the patient should take it — route, frequency, timing in relation to food, and duration, written in language the patient understands.
- **Refill and follow-up instructions:** whether and when the medicine may be repeated, and when to return.
- **Signature:** your legible signature and registration number close the document.

Check any prescription you write against this list before it leaves your hand. A ten-second check is the cheapest safety intervention in medicine.

## Write Generic Names, Strengths and Clear Directions

In India, prescribers are directed to write medicines by their **generic (non-proprietary) name**, with the first letter capitalised, rather than by brand name. This reduces cost to the patient, avoids confusion between similar brand names, and is a professional and regulatory expectation. Follow these clarity rules:

- Write the **full drug name, strength and form**: "Tablet X 500 mg", never a bare name when several strengths exist.
- Write frequency in plain words or standard Latin-derived timings expanded clearly — "once daily in the morning" is safer than a cryptic code the patient must decode.
- Use **"units" written in full** for insulin and similar drugs; the abbreviation "U" is a classic cause of tenfold errors and should be avoided.
- Avoid trailing zeros (write 5 mg, not 5.0 mg, which can be misread as 50) and always use a leading zero for fractions (0.5 mg, not .5 mg).
- State the **duration and total quantity** — "for 5 days" prevents both under-treatment and silent indefinite continuation.
- Add the **indication** where it helps safety, especially for drugs with several uses at different doses.

Practise recognising drug names, classes and adverse effects alongside — pharmacology is heavily tested in the [question bank](/qbank/) and in the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/), and the same familiarity makes your prescribing faster and safer.

## Abbreviations: Use Few, Avoid the Dangerous Ones

Some abbreviations are standard and safe, but many traditional shorthand forms are on international **error-prone abbreviation** lists (the Institute for Safe Medication Practices, ISMP, maintains the best-known one) because they are repeatedly misread. High-risk examples to avoid include:

- **U** for units — misread as a zero, causing tenfold insulin errors. Write "units".
- **QD / QOD** for daily or alternate-day dosing — confused with each other and with QID. Write "daily" or "alternate days".
- **µg** handwritten for micrograms — misread as mg, a thousandfold difference. Write "micrograms".
- **HS** — confused between "at bedtime" and "half strength". Write the instruction out.
- Decimal shortcuts and chemical symbols written hurriedly, which invite misreading at the pharmacy counter at midnight.

The rule is simple: if an abbreviation could be read two ways by a tired pharmacist, it does not belong on your prescription. When in doubt, write it in full sentences. Legibility is not cosmetic — if your handwriting is poor, print in capitals or use a computerised prescription where available.

## Special Situations and Legal Habits

Some prescriptions carry extra duties. **Controlled drugs** (such as opioids and certain sedatives) have strict legal requirements for quantities in words and figures, record-keeping and validity — learn your institution's and the law's exact rules before prescribing them, and never sign a blank or incomplete controlled-drug form. For **children**, record weight and check every dose per kilogram. For **elderly patients**, review the whole list at every visit: polypharmacy and drug interactions cause a large share of admissions, and stopping an unnecessary drug is also prescribing. For every patient, document **allergies** prominently, ask about pregnancy where relevant, and reconcile the new prescription against what the patient already takes — including over-the-counter and traditional medicines they may not volunteer.

Finally, sign only what you have written and understood. Never sign prescriptions in advance, never prescribe for yourself or family as a habit, and never copy a senior's prescription you do not understand — your signature carries your registration and your responsibility.

## A Habit to Start in Internship

Adopt the five rights as a reflex before signing: right **patient**, right **drug**, right **dose**, right **route**, right **time** — and add a sixth: right **duration**. Then read the finished prescription once as if you were the patient receiving it. If any part needs you standing beside it to explain, rewrite it until it does not.

Q: What are the main parts of a prescription?
A: Prescriber details, date, patient details, the Rx symbol, the inscription (drug, strength, form), subscription (quantity to dispense), signatura (directions to the patient), refill instructions and the prescriber's signature with registration number.
Q: Why should generic names be used in prescriptions?
A: Generic names reduce cost, avoid confusion between similar-sounding brand names, and are the professional and regulatory expectation for prescribers in India.
Q: Which abbreviations should be avoided on prescriptions?
A: Error-prone ones such as U for units, QD/QOD, handwritten µg, and HS — all repeatedly misread and listed by safety bodies such as the ISMP. Write instructions in full instead.
Q: What are the five rights of prescribing?
A: Right patient, right drug, right dose, right route and right time — with duration added as a practical sixth check before signing.
