Three months before FMGE is not the time for a perfect plan — it is the time for a ruthless one. You cannot read everything, and this **fmge 3 month study plan** does not pretend otherwise. What you *can* do in roughly 90 days is cover the high-yield core of all 19 subjects, solve thousands of questions, and train yourself to convert knowledge into at least **150 out of 300** under exam conditions. That is the only goal. Everything below serves it.

## The maths of an FMGE 3 month study plan

The exam is **300 MCQs (Part A 150 + Part B 150)**, **+1 per correct answer, no negative marking**, conducted by NBEMS. You need exactly half. Now look at the weightage:

- **Medicine 33 + Surgery 32 + OBG 30 + Community Medicine 30 = 125 questions**
- Add **Paediatrics, Ophthalmology, ENT (15 each)** and **Pathology, Microbiology, Pharmacology (13 each)** and you reach **209 questions**
- The remaining subjects — Anatomy, Physiology, Biochemistry (17 each), Forensic Medicine and Radiology (10 each), and the four 5-mark subjects — supply the rest

A candidate who prepares the top ten subjects well and the rest at a basic level has a realistic route past 150. A candidate who spends month one reading Anatomy cover to cover does not. **Weightage is your syllabus now.**

## Month 1: the big four, at speed

**Weeks 1–2 — Medicine.** Systems in order of yield: cardiology, neurology, respiratory, endocrine, GI, renal, infectious diseases. For each system: read concise notes, then immediately solve topic questions in the [question bank](/qbank/). No system is "done" until you have answered questions on it.

**Week 3 — Surgery.** General surgery principles, GI surgery, trauma, burns, hernia, surgical emergencies. Make one-page lists of classifications, investigations of choice and most-common facts.

**Week 4 — OBG + Community Medicine (split the week).** OBG: labour, obstetric emergencies, antenatal care, contraception. SPM: epidemiology, screening, vaccines, national programmes, nutrition. Both are high-yield and learnable fast.

Every Sunday of month 1: take a [subject-wise test](/subjects/) for whatever you finished that week. You are building the habit of being tested, not just studying.

## Month 2: the middle band + weekly full papers

**Weeks 5–6 — Paediatrics, Ophthalmology, ENT.** 45 questions. Milestones and immunisation; cataract, glaucoma and eye emergencies; ear-nose-throat conditions with their classic presentations.

**Week 7 — Pathology, Microbiology, Pharmacology.** 39 questions. General pathology and the big systemic topics; organism–disease pairs; drug classes, mechanisms and adverse effects. Learn these through questions as much as notes — these subjects are MCQ-shaped by nature.

**Week 8 — Anatomy, Physiology, Biochemistry (high-yield only).** Do not read these like a first-year student. Neuroanatomy and commonly tested regions; CVS, respiratory and renal physiology; metabolic pathways, enzymes, molecular biology. You are collecting the frequently asked core, not mastering the subjects.

**One full paper every week of month 2** — sit a complete paper from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) in a single day, Part A and Part B with a break between, timed like the real thing. Analyse it the next day: every error labelled knowledge gap, silly mistake, or failed guess. Knowledge gaps feed directly into your revision list.

## Month 3: small subjects, grand tests, revision cycles

**Week 9 — the small five.** Forensic Medicine (10), Radiology (10), Psychiatry, Dermatology, Anaesthesia, Orthopaedics (5 each). Forty questions from the most compact syllabi in the exam. One focused week here is the best marks-per-hour investment in the whole plan.

**Weeks 10–11 — grand tests + targeted revision.** Take two tests a week from the [grand test series](/tests/). Between tests, revise only three things: your error-log topics, your marked questions, and rapid fact lists (classifications, most-commons, normal values, programme details). No new textbooks. No new subjects.

**Week 12 — taper and simulate.** Two grand tests early in the week, light revision after, and the last two days kept for logistics and rest. Attempt one final paper three days before the exam, analyse it, and stop.

## What a typical day looks like in this plan

Structure beats intensity. A workable day in months 1 and 2 runs in three blocks: a **morning block** (about 4 hours) for the current subject's new material, an **afternoon block** (about 3 hours) of topic questions on exactly what the morning covered, and an **evening block** (about 2 hours) of mixed questions from subjects finished in earlier weeks, so they stay warm. Close the day by writing tomorrow's topic list and adding the day's mistakes to the error log. That is roughly nine focused hours — demanding, but this is a crash plan, and the evening mixed block is what stops week one's Medicine from evaporating by week eight.

## Rules that make a crash plan work

- **Questions every single day.** In a crash plan, solving is studying. Set a daily floor and protect it.
- **Attempt everything, always.** No negative marking means blank answers are pure loss — practise guessing intelligently in every mock so it is automatic in the hall.
- **One error log.** A single notebook or document where every mock mistake lands, revised weekly. In month 3, this log *is* your textbook.
- **Cut without guilt.** A topic that takes two days and yields one possible question is a bad trade in month 2. Park it.
- **Sleep is a study tool.** A tired brain drops silly-mistake marks, which are exactly the marks a crash plan cannot afford.

Three months will not make you complete — it can make you sufficient, and sufficient is 150. Begin this week: sit one full paper from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) to get your honest baseline, then start Medicine the same day. Every week after that, let your mock scores — not your anxiety — tell you whether the plan is working.

Q: Can I really prepare for FMGE in 3 months?
A: It is demanding but realistic if you prioritise by weightage — the top subjects first, high-yield topics in the rest — solve questions daily, and take weekly full papers. The pass mark is 150 of 300, not a top rank.
Q: Which subjects matter most in a 3-month FMGE crash plan?
A: Medicine (33 questions), Surgery (32), Obstetrics & Gynaecology (30) and Community Medicine (30) come first, followed by Paediatrics, Ophthalmology, ENT, Pathology, Microbiology and Pharmacology.
Q: Should I read textbooks during a 3-month FMGE plan?
A: Use textbooks only as reference for topics you get wrong. A crash plan runs on concise notes, previous-year questions and mock tests, not cover-to-cover reading.
Q: How many full mock tests should I take in the last 3 months?
A: One previous-year paper a week in the second month, rising to two grand tests a week in the final month, each followed by a proper error analysis.
