The Reddy forensic medicine book — the text by K. S. Narayan Reddy — is one of those rare standard books that is also genuinely exam-friendly. Forensic medicine is a compact subject with a loyal, predictable examination pattern, and Reddy's organisation matches it: clear definitions, classifications everywhere, and the medico-legal framing that Indian courts and Indian examiners expect. Students who treat forensic medicine as a small, finishable subject — and Reddy as the single book to finish — routinely convert it into some of the most reliable marks of their second and third years. This guide shows how to use the book section by section.

## Reddy forensic medicine book: know its four territories

Reddy's text divides naturally into four territories, and each needs a different study method.

**Thanatology and postmortem changes** — death, its signs, postmortem changes, autopsy and exhumation. This is the conceptual heart of the subject: the sequence of changes after death, how time since death is estimated, and how the autopsy is conducted and documented. Examiners return here constantly.

**Injuries and mechanical trauma** — wounds classified by cause and by law, regional injuries, and the medico-legal questions each injury raises: ante-mortem or post-mortem, suicidal, homicidal or accidental, and the age of the injury.

**Toxicology** — the poisons: general principles of management, then individual poisons with their signs, diagnosis and medico-legal importance. This is the largest memorisation load in the book.

**The medico-legal and legal sections** — consent, medical negligence, certificates, courts and evidence, sexual offences, and the duties of a doctor in the eyes of the law. This territory is uniquely Indian in flavour and uniquely examinable, because it tests the doctor you are about to become, not just facts you can recite.

## How to study each territory

**Thanatology** rewards understanding over lists. Learn postmortem changes as a *timeline* — what appears in the first hours, the first day, the first week — and practise estimating time since death from descriptions, because that is how the topic is asked. Draw the sequence once, properly, and revise from your drawing.

**Injuries** reward classification discipline — Reddy's strength. For each wound type, fix four things: how it is produced, how it looks, how it heals, and what the court will ask about it. Viva examiners in particular live in this territory, because injury questions can be asked from a specimen, a photograph or a case in a single breath.

**Toxicology** rewards triage. Not every poison is equal: a core group — organophosphates, common corrosives, alcohol, opioids, snakebite and a handful of classic poisons such as copper sulphate and dhatura — dominates papers and practicals. Learn the core in full (source, signs, diagnosis, broad management principles, medico-legal points), and cover the long tail at recognition level. Note that this is exam study of principles; actual poisoning management is a supervised clinical act, never a textbook exercise.

**The medico-legal sections** reward reading like a future practitioner. Consent, negligence and certification questions are usually short, direct and drawn from these chapters almost verbatim. They are also the part of forensic medicine you will use in the first month of internship, which makes them doubly worth learning properly. One caution: laws and procedures are amended from time to time, so treat the legal detail in any textbook as a foundation and check the current position when it matters in real practice.

## Why forensic medicine is a scoring subject — if you start it early

Forensic medicine carries a modest weightage in FMGE and a full subject paper in university exams, but its syllabus is finite and its questions repeat in recognisable patterns. That combination — small syllabus, loyal questions — makes it one of the best marks-per-hour investments in the course. The students who score poorly are usually those who started it in the last month and met toxicology's long lists without a system. Start Reddy early in the term, finish it once slowly, and revise it twice quickly; the subject then stays finished.

## Autopsy and practical examinations: where Reddy pays you back

Forensic medicine practicals are unlike any other subject's, because the examination itself is a medico-legal performance: spotting and describing injuries or specimens, examining a case, or answering questions around the autopsy you have watched through the term. Reddy prepares you for this better than students expect, provided you read the practical-facing material as instructions rather than theory. The chapters on autopsy procedure, preservation of viscera, and the examination and certification of injuries and sexual offence cases describe, step by step, what a doctor actually does and writes. Learn those sequences as checklists. In the practical hall, the student who narrates a specimen in Reddy's order — what it is, how it was produced, what it proves, what the court will ask — sounds like a doctor; the student who recites a memorised paragraph sounds like a parrot, and examiners reward the difference.

## Common mistakes with Reddy

- **Treating toxicology as the whole subject.** It is one territory of four. Thanatology and the medico-legal chapters are smaller, easier and just as examined.
- **Memorising without the medico-legal angle.** Every topic in this book has a "what will the court ask" dimension; answers that include it score visibly better.
- **Skipping definitions and classifications.** This subject's examiners ask them directly and literally. Reddy gives them cleanly — learn them as given.
- **Leaving the subject to the end.** Its lists need spaced revision, not a single desperate read.

Reddy has remained the standard because it respects both halves of its subject: the medicine and the law. Use it as one complete book, study each territory with its own method, keep the medico-legal question in view throughout, and forensic medicine becomes what it should be — a compact, finished, scoring subject.
