This Ganong physiology guide exists because of a common first-year scene: a student opens Ganong's Review of Medical Physiology, reads three dense pages on membrane potentials, understands half, and concludes the book is "too tough." The book is not too tough — it is being used wrong. Ganong is a **review**: it assumes teaching has happened (in lectures, practicals or another text) and compresses physiology into precise, mechanism-first summaries. Used after teaching, actively and selectively, it is the fastest route to genuine physiological understanding. Used as a first introduction, cold, it frustrates almost everyone.

## Ganong physiology guide: the right way into each chapter

Enter every chapter through its edges before reading its middle. Ganong chapters typically open with an overview and close with a **chapter summary** — read the summary first. It tells you the destination: the handful of mechanisms the chapter exists to teach. Then look at the figures and tables, because Ganong's diagrams often carry the argument more clearly than its paragraphs. Only then read the full text, and you will find the density drops sharply, because you already know what each section is trying to prove.

Read with a pen and a question: *what changes, what senses the change, and what corrects it?* Physiology is the science of regulation, and most Ganong passages are feedback loops wearing different clothes. If you can draw the loop — sensor, integrator, effector, response — you understand the topic, whether it is blood pressure, temperature or blood glucose.

## The clinical boxes are not decoration

Ganong weaves **clinical material** through its chapters — short passages connecting a mechanism to the disease that appears when it fails. Students routinely skip these to "save time," which is exactly backwards. Those boxes are where examiners live. A question on why a patient with heart failure is breathless at night is a Ganong clinical box converted into an MCQ. When you read a mechanism, always ask the box's question in reverse: *if this breaks, what does the patient look like?* That single habit links first-year physiology to medicine, surgery and the FMGE paper you will eventually sit.

Keep a small notebook titled "when it breaks." One line per mechanism: the failure, the finding. By the end of the book you own a personal clinical physiology text, written by you.

## Which parts of Ganong deserve your deepest time

Not all physiology is equal in exams or in life. Give your deepest Ganong time to **cardiovascular physiology** (haemodynamics, cardiac cycle, regulation of blood pressure), **respiratory mechanics and gas exchange**, **renal handling of water and electrolytes**, **gastrointestinal secretion and motility**, and **endocrine feedback systems** — insulin and glucose, thyroid, adrenal and calcium regulation. These systems dominate university papers and return constantly in clinical subjects.

Neurophysiology splits: the sensory, motor and special senses sections are heavily asked and reward careful reading, while some higher-function detail can be covered more lightly on a first pass. Cellular and membrane physiology at the book's start feels abstract but underpins everything — invest early there, because a shaky first month makes every later chapter slower.

## Ganong or Guyton? Use both, in the right order

The Ganong-versus-Guyton debate wastes a lot of first-year energy. They do different jobs. **Guyton and Hall** explains at length, narrating mechanisms step by step — ideal when a topic simply will not make sense. **Ganong** compresses and sharpens — ideal for consolidating, revising and thinking clinically. The efficient pattern: attend the lecture or class, read Ganong actively; when a section resists two honest attempts, read the corresponding Guyton explanation, then return to Ganong and summarise it in your own words. Owning Ganong and borrowing Guyton from the library is the combination most strong students actually use.

## Using Ganong alongside practicals

Physiology practicals are where Ganong quietly earns extra marks. Experiments on blood, nerve-muscle preparations, spirometry, ECG and clinical examination of the systems are all miniature versions of chapters in the book — the haematology experiments mirror the blood and immunity chapters, the respiratory exercises mirror mechanics and control of breathing, and the cardiovascular examination mirrors haemodynamics and the cardiac cycle. Read the relevant Ganong section **before** the practical, not after: you will know what the graph or tracing is supposed to show, which means you can spot when your own result is wrong and, more importantly, explain the normal result in the practical viva in mechanism language rather than memorised values. Examiners in practicals are rarely hunting for numbers; they are hunting for whether you understand what your hands just measured. Ganong, read in advance, is precisely that understanding.

## Turning Ganong into marks

Physiology is learned by retrieval, not exposure. After each chapter, close the book and write the summary from memory; the gaps in your recall are your real study list. Then test the topic — draw the graphs examiners love (cardiac cycle, pressure-volume loops, oxygen dissociation), because physiology answers are so often graphs explained in words. For objective drilling as you progress through medical school, free practice in a [question bank](/qbank/) builds the same retrieval habit in MCQ form, and MedSetu's [resources](/resources/) can help you plan the longer road to FMGE.

Revise Ganong in passes: first pass for understanding, second for the summaries and tables alone, third — before exams — for your own notes and the "when it breaks" notebook. Each pass is faster, and each pass is aimed at a different layer of the same knowledge.

Ganong rewards students who treat it as a coach rather than a storybook: summary first, figures before text, clinical boxes always, and a feedback loop drawn for every system. Work that way and the "tough book" reputation disappears — what remains is the clearest short account of how the body works that a student can own.

Q: Why does Ganong feel so difficult in first year?
A: Ganong is a review text — it compresses physiology and assumes lectures or teaching have introduced the topic first. Read the chapter summary and figures before the main text and the density becomes manageable.
Q: Should I read Ganong or Guyton?
A: Use both for different jobs: Guyton for long, clear explanations when a topic will not settle, Ganong for consolidation, revision and clinical links. Many students own Ganong and borrow Guyton.
Q: Are the clinical boxes in Ganong important for exams?
A: Yes. They connect mechanisms to disease, which is exactly how physiology is tested in vivas, clinical subjects and FMGE. Skipping them removes the most exam-relevant part of many chapters.
Q: How should I revise Ganong before exams?
A: In passes — first the full chapter for understanding, then summaries and tables only, and finally your own notes. Always finish a topic by writing its summary from memory to expose gaps.
