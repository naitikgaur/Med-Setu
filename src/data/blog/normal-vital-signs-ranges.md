Vital signs are the first numbers recorded on every patient, and knowing the **normal vital signs ranges** by heart is what lets you spot the one abnormal value in a full observation chart at a glance. Pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature and oxygen saturation are called "vital" because together they describe the basic state of the circulation, breathing and temperature control. This article gives the standard adult resting ranges taught in clinical-methods textbooks, explains what pushes each value up or down, and notes honestly where children differ — because paediatric values are not small-adult values.

## Normal Vital Signs Ranges at a Glance (Adults at Rest)

Commit this table to memory first; interpretation comes after.

- **Pulse (heart rate):** 60–100 beats per minute
- **Blood pressure:** around 120/80 mmHg is the textbook normal; values persistently at or above 140/90 mmHg meet the standard threshold for hypertension in most classifications, with 120–129 systolic and below 80 diastolic often labelled "elevated" in the American classification
- **Respiratory rate:** 12–20 breaths per minute
- **Temperature:** approximately 36.5–37.5 °C (oral readings vary slightly by site and time of day)
- **Oxygen saturation (SpO2):** about 95–100% breathing room air
- **Capillary refill time:** under 2 seconds

These are **resting adult** values. Every one of them moves with pain, fever, anxiety, exercise, caffeine and posture — which is why a single reading is a snapshot and a **trend** is a story. Early-warning scores used in hospitals are built exactly on these ranges, awarding points as values drift further from normal. ## Pulse and Blood Pressure

**Pulse.** Count for a full minute when the rhythm is irregular, and record rate, rhythm, volume and character — not just the number. **Bradycardia** (below 60) is normal in trained athletes and during sleep but can reflect heart block, hypothyroidism or raised intracranial pressure. **Tachycardia** (above 100) is the body's all-purpose alarm: fever, pain, anxiety, blood loss, infection, hyperthyroidism and heart failure all drive it up. A new irregular rhythm, classically the irregularly irregular pulse of atrial fibrillation, always deserves an ECG.

**Blood pressure.** Technique decides truth: correct cuff size, arm supported at heart level, patient seated and rested, no talking, and both arms checked at the first visit. One high reading is not hypertension — diagnosis needs repeated measurements, often including home or ambulatory readings, because of the **white-coat effect**. Standard classification describes normal as below 120/80, with hypertension diagnosed at persistently raised levels (the widely used threshold is 140/90 mmHg in clinic, with some guidelines, notably the American one, using 130/80). At the other extreme, a falling blood pressure with a rising pulse is the classic late picture of shock — blood pressure is defended until late, so do not wait for it to fall before worrying.

## Respiratory Rate, Temperature and Oxygen Saturation

**Respiratory rate** is the most neglected and most predictive vital sign. Count it quietly for a full minute — ideally while appearing to count the pulse, because patients alter their breathing when watched. A rate climbing above the low twenties is often the **earliest** sign of deterioration, preceding changes in saturation or blood pressure. A very low rate suggests opioid or sedative effect or severe exhaustion in a struggling patient.

**Temperature.** Fever is a sign, not a disease: infection is the commonest cause, but inflammation, malignancy, drugs and heat stroke all raise it. Record the site and method, because axillary readings run lower than oral, which run lower than core. A temperature below 35 °C is hypothermia. Remember the pattern matters — the rigors, the timing, and whether fever responds to antipyretics all feed the history you connect it to.

**Oxygen saturation.** Pulse oximetry estimates arterial saturation from a fingertip probe. About **95–100% on room air** is expected in healthy adults; chronic lung disease patients may be managed to lower individualised targets set by their clinicians. Poor perfusion, cold hands, nail polish and movement all corrupt the reading — warm the hand and check the waveform before believing an alarming number from a comfortable patient. And note what saturation cannot tell you: it says nothing about ventilation or carbon dioxide, which is why a drowsy patient with a "normal" SpO2 can still be in danger.

## Children Are Different

Paediatric ranges vary strongly with age, and using adult cut-offs on children is a classic error. As a broad orientation: newborns breathe **30–60 times per minute** and have heart rates around **100–160 per minute**; infants remain much faster than adults on both counts; rates fall steadily through childhood towards adult values by adolescence. Fever assessment, hydration signs and parental concern carry extra weight in children. Always check the age-specific chart used in your institution rather than estimating — precision matters most in the smallest patients. ## When a Number Should Make You Act

Ranges tell you what is usual; judgement tells you what is urgent. Escalate promptly when you see combinations rather than single values: rising respiratory rate with falling saturation, tachycardia with falling blood pressure, fever with confusion, or any rapid change from the patient's own baseline. Record accurately, repeat doubtful measurements with correct technique, compare with earlier readings, and hand over the trend. That discipline — measure well, compare, escalate early — is the entire clinical value of vital signs.

Q: What are the normal vital signs for an adult?
A: At rest: pulse 60–100/min, respiratory rate 12–20/min, temperature about 36.5–37.5 °C, SpO2 about 95–100% on room air, and blood pressure around 120/80 mmHg.
Q: What blood pressure counts as hypertension?
A: Persistently raised readings at or above 140/90 mmHg in clinic meet the standard threshold in most classifications; diagnosis requires repeated measurements, and some guidelines use 130/80 mmHg.
Q: Why is respiratory rate so important?
A: It is often the earliest sign of deterioration, rising before oxygen saturation or blood pressure change — and it is the vital sign most often estimated instead of properly counted.
Q: Are children's vital signs the same as adults'?
A: No. Children have faster heart and respiratory rates that vary with age — newborns breathe 30–60 times per minute with heart rates around 100–160 — so age-specific charts must be used.
