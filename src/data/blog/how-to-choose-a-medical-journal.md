Sooner or later, every medical student faces two versions of the same problem: which journals deserve your limited reading time, and — when you have a case report or a study of your own — which journal deserves your work. Knowing **how to choose a medical journal, predatory journals** included in the lesson, protects both your learning and your career. The uncomfortable truth is that publishing is a business with a low-quality fringe: alongside rigorous journals sit **predatory journals** that will print almost anything for a fee, with little or no real peer review. Telling them apart is a learnable skill, built on a few verifiable checks.

## How to Choose a Medical Journal: Predatory Journals vs Legitimate Ones

A legitimate journal does three things visibly well. It applies **peer review** — independent experts scrutinise submissions and many are rejected or sent back for revision. It is **indexed** in recognised databases, meaning it has passed an external quality screen. And it is **transparent** about who edits it, what it charges, and how it operates. A predatory journal imitates the appearance of all three while performing none of them properly. Your job is to check the substance, not the website design — predatory sites often look polished.

## Check the Indexing: PubMed, Scopus and Other Databases

Indexing is your strongest shortcut. A journal listed in **PubMed/MEDLINE** has been reviewed for scientific and editorial quality by the US National Library of Medicine's selection process; listing in **Scopus** or **Web of Science** likewise means the journal passed an external evaluation and continues to be monitored. Practical checks:

- Search for the journal by name in **PubMed** and in the **Scopus source list** — do not trust a logo on the journal's own homepage; verify in the database itself
- Be wary of impressive-sounding but meaningless "indexing" claims listing obscure directories nobody uses
- Treat claims of an "impact factor" from unknown agencies as a red flag; the recognised Journal Impact Factor comes from one source (Clarivate's Journal Citation Reports), and many good specialist journals are valued for reasons beyond any single metric

Indexing is not a guarantee of brilliance — weak papers appear in indexed journals — but absence from every major database, for a journal claiming international standing, is a serious warning.

## Peer Review and Editorial Transparency

Legitimate journals name their **editor and editorial board** — real academics, at real institutions, whose own reputations are staked on the journal. The submission process states realistic timelines; genuine peer review takes weeks to months because reviewers must read, critique and re-read. Fees, where they exist (open-access article processing charges are legitimate and common), are declared openly **before** submission, on the journal's site — never sprung on you in a surprise invoice after acceptance.

Classical warning lists, in the tradition of **Beall's criteria** (the librarian Jeffrey Beall's pioneering work cataloguing predatory publishers), converge on the same red flags:

- **Spam invitations** flattering you and begging for submissions on any topic
- **Promised acceptance in days** — real peer review cannot work at that speed
- A **huge, incoherent scope** ("journal of medicine, engineering and humanities")
- **Fake or unverifiable editorial boards**, or academics listed without their knowledge
- Journal names **mimicking famous titles** with a word changed
- **No clear publisher address**, or contact only through generic webmail
- Metrics and indexing claims that **cannot be verified** in the actual databases

Any one flag deserves a question; three or more mean walk away. If you are choosing where to *read*, the same checks apply in reverse: your default reading should come from indexed, peer-reviewed sources — the habit that also underpins evidence-based practice, which you can revise through our [resources](/resources/) section.

## Choosing Well for Your Stage

As a student, you rarely need to chase prestige. For **reading**, prefer established indexed journals in your subject and good review articles. For a **first publication** — a case report or a small study — a legitimate, indexed, mid-tier journal that genuinely reviews your work teaches you more and looks better on a CV than a predatory outlet that accepts in 48 hours; experienced faculty can smell the difference instantly, and publication in a predatory journal can actively embarrass an application. Ask a mentor or your institution's librarian before submitting anywhere unfamiliar — librarians are the unsung experts of exactly this terrain, and many institutions maintain approved-journal guidance for their researchers.

One more reliable landmark: journals published by established **medical societies and universities** — the official journal of a national specialty association, for example — carry the society's reputation as collateral and are almost always legitimately reviewed and indexed. When in doubt, start from a society's or a university's website and follow its link to the journal, rather than trusting a search result or an emailed invitation. This "arrive from a trusted address" habit defeats most mimicry, because predatory journals depend on reaching you first, on their own terms.

Finally, keep perspective: the goal of choosing a journal well is not snobbery about brands. It is ensuring that the work you read was checked, and the work you write gets checked — because peer review, for all its flaws, remains the filter that separates medical literature from medical advertising. Q: What is a predatory journal?
A: A publication that charges authors fees while providing little or no genuine peer review or editorial quality control, prioritising revenue over science. Such journals often solicit submissions by spam email and promise unrealistically fast acceptance.
Q: How can I check whether a journal is indexed in PubMed?
A: Search for the journal by its exact name or ISSN in PubMed or the NLM Catalog itself. Do not rely on indexing logos displayed on the journal's own website, which predatory journals sometimes display falsely.
Q: Is it bad if a journal charges a publication fee?
A: Not necessarily. Many legitimate open-access journals charge article processing charges, declared openly in advance. The warning sign is fees that are hidden, sprung after acceptance, or combined with absent peer review.
Q: What were Beall's criteria?
A: Librarian Jeffrey Beall compiled lists of criteria for identifying predatory publishers — including deceptive journal names, fake editorial boards, unverifiable metrics and aggressive solicitation. His work founded the modern framework for spotting predatory publishing.
