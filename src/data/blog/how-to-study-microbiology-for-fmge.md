Microbiology has a reputation for being a giant list of organisms, and students respond by trying to memorise the list — then forget it within a fortnight. There is a better way to approach **microbiology for FMGE**. The subject carries **13 questions**, and they cluster around a manageable core: the classic organisms and their identifying features, laboratory diagnosis, sterilisation and disinfection, immunology basics, and the hospital-acquired and opportunistic infections that overlap with medicine. If you organise the subject the way examiners think — organism, disease, diagnostic test, one distinguishing fact — microbiology becomes one of the most reliable scoring areas in the paraclinical block. This guide gives you that organisation.

## Microbiology for FMGE: weightage and question style

The FMGE, conducted by **NBEMS**, is 300 MCQs in two parts of 150 with **no negative marking** and a pass mark of **150 out of 300**. Microbiology contributes 13 questions, commonly:

- **Organism identification** — from a clinical vignette, a morphology description, or a culture/stain finding
- **Laboratory diagnosis** — the test of choice, transport media, culture media and their selective ingredients
- **General microbiology** — sterilisation and disinfection methods, bacterial growth and genetics, staining techniques
- **Immunology** — hypersensitivity types, immunoglobulins, vaccines, immune deficiency clues
- **Systemic infections** — CNS, respiratory, GI, genitourinary and bloodstream infections with their most likely pathogens

The questions are factual, but they are *patterned* facts — the same organism–feature pairings recur across papers.

## High-yield areas

- **Bacteriology** — Staphylococcus, Streptococcus (with its classification and sequelae), Neisseria, Mycobacterium tuberculosis and leprae, Salmonella, Vibrio, E. coli, Pseudomonas, Clostridium species and their toxins
- **Virology** — hepatitis viruses (a favourite: markers and their interpretation), HIV, dengue, rabies, influenza, herpesviruses, and virus–cancer associations
- **Mycology** — Candida, Aspergillus, Cryptococcus, Mucor, dermatophytes and opportunistic fungi in immunocompromised hosts
- **Parasitology** — Plasmodium species and life cycles, Entamoeba, Giardia, helminths with their infective stages and diagnostic forms
- **Immunology** — hypersensitivity reactions with examples, immunoglobulin classes, primary immunodeficiencies, transplant rejection basics
- **Sterilisation and hospital infection** — autoclave and other methods with their applications, disinfectant choices, biomedical waste categories, common hospital-acquired pathogens

## Books that are enough

- **Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology** — the standard Indian text; thorough on bacteriology and systemic infections
- **Paniker's Textbook of Medical Parasitology** — the companion standard for parasites, life cycles and diagnostic stages
- **Review-style summaries** — once your base is built, condensed tables (media, toxins, vaccines) are the highest-yield revision material; build them yourself from your main text so they match your memory

Microbiology is a subject where your tables matter more than your textbook in the final month: media–organism, toxin–organism, vector–disease, vaccine schedules.

## The organism-card method

- **One card per organism** — with fixed fields: morphology/Gram character, virulence factor or toxin, diseases caused, lab diagnosis (best test), and one "most favourite fact" the examiners ask
- **Learn toxins and enzymes as a separate list** — many questions are simply "which organism produces X toxin"
- **Media and tests in tables** — selective/differential media with what grows on them; serological tests and what they detect (especially hepatitis markers, arranged by timeline)
- **Life cycles drawn once** — for parasites, draw the cycle with infective stage and diagnostic stage marked; questions pick from exactly those two points
- **Revise in mixed batches** — interleave bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites in revision, because the paper does; blocked revision feels easier but transfers worse

After each section, solve topic-wise questions from a [question bank](/qbank/). Microbiology questions are often won on a single keyword — "rice-water stools", "negri bodies", "owl's eye inclusion" — and practice teaches your eye to catch them.

## Revision plan

- **First pass:** general microbiology and immunology properly, then organism cards system-wise; topic-wise questions after each system
- **Second pass:** cards and tables only — media, toxins, vectors, vaccines; a timed microbiology test from the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/)
- **Final phase:** one-liner revision (classic findings, test of choice), integrated revision with pharmacology (antimicrobials) and medicine (infectious diseases), plus full mocks from the [grand test series](/tests/)

## Common mistakes that cost microbiology marks

- **Reading the textbook like a novel** — microbiology read passively produces a warm feeling of familiarity and almost no recall. The subject only yields to extraction: cards, tables and lists you actively build and repeatedly test yourself against
- **Studying organisms without their diagnosis** — knowing everything about an organism except how it is diagnosed is a classic half-preparation; a large share of questions end at "which test confirms it", so every organism card must carry its best diagnostic method
- **Treating immunology as a footnote** — hypersensitivity, immunoglobulins and vaccines form a compact block of near-guaranteed questions, and they also support medicine and paediatrics. It deserves a proper pass, not leftovers
- **Cramming parasitology life cycles the night before** — cycles are spatial information; they need to be drawn early and re-drawn in revision, or the infective and diagnostic stages will swap places in your memory under pressure
- **Ignoring sterilisation and disinfection** — it looks like a dull opening chapter, but the methods, their temperature–time combinations and their correct applications form a small, closed set of questions that prepared students simply do not miss
