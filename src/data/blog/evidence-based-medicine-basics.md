Two doctors can read the same study and reach opposite conclusions about whether to change their practice. What separates them is usually not intelligence — it is method. **Evidence based medicine basics** begin with a simple definition: evidence-based medicine (EBM) is the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of the **best available research evidence**, integrated with **clinical expertise** and the **patient's values and preferences**, to make decisions about care. All three legs matter. Evidence alone does not dictate; it informs.

## Evidence Based Medicine Basics: Start With a Good Question

EBM starts before you open a database. It starts with turning a clinical uncertainty into an **answerable question**, and the standard tool is the **PICO framework**:

- **P — Patient or Problem:** who is this about? (age, condition, setting)
- **I — Intervention:** what are you considering doing? (a drug, test, procedure)
- **C — Comparison:** compared with what? (placebo, usual care, another treatment)
- **O — Outcome:** what result matters? (mortality, recovery, side effects, quality of life)

"Is this drug good?" is unanswerable. "In adults with type 2 diabetes (P), does drug X (I), compared with standard therapy (C), reduce cardiovascular events (O)?" can be searched, studied and answered. Notice that the outcome must be one that matters to patients — a fall in a laboratory number is only interesting if it translates into people living longer or feeling better. Learning to frame PICO questions is a skill you can practise early, and it quietly improves how you read every paper afterwards.

## The Hierarchy of Evidence

Not all study designs answer a question with equal reliability. The traditional **hierarchy of evidence** ranks designs by how well they protect against bias when judging whether a treatment works:

- **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** of randomised trials (top)
- **Randomised controlled trials (RCTs)**
- **Cohort studies**
- **Case-control studies**
- **Case series and case reports**
- **Expert opinion and laboratory/mechanistic reasoning** (base)

The logic is about **bias control**. Randomisation balances unknown confounders; following a cohort forward avoids some selection problems that plague retrospective designs; a single case report can generate a hypothesis but cannot test one. Two cautions keep the hierarchy honest. First, it applies mainly to questions of treatment effect — for diagnosis, prognosis or rare harms, other designs (cross-sectional studies, large cohorts, registries) are the right tools. Second, a badly conducted RCT can mislead more than a careful cohort study. The hierarchy is a starting point for appraisal, not a substitute for it. ## Critical Appraisal: The Three Questions

Appraisal is where EBM becomes a habit rather than a slogan. For any study, ask:

- **Is it valid?** Was the design sound — proper randomisation and blinding for a trial, complete follow-up, analysis as randomised? Internal validity comes first; a biased study's results are not worth interpreting.
- **Is it important?** How large is the effect, and how precise is the estimate? A statistically significant but trivially small benefit may not change practice.
- **Is it applicable?** Were the study patients like mine? Is the treatment available and acceptable here? External validity decides whether results travel from the paper to your ward.

This three-part rhythm — validity, importance, applicability — works for trials, diagnostic studies and reviews alike, with design-specific checklists under each heading.

## Putting It Together: The Five Steps of EBM

Teachers often summarise the whole method as five steps, sometimes called the **five As**:

- **Ask** — convert the clinical problem into an answerable PICO question
- **Acquire** — search for the best available evidence efficiently
- **Appraise** — judge that evidence for validity, importance and applicability
- **Apply** — integrate the evidence with your expertise and the patient's preferences, and act
- **Assess** — afterwards, evaluate how it went and what you would do differently

Notice that searching is only step two of five. Students often imagine EBM is mainly about databases, but the discipline lives in the steps around the search: asking precisely, appraising sceptically, applying thoughtfully, and reviewing honestly. A doctor who searches brilliantly but appraises poorly is still practising badly. The five As also scale down beautifully to student life — you can run the same loop on a single ward case or a single viva question, and each pass makes the next one faster.

## Why EBM Matters for a Student Now

It is tempting to file EBM under "research, later." Resist that. As a student you are already consuming evidence daily — every textbook statement, every senior's "in my experience," every drug advertisement makes a claim. EBM gives you the tools to ask *how do we know that, and how strongly?* It also protects you from the two classic traps: adopting a treatment because it is new and fashionable, and rejecting one because it is old. Neither age nor novelty is evidence.

Finally, remember the third leg of the definition: **patient values**. Two patients with identical evidence may choose differently — one prioritising length of life, another prioritising freedom from side effects. EBM does not remove judgement or conversation; it disciplines them. The student takeaway: ask a precise question (PICO), respect the hierarchy without worshipping it, and appraise before you adopt. Q: What does PICO stand for?
A: Patient or Problem, Intervention, Comparison and Outcome. PICO is the standard framework for turning a clinical uncertainty into a focused, answerable question that can be searched and studied.
Q: Why are randomised controlled trials ranked above cohort studies?
A: Because randomisation balances both known and unknown confounders between groups, reducing bias when estimating a treatment effect. Cohort studies cannot guarantee that balance, so their estimates are more vulnerable to confounding.
Q: Does evidence-based medicine mean ignoring clinical experience?
A: No. The classic definition integrates three things: best research evidence, clinical expertise, and the patient's values and preferences. Evidence informs decisions; it does not replace judgement.
Q: What are the three key questions of critical appraisal?
A: Is the study valid (free of major bias)? Is the result important (large and precise enough to matter)? And is it applicable to my patient and setting? A study must pass all three to justify changing practice.
