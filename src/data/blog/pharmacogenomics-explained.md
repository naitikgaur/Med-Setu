Every medical student has heard the puzzle: two patients receive the same drug, at the same dose, for the same disease — one recovers beautifully, the other develops a serious adverse reaction. **Pharmacogenomics explained** in one line is this: it is the study of how a person's inherited genetic makeup influences their response to drugs. It sits at the meeting point of **pharmacology** (what drugs do) and **genomics** (what our genes say), and it is quietly changing prescribing from "one dose fits all" to "the right dose for this patient."

## Pharmacogenomics Explained: Genes Meet Drug Response

A drug's journey through the body — absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion — is run almost entirely by proteins: enzymes that break drugs down, transporters that move them across membranes, and receptors they bind to. Every one of those proteins is encoded by a gene, and genes vary between people. Small inherited variations, often single-letter changes in DNA called **single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)**, can make an enzyme work faster, slower, or not at all.

The result is that patients fall into functional groups for many drug-metabolising enzymes:

- **Poor metabolisers** — little or no enzyme activity; standard doses can accumulate to toxic levels
- **Intermediate metabolisers** — reduced activity; may need lower doses
- **Extensive (normal) metabolisers** — the "average" response most dosing is designed around
- **Ultrarapid metabolisers** — extra enzyme activity, sometimes from duplicated genes; standard doses may fail or, for prodrugs, become dangerous

Most of the classic examples involve the **cytochrome P450 (CYP)** family of liver enzymes — names like CYP2D6, CYP2C9 and CYP2C19 that are worth learning properly, because examiners love them and real patients live by them.

## Classic Examples Every Student Must Know

Textbooks return to the same handful of examples because each one teaches a distinct principle. Learn these and you understand the field.

- **CYP2C9, VKORC1 and warfarin.** Warfarin is broken down mainly by CYP2C9, and its target is the enzyme encoded by **VKORC1**. Variants in either gene change how much warfarin a patient needs — some people are stable on a fraction of the usual dose, and genetic variation is one reason warfarin dosing is guided by regular INR monitoring rather than a fixed prescription.
- **TPMT and thiopurines.** The enzyme **thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT)** inactivates drugs such as azathioprine and mercaptopurine. People who inherit very low TPMT activity cannot clear these drugs properly, and standard doses can cause severe, life-threatening bone-marrow suppression. This is one of the oldest and clearest cases where a genetic test directly prevents harm.
- **G6PD deficiency and oxidative drugs.** **Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)** protects red blood cells from oxidative damage. In G6PD-deficient individuals, certain drugs — the antimalarial primaquine is the classic example — can trigger acute haemolytic anaemia. The defect is in red-cell defence, not drug metabolism, which is why it is taught as pharmacogenetics in its broadest sense.
- **CYP2D6 and codeine.** Codeine is a **prodrug**: it works only after CYP2D6 converts it into morphine. Poor metabolisers get almost no pain relief from it, while ultrarapid metabolisers convert it so fast that dangerous morphine levels can build up — the reason codeine carries special warnings in children and breastfeeding mothers.

Notice the pattern: in every case, the gene variation was discovered because patients were harmed or failed treatment first. Pharmacogenomics is largely the science of explaining those "unexpected" reactions after the fact — and increasingly, of predicting them before the first dose. ## Pharmacogenetics vs Pharmacogenomics

You will see both terms, sometimes used interchangeably. Traditionally, **pharmacogenetics** referred to the study of a single gene's effect on a drug response (TPMT is the textbook case), while **pharmacogenomics** takes the wider view — many genes across the genome, plus how they interact. In modern usage the boundary has blurred, and either term in an exam question is pointing you at the same idea: inherited variation changes drug handling.

## Where the Field Is Heading

The direction of travel is **pre-emptive testing**: instead of ordering a genetic test after a patient reacts badly, hospitals increasingly test a panel of pharmacogenes once and store the results, so every future prescription can be checked against them. Drug labels from regulators now carry pharmacogenomic information for a growing list of medicines, and clinical guidelines exist for several gene–drug pairs.

There are honest limits. Most drug responses are influenced by many genes plus age, kidney and liver function, other medicines and diet — genetics is one layer, not the whole story. Cost, access and the evidence bar for changing practice all matter too. But the conceptual shift is permanent: variability between patients is not noise, it is biology, and it can be measured.

## What This Means for Your Exams

For FMGE and university exams, pharmacogenomics questions are usually built around the classic pairs above. Anchor on four: **warfarin–CYP2C9/VKORC1**, **thiopurines–TPMT**, **primaquine–G6PD**, and **codeine–CYP2D6**. Know what poor and ultrarapid metabolisers mean, and remember that a prodrug behaves oppositely to an active drug when metabolism speeds up or slows down. Q: What is the difference between pharmacogenetics and pharmacogenomics?
A: Pharmacogenetics traditionally studies how one gene affects response to a drug, while pharmacogenomics looks across many genes and the whole genome. In practice the terms overlap heavily and point to the same idea: inherited variation changes drug handling.
Q: Why does codeine not work in some patients?
A: Codeine is a prodrug that must be converted to morphine by the enzyme CYP2D6. People who are CYP2D6 poor metabolisers make very little morphine and get poor pain relief, while ultrarapid metabolisers convert it dangerously fast.
Q: Which enzyme deficiency causes haemolysis with primaquine?
A: G6PD deficiency. Without enough glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, red blood cells cannot withstand oxidative stress, so oxidative drugs like primaquine can trigger acute haemolytic anaemia.
Q: How does genetics affect warfarin dosing?
A: Variants in CYP2C9 (which metabolises warfarin) and VKORC1 (which encodes warfarin's target) change dose requirements substantially, which is one reason warfarin therapy is individualised and monitored with regular INR testing.
