Open any dhingra ent book discussion among students and one point is agreed: the dhingra text made ent feel manageable, because this book respects the subject's natural structure instead of fighting it. This dhingra ent book guide shows you how Diseases of ENT is organised, which topics examiners return to, and how to study a subject that is really three smaller subjects sharing one textbook.

## Dhingra ENT Book: Three Subjects in One Cover

**Diseases of ENT by PL Dhingra** is organised the way clinics are organised: **ear, nose and paranasal sinuses, and pharynx, larynx and neck**, with supporting sections on audiology, operative procedures, instruments and recent advances. That division is your study plan. Each block has its own anatomy, its own classic diseases and its own favourite instruments and procedures, and examiners draw from all three predictably.

Within each block, the book follows a dependable rhythm: anatomy and physiology first, then methods of examination and investigation, then diseases in a logical order — congenital, inflammatory, traumatic, neoplastic — and finally operations. Once you see the rhythm, revision becomes systematic: for any organ you can ask what can go wrong with it, in those four ways, and you have the chapter map.

Foundations that pay for themselves:

- **Ear** — anatomy of the external, middle and inner ear, and the physiology of hearing and balance
- **Nose** — anatomy of the nasal cavity and sinuses, and the functions of the nose
- **Throat** — anatomy of the pharynx and larynx, including the airway and voice
- **Audiology basics** — types of hearing loss and the tuning fork tests and audiometry that distinguish them

## High-Yield Topics Across Ear, Nose and Throat

In the **ear**, chronic suppurative otitis media in its types, complications of otitis media, otosclerosis, Meniere's disease and hearing loss classification are perennial favourites. The distinction between conductive and sensorineural hearing loss — and the tests that separate them — is foundational and asked in many disguises.

In the **nose and sinuses**, rhinosinusitis and its complications, epistaxis and its management, deviated nasal septum, nasal polyps and allergic rhinitis carry the weight. In the **throat and neck**, tonsillitis and adenoid problems, laryngeal disorders and causes of stridor and hoarseness, deep neck space infections and common neck swellings are the core.

A compact high-yield list:

- **CSOM and its complications** — safe versus unsafe types is a classic distinction
- **Epistaxis** — sites of bleeding and stepwise management
- **Tonsils and adenoids** — indications for surgery and complications
- **Hearing loss** — conductive versus sensorineural, with tuning fork test logic
- **Stridor and airway emergencies** — causes by age and level of obstruction
- **Instruments and procedures** — the standard ENT instruments and what each is used for, a reliable source of image questions

## How to Study ENT Block by Block

Study one block completely before starting the next. ENT facts are numerous but shallow-rooted — they stick when grouped by organ and fail when mixed randomly. For each block, build the same three documents: an **anatomy sketch** you can reproduce, a **disease table** organised by the congenital–inflammatory–traumatic–tumour rhythm, and a **procedures and instruments page** with a picture attached to each name.

Hearing deserves special handling because it is conceptual, not just factual. Work through the logic of Rinne, Weber and Schwabach tests with examples until you can reason out any combination an examiner invents; this small topic generates a disproportionate number of questions and is pure profit once understood.

Clinical correlation is unusually easy in this subject, and worth exploiting. ENT outpatient departments run on the book's core list — ear discharge, hearing complaints, nasal obstruction, sore throats, hoarseness — so a single posting week shows you most of what the chapters describe. Watch how the examination is done, in what order and with which instruments, and the practical sections of Dhingra stop being lists and become descriptions of things you have seen performed.

Practical habits:

- **Learn instruments visually** — name, picture and use together, never the name alone
- **Pair every disease with its complication list** — ENT complications follow anatomy, so they are logical, not arbitrary
- **Revise the ear's anatomy drawing** before every revision cycle; half the ear questions are applied anatomy
- **Keep a cancers page** — common head and neck malignancies, their sites, spread and presentation in outline

## Common Mistakes in ENT Preparation

The first mistake is treating ENT as a minor subject to be skimmed in the final week. Like ophthalmology, it is compact and pattern-based, and in FMGE it contributes a full subject's share of questions where prepared students score heavily. Skimming produces recognition without recall, which MCQs punish.

The second mistake is ignoring instruments and operative basics as "practical exam only" material. Image-based questions on instruments, and factual questions on standard procedures and their complications, are regular features of the written paper too. The third is studying diseases without their anatomy — in ENT, the route of spread of infection and the relations of structures explain most complications, and questions test exactly those routes.

A final mistake is mixing the three blocks in revision. Keep ear, nose and throat revisions separate and complete; the subject's marks come from breadth across all three, not depth in a favourite one.

## A Compact Subject, Finished Properly

ENT rewards the same approach as ophthalmology: one disciplined pass in the book's own order, tables and sketches as you go, visual learning for instruments, and short repeated revisions. Done that way, Dhingra converts a fact-heavy subject into one of your most dependable scoring areas.
