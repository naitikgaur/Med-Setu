Ask a room of FMGE aspirants how many hours to study for fmge and you will hear everything from four to fourteen. The spread exists because the question, by itself, is incomplete. Hours only mean something against two other numbers: **how many months you have** and **how much of each hour is active work**. This article gives you a practical framework for deciding your daily hours — and, more importantly, for making those hours produce marks in a 300-question exam where the pass line is **150**.

## How many hours to study for FMGE: decide by your timeline, not by someone else's routine

**Six or more months available.** A sustainable pace of **6–8 focused hours a day** is enough to complete a first reading of all 19 subjects, a second pass, and regular mock tests. At this timeline, consistency is the whole game — six hours daily for six months is over a thousand hours of preparation.

**Three to four months available.** Plan on **8–10 focused hours a day**. The syllabus does not shrink, so the daily load grows. At this pace you must prioritise by weightage from day one: Medicine (33 questions), Surgery (32), OBG (30) and Community Medicine (30) first, everything else in high-yield form.

**Under two months.** You are in revision-and-testing territory, and **9–11 hours** split between revision, question practice and mocks is realistic for a short burst — but only because the period is brief. This pace is not sustainable for months, and it assumes you have already studied the subjects once.

Notice what these ranges share: they describe *focused* hours. A focused hour means phone away, one subject, one task, with questions being solved — not a textbook open beside a social feed.

## What should happen inside the hours

An hour of FMGE preparation can be spent three ways, and the mix matters more than the total:

- **Reading and notes** — necessary for first coverage, but the lowest-yield activity per hour if done alone.
- **Solving questions** — the highest-yield activity. The exam is 300 MCQs; every hour in the [question bank](/qbank/) trains the exact skill being tested, and wrong answers show you precisely what to read next.
- **Revision** — re-visiting notes, marked questions and errors. This is what moves material from "I studied that" to "I can answer that in the hall".

A workable daily split during the main study phase is roughly **40% reading, 40% questions, 20% revision** of earlier material. In the final month, flip it: revision and mocks should take most of the day. Candidates who spend 10 hours a day only reading are routinely beaten by candidates who spend 7 hours split properly — because the second group's hours are aimed at the answer sheet.

## Quality signals: are your hours working?

Do not judge a study day by how tired you feel. Judge it by outputs:

- **Questions attempted today** — a visible daily count, in Practice or Exam mode
- **New topics closed** — finished *and* tested, not just read
- **Errors logged** — every mock and practice mistake written into one error log
- **Weekly mock score** — one full paper a week from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) or the [grand test series](/tests/) tells you whether the hours are converting

If your weekly mock score is climbing toward and past 150, your hours are working — whatever the number is. If it is flat for three weeks, adding two more hours of the same activity will not fix it; changing the *mix* will.

## The marathon trap

Fourteen-hour days have a hidden cost structure. Beyond a point, extra hours are low-concentration hours: you read the same paragraph twice, silly mistakes multiply in practice sets, and sleep — which is when memory consolidates — gets cut to pay for it. Then the next day starts at a deficit. Many repeaters describe exactly this spiral: enormous effort, flat scores. The fix is unglamorous: a hard stop in the evening, a weekly half-day off, and sleep treated as part of the timetable rather than what is left over.

There is also an honesty point. Nobody studies 12 focused hours a day for months. People *sit* for 12 hours and study perhaps 7 of them. Track the focused number, not the seated number — a stopwatch or a simple tally per session keeps you truthful.

## A simple way to set your number this week

1. Count your available days until the exam.
2. Pick the band above that matches your timeline.
3. Schedule the hours in fixed blocks — for example three blocks with real breaks — at the same times daily, so starting costs no willpower.
4. Reserve the last block of the day for questions and revision, never for starting a new heavy topic.
5. Review every Sunday with a full mock or a [subject-wise test](/subjects/), and adjust the mix — not just the hours — for the next week.

The candidates who clear FMGE are rarely the ones who studied the most hours in a single day; they are the ones whose hours were still happening, at good quality, in month four and month five. Choose a number you can repeat daily, fill it with questions and revision rather than passive reading, and let your weekly mock scores — checked honestly against the 150 line — be the judge. Start by timing one honest day of practice in the [question bank](/qbank/) today, and build your routine around what that day actually looked like.

Q: How many hours should I study daily for FMGE with 6 months left?
A: Around 6–8 focused hours a day is sufficient over six months — enough for two readings of the syllabus plus regular mock tests — as long as the hours include daily question practice and revision, not reading alone.
Q: Is studying 12 hours a day necessary to pass FMGE?
A: No. FMGE is a pass-mark exam (150 of 300), not a rank competition. Sustainable, focused hours with a good mix of reading, questions and revision beat unsustainable marathon days that destroy sleep and concentration.
Q: What is the best way to split FMGE study hours?
A: During the main phase, roughly 40% reading, 40% solving questions and 20% revision works well. In the final month, shift most hours to revision and full mock tests.
Q: How do I know if my study hours are effective?
A: Track outputs, not hours: questions attempted daily, topics finished and tested, errors logged, and a weekly full mock score. If the mock score is not moving toward 150 over a few weeks, change the activity mix rather than just adding hours.
