The most underused advantage in this exam is built into its rules, and a sound **fmge attempt strategy no negative marking** plan starts from one fact: a wrong answer costs exactly the same as a blank answer — nothing. The FMGE paper has **300 MCQs, Part A 150 and Part B 150, +1 for every correct answer, no negative marking, and a pass mark of 150**. In an exam with negative marking, a blank can be the smart choice. In the FMGE, a blank is a guaranteed zero on a question where even a poorly informed choice had some chance of being right. Yet in every session, candidates leave questions unattempted — out of habit carried over from other exams, out of time pressure, or out of a feeling that guessing is somehow against the spirit of the test. It is not. This article works through the logic carefully: why attempting all 300 is mathematically sound, how to guess intelligently rather than randomly, and how to manage your time so that no question is ever lost to the clock.

## The FMGE Attempt Strategy No Negative Marking Makes Possible

Consider the worst case: a question you know nothing about, with four options. A pure random choice is right one time in four, on average. That is not a study strategy — it is arithmetic. Across many such questions, guessing returns roughly a quarter of their marks, while leaving them blank returns exactly zero, every time. Nobody is claiming guesswork replaces preparation; across a whole paper, blind guessing alone comes nowhere near 150. The claim is narrower and unanswerable: **between two answers that both feel uncertain, the attempted one can gain a mark and the blank one cannot.** When a pass is decided at a fixed line of 150, the handful of marks recovered from questions you half-knew can be the handful that matters.

The logic strengthens as your knowledge improves. Most "guesses" in a real attempt are not blind. They are questions where you can eliminate one or two options with confidence. Eliminate one option and a guess is right about one time in three; eliminate two and it is a coin toss. A prepared candidate makes dozens of such judgements in a paper, and the expected gain from attempting all of them — rather than skipping the ones that feel uncomfortable — is substantial. This is reasoning, not a promise: the point is that the rules reward the attempt every single time.

## The Two-Pass Method Inside Each Part

Attempt strategy is mostly time strategy. The reliable way to attempt all 300 without chaos is the **two-pass method** within each part:

### First pass: bank the certain marks

Move steadily through the part, answering every question you are confident about and every question that yields to quick elimination. When a question resists — a long vignette, an unfamiliar topic, two options that look identical — mark it and move on immediately. Do not let one hard question consume the time of four easy ones. Your first pass should cover the whole part and collect the large majority of your marks.

### Second pass: work the marked questions

Return to the marked questions with the remaining time. Now spend your effort where it earns most: questions where you can eliminate options, recall a related fact, or reason from the stem. Decide, attempt, and move on. In the final minutes, ensure **nothing remains unattempted** — if a question is still a complete blank slate, choose an option anyway. A random attempt in the last minute costs nothing and occasionally pays.

## How to Guess Intelligently

Educated guessing is a skill with learnable components:

- **Eliminate with reasons, not vibes.** Cross out options you can argue against — wrong age group, wrong side, a drug contraindicated in the condition described, an investigation that does not answer the question asked. Each argued elimination raises the value of your guess.
- **Read the stem's demand precisely.** "Most likely diagnosis", "best next step", "drug of choice" and "all EXCEPT" are different questions wearing similar clothes. Many wrong attempts are right answers to a question that was not asked.
- **Trust absolute words cautiously.** Options claiming "always" or "never" are often wrong in clinical medicine, where exceptions abound — a tendency to use as a tiebreaker, not a rule.
- **Use the vignette's details.** Examiners include age, sex, duration and key findings because they discriminate between options. A detail you ignored is often the one that eliminates two options at once.
- **Do not change answers without a reason.** If you revisit a question and find a concrete error — a misread word, a recalled fact — change it. If you merely feel less sure than before, your first reasoning is usually the better evidence.

## What This Strategy Is Not

It is not a licence to underprepare. Guessing recovers marks at the margins of a prepared attempt; it does not create a pass out of an unprepared one. The bulk of your 150 must come from knowledge, built through study and tested through the [previous-year papers](/papers/) and the [grand test series](/tests/). Nor is it an excuse to rush: an attempt made after ten seconds of elimination is worth far more than a click made in panic. The strategy works because it is calm, systematic and practised — which is why you should rehearse it in every mock, using the attempt analysis in your report to count unattempted questions and slow questions until both numbers approach zero.

## Practising the Strategy Before Exam Day

Build the habit in three steps. First, in your next mock, simply count how many questions you left blank and how many you changed — awareness alone changes behaviour. Second, practise the two-pass method deliberately in the [question bank](/qbank/) Exam mode sets, where the same runner and palette apply without the full-paper time cost. Third, in your final mocks, set a hard rule: the last five minutes of each part belong to unattempted questions only. By exam day, "attempt everything" should be a reflex, not a resolution.

The rules of this exam are unusually forgiving of uncertainty — but only for candidates who use them. Prepare thoroughly, eliminate honestly, attempt all 300, and let the arithmetic work for you. Start rehearsing today with a timed set on MedSetu's [question bank](/qbank/), and check your attempt analysis after every session until leaving a blank feels as wrong as it should.

Q: Is there negative marking in FMGE?
A: No. The FMGE has no negative marking. Every correct answer carries +1, and a wrong answer scores the same as a blank — zero.
Q: Should I attempt all 300 questions in FMGE?
A: Yes. Since a wrong answer costs nothing, an attempt always has at least some chance of a mark while a blank has none. Attempting everything is the logical strategy.
Q: How can I guess more effectively in FMGE?
A: Eliminate options you can argue against, read exactly what the stem asks, use the clinical details given, and prefer your first reasoned answer unless you find a concrete error.

A: No. Educated guessing only recovers marginal marks within a prepared attempt. Most of the 150 passing marks must come from knowledge and practice.
