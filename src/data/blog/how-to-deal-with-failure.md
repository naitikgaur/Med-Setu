The result is out, and your name is not on the list. Before anything else, understand this: learning **how to deal with failure in exams** is a skill every long medical career quietly demands, and a failed attempt at an exam like FMGE is an event, not an identity. You are a doctor who has not cleared a licensing exam yet — that "yet" is doing real work in that sentence. This guide is not about motivational quotes. It is about the practical sequence that repeat attempters who eventually clear actually follow: feel it, read it honestly, fix the system, and go again with a plan instead of just hope.

## How to Deal With Failure in Exams: The First Week

The first week is not for studying, and pretending otherwise usually backfires. Grief after a failed result is real — you invested months, money and other people's expectations. Give it a few honest days.

- **Tell the people who need to know, once.** Family, close friends, your support circle. One clear conversation beats weeks of avoiding the topic.
- **Stay off result-day social media.** Other people's celebrations and cut-off speculation will only distort your thinking while you are raw.
- **Do not make permanent decisions in week one.** Dropping the next attempt, changing countries, changing careers — none of these should be decided in the worst week of the year.
- **Rest properly.** Sleep, eat, step outside. Your next attempt will be built by a rested person, not this exhausted one.

What you are doing here is not quitting. You are clearing the emotional static so that when you analyse the attempt, you can actually see it clearly.

## Read the Result Honestly, Then Close It

Once the first week passes, sit down with the result like a clinician with a case file. FMGE needs **150 out of 300** to pass, with no negative marking — so your score tells a precise story if you let it.

Ask three questions, in writing:

- **How far was I?** A score of 138 and a score of 96 are different problems. The first is a conversion problem — knowledge exists but leaks under exam conditions. The second is a coverage problem — whole areas were never built.
- **Which subjects carried me, and which sank me?** Most repeat attempters discover the same pattern: two or three weak subjects quietly cost 25–40 marks while they kept polishing their strongest subject.
- **Was it knowledge, or was it the exam itself?** Running out of time, misreading stems, changing correct answers — these are technique failures, and they are the cheapest kind to fix.

Write the answers down, then close the file. You will consult this diagnosis when planning, but you do not live inside it. Rumination feels like analysis; it is not.

## Fix the System, Not Just the Syllabus

Almost every failed attempt, honestly examined, traces back to a **system failure** rather than an intelligence failure. The common ones:

- **Coverage without retrieval.** Reading notes for months but rarely testing recall. Knowledge that has never been retrieved under pressure often does not appear under pressure.
- **No full-length practice.** FMGE is 300 questions in two sittings. Attempting it without having repeatedly sat full papers is like training for a marathon with only short walks.
- **Revision debt.** Topics studied once in month one and never revisited are, by exam day, effectively unstudied.
- **Ignoring the weak third.** Comfort studying keeps you in subjects you already like while the weak subjects decide your result.

Your next attempt should be engineered around whichever of these was yours. That means scheduled full mocks from the [previous-year papers](/papers/), a revision cycle that revisits every topic at planned intervals, and deliberate extra weight on your two weakest subjects — not equal time for all nineteen.

## The Next Attempt: A Practical Timeline

A repeat attempt is not a first attempt done again; it is a shorter, sharper campaign, because your base knowledge is real even if it was incomplete.

- **Weeks 1–2:** Diagnosis and planning. Map every subject into strong, shaky and weak using your result and one fresh mock.
- **Months 1–2:** Weak-subject repair plus steady revision of the strong ones, so they do not decay while you fix the gaps.
- **Middle phase:** Weekly full-length mocks in real exam conditions, each followed by a written error review. Your mock scores matter less than your error patterns shrinking.
- **Final month:** Revision only, plus mocks. This is where attempts are converted — most repeat clearers say the last month of revision and testing is what changed the outcome.

Track one number through all of this: your mock score trend. It will wobble. The trend, not any single test, is your truth.

## A Word on Comparison and Shame

Someone from your batch will clear before you. That is arithmetic, not a judgement on your worth. Medical careers are long, and the year you spent repeating will be a footnote in a forty-year practice — invisible to every patient you will ever treat. What stays with you is not the failed attempt; it is whether you built the honesty and systems that the failure offered to teach you.

When you are ready to begin again, start gently and measurably: one topic set a day in the [question bank](/qbank/), one full paper a week from the start rather than the end. MedSetu is free, needs no sign-up and stores nothing about you — your attempts live only in your browser — so the only thing a fresh start costs you is the decision to make it. The result you got is information. What you build from it is the story.

Q: How long should I rest after failing FMGE before starting again?
A: Most repeat attempters benefit from one to two weeks of genuine rest, then a structured restart. Longer breaks risk losing the knowledge base you already have; starting the very next day usually means studying while emotionally exhausted, which does not stick.
Q: I scored close to 150. What should I change?
A: A near-miss usually means a conversion problem, not a knowledge problem: exam technique, time management, weak-subject leakage or unforced errors. Prioritise full-length timed mocks, written error reviews and your two weakest subjects rather than re-reading everything.
Q: Is it worth attempting FMGE again?
A: That is a personal decision, but factually the exam is held twice a year by NBEMS, has no negative marking, and a large share of those who clear do so on a repeat attempt after fixing specific gaps. A diagnosed, system-based retry is a very different attempt from simply repeating the same preparation.
Q: How do I handle family disappointment after a failed attempt?
A: Have one honest conversation: share your score, your diagnosis of what went wrong and your concrete plan for the next attempt. Families usually respond better to a clear plan than to either silence or vague promises.
