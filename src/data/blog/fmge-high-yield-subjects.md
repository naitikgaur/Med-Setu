Ask where FMGE marks hide and the answer is not a secret list of topics — it is a published table. NBEMS distributes the 300 questions across 19 subjects in fixed proportions, and those proportions are the most honest definition of **fmge high yield subjects** there is. This article lays out the complete weightage, groups it into tiers, and shows how to convert the table into decisions: what to study first, what to study deepest, and where the cheapest marks in the paper are sitting. In an exam with a pass mark of **150 out of 300** and **no negative marking**, planning by weightage is not gaming the system — it is simply reading the blueprint before building.

## Tier 1: the big four — 125 questions

**Medicine (33), Surgery (32), OBG (30) and Community Medicine (30)** together supply nearly **42% of the entire paper**. A candidate cannot fail these and pass comfortably, and cannot master these and fail easily. Medicine is the deepest syllabus of the four and rewards system-wise study plus heavy question practice. Surgery's questions lean on classifications, staging and management steps. OBG is the best marks-per-topic ratio among the giants — a bounded syllabus carrying 30 questions. Community Medicine is the most underrated: Park-based facts, programmes, vaccines and epidemiology that are entirely learnable, carrying the same 30 questions as OBG. If your preparation time is short, it is short *here last*.

## Tier 2: the 17s and 15s — 96 questions

**Anatomy, Physiology and Biochemistry (17 each)** and **Paediatrics, Ophthalmology and ENT (15 each)** form the second band. The pre-clinical 17s frighten candidates because the subjects are vast — but 17 questions do not require the whole subject. They require its high-yield core: neuroanatomy and recurring regions, cardiovascular-respiratory-renal physiology, metabolic pathways and molecular basics. Paediatrics (milestones, immunisation, common childhood disease), Ophthalmology (cataract, glaucoma, refractive errors, emergencies) and ENT are more bounded: a focused block genuinely covers most of what these subjects ask.

## Tier 3: the para-clinical 13s and the 10s — 59 questions

**Pathology, Microbiology and Pharmacology (13 each)** punch above their count because they leak into other subjects: pathology concepts inside Medicine and Surgery questions, pharmacology inside every clinical paper section. Studying them well is worth more than 39 marks in practice. **Forensic Medicine (10)** and **Radiology (10)** are compact and pattern-driven — legal procedures, poisons and injuries on one side; imaging signs and investigation-of-choice on the other. Both reward a single thorough pass plus revision, and both are frequently left unprepared by candidates who ran out of time — which makes them quiet differentiators.

## Tier 4: the 5-mark subjects — 20 cheap questions

**Psychiatry, Dermatology, Anaesthesia and Orthopaedics (5 each)** have the smallest syllabi in the exam. Twenty questions is 13% of the pass mark, available from subjects you can cover in days rather than weeks. Skipping them is the classic unforced error: candidates leave these 20 marks on the table while grinding a second extra week into a Tier 1 subject for perhaps two. High-yield does not only mean "biggest" — it also means **most marks per hour of study**, and by that measure the 5-mark subjects are the highest-yield in the paper.

## Turning the table into a plan

- **Sequence by tier, revise by decay.** Study Tier 1 first and deepest; bring fact-heavy Community Medicine and Pharmacology back again close to the exam.
- **Set mock targets by tier.** After each full paper from the [previous-year papers](/papers/), check your per-subject scores against the weightage: losing 15 marks in Medicine is a different emergency from losing 3 in Dermatology.
- **Use subject tests to certify each tier.** As you finish a subject, verify it with the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) before moving on — weightage tells you the order, tests tell you the truth.
- **Practise the mix, not just the parts.** The [grand test series](/tests/) and topic practice in the [question bank](/qbank/) expose you to subjects in the same proportions the real paper uses, so your sense of the exam matches the exam.

## A worked example: reading your own score against the table

Suppose a full mock returns 138 — twelve short. The total alone tells you nothing useful; the table tells you where the twelve are hiding. If Medicine gave you 16 of 33 and Surgery 17 of 32, your two biggest subjects are performing at roughly half their value, and the missing marks are concentrated exactly where recovery is cheapest: a focused fortnight on those two subjects has 65 questions to work with. If instead the big four are healthy and the losses are scattered across the 5-mark subjects, the fix is one small-subject sweep. Same total, opposite prescriptions. This is why per-subject analysis after every mock matters more than the headline score — the weightage table turns a vague "I need to improve" into a named subject, a named week and a measurable target.

The weightage table will not study for you. But it guarantees that the hours you do study are spent where the questions actually are — and in a pass-mark exam, that allocation, repeated daily, is most of the battle. Open the table, mark your current strength honestly against each row, and let the gaps in the big tiers set your next month's timetable.
