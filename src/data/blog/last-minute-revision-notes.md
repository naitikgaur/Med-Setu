The final weeks before a medical exam are a different country. There is no time to re-read chapters, and the notes that served you all year — thorough, detailed, careful — are suddenly too big to use. Last minute revision notes solve this problem: they are a separate, ruthless document containing only what must be fresh in your mind on exam morning. Building them is not copying your old notes smaller. It is deciding, topic by topic, what is actually worth marks — and letting everything else go.

## Last Minute Revision Notes: What Belongs in Them

Think of these notes as the answer to one question: *if I could carry five pages per subject into the exam hall, what would be on them?* Four kinds of material earn their place:

**Volatile facts.** The things you know but keep dropping: normal values and cut-offs, drug doses and durations, staging criteria, classifications, scores. These fade fastest and are asked constantly, so they dominate last-minute notes.

**Comparisons you confuse.** Every student has pairs they swap under pressure — two similar diseases, two similar drugs, two similar investigations. One table per confused pair, built from your own errors, is worth pages of general notes.

**Your mistakes list.** By the final weeks you have attempted hundreds of questions. The ones you got wrong form the most personal high-yield list in existence: it is, literally, the gap between you and a higher score. Every mistake that taught you something becomes a line in these notes.

**One-line frameworks.** For big topics, the skeleton only: causes in one line, the management steps in order, the complications as a list. In the exam you do not need the essay — you need the structure that lets the details reassemble.

## What to Cut Without Mercy

- **Anything you have never once got wrong.** If a fact has survived every test all year, it does not need exam-week space.
- **Background and explanations.** Understanding was the work of the earlier months. These notes hold conclusions, not reasoning.
- **Rare, low-yield detail.** The final weeks are for protecting the common marks, not hunting exotic ones.
- **Neatness.** These notes are a tool, not a portfolio. Crossings-out, arrows and cramped additions are fine — nobody awards marks for handwriting in a document only you will read.

## How to Build Them in the Final Weeks

Do not sit down and rewrite the syllabus — that way lies panic and a wasted week. Build the notes **from your revision and your tests** instead:

1. Revise a subject from your regular notes, quickly.
2. Attempt questions on it immediately — a subject test or a topic set.
3. Whatever you missed, hesitated on or could not recall goes into the last-minute notes for that subject. Whatever you knew cold does not.

This produces notes that are automatically personal and automatically weighted towards your weaknesses. A subject you are strong in might generate half a page; a weak one, three pages — and that imbalance is correct. ## How to Use Them in the Last Days

In the final week, these notes become your primary text. Work through them by **covering and recalling**, not by reading: cover a table, produce it, check. The testing effect applies here as everywhere — retrieval with feedback is what fixes memory, and Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing among the highest-utility techniques while rating plain re-reading among the lowest. A page you have actively recalled three times in the last week is worth more than a chapter read once.

Keep the sessions short and rotating: one subject's notes, a break, the next subject. And protect the night before the exam — a light pass over the most volatile pages (values, doses, criteria) is useful; an all-night cram trades a clear head for a handful of facts, and the exchange rate is poor.

**Order the subjects by weakness, not by preference.** In the last week it is tempting to revise your favourite subject once more because it feels productive. Resist it. Rank your subjects by your recent test performance and give the earliest, freshest slots to the weakest ones; strong subjects need only a light pass over their volatile pages. If you are unsure where you stand, one full mock in the final fortnight will rank them for you honestly — and its topic-wise breakdown will tell you not just which subjects, but which exact topics, deserve the remaining days.

One more rule for the final days: **no new sources.** Not a new book, not a new set of printed notes, not a senior's legendary PDF. New material at this stage mostly manufactures anxiety and displaces the revision that actually scores. Your own compressed notes, built from your own mistakes, are the highest-yield document you own.

Q: When should I start making last-minute revision notes?
A: In the final three to four weeks. Build them from your revision sessions and mock-test mistakes rather than rewriting the syllabus — whatever you miss or hesitate on earns a line, whatever you know cold does not.
Q: How many pages should last-minute notes be?
A: As few as you can manage while keeping the volatile facts, your confused comparisons and your mistakes list — often a handful of pages per subject. If you cannot revise them fully in the last days, they are too long.
Q: Should I buy or download printed rapid-revision notes instead?
A: Printed notes cannot contain your mistakes or your personal confusions, which are the highest-yield part. Use your own compressed notes as the base; printed material, if used at all, is only a checklist for gaps.
Q: What should I revise on the last day before the exam?
A: A light, active pass over your most volatile pages — values, doses, criteria, and your mistakes list — using cover-and-recall. Stop in good time; sleep is worth more marks than a few extra facts.
