Pathology is the bridge subject of medicine: everything in medicine and surgery is applied pathology with a patient attached. For the exam itself, **pathology for FMGE** carries **13 questions** — modest on paper, but the concepts you build here silently answer questions in at least four other subjects. Students struggle with pathology for one reason: volume. Robbins is enormous, and an unplanned cover-to-cover attempt collapses by the systemic half. The solution is not to study less pathology; it is to study it in the right order, with general pathology mastered first and systemic pathology organised around morphology and mechanisms. This guide shows you how.

## Pathology for FMGE: weightage and question style

The FMGE by **NBEMS** consists of 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, **no negative marking**, pass mark **150 out of 300**. Pathology contributes 13 questions, drawn from:

- **General pathology** — cell injury, inflammation, healing, haemodynamic disorders, neoplasia basics, genetic and environmental diseases
- **Systemic pathology** — cardiovascular, respiratory, GI, hepatobiliary, renal, haematology, endocrine and CNS pathology
- **Morphology and image questions** — gross specimens and histopathology images: identify the lesion or the diagnosis from its appearance
- **Laboratory and marker questions** — tumour markers, cytogenetics of tumours, stains and classic microscopic findings

Pathology questions are concept-plus-recognition: they ask whether you understand a mechanism *and* can recognise its classical description — the "currant jelly", "nutmeg liver", "starry sky" style of one-liners is very much alive here.

## High-yield areas

- **Cell injury and inflammation** — reversible vs irreversible injury, free radicals, mediators of inflammation, chronic inflammation and granulomas, wound healing and keloid vs hypertrophic scar
- **Neoplasia** — tumour markers, paraneoplastic syndromes, oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes, carcinogens, grading vs staging, common translocations
- **Haematology** — anaemias (iron deficiency, megaloblastic, haemolytic), leukaemias and lymphomas, bleeding disorders; this cluster overlaps with medicine and is doubly valuable
- **Cardiovascular pathology** — atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction timeline and complications, rheumatic heart disease, endocarditis, cardiomyopathies
- **Respiratory pathology** — pneumonia types, tuberculosis pathology, obstructive vs restrictive diseases, lung tumours
- **Renal pathology** — glomerulonephritis patterns (nephritic vs nephrotic), classic electron-microscopy and immunofluorescence findings
- **GI and liver** — hepatitis, cirrhosis and its complications, inflammatory bowel disease, GI tumours and their markers
- **Genetic disorders** — inheritance patterns, Down syndrome, cystic fibrosis, storage disorders

## Books and how to use them

- **Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease** — the gold standard. Read it selectively: all of general pathology properly, and the high-yield systemic chapters; use its summary boxes and tables heavily
- **Harsh Mohan's Pathology** — a solid Indian text, useful if Robbins feels too dense for a first pass
- **Your own tables** — tumour markers, translocations, stains, and "classic finding" one-liners collected in one place will be your highest-yield revision asset

A practical approach many toppers use: read the Robbins chapter, then immediately compress it into a one-page sheet of mechanisms, morphology keywords and markers. The sheet — not the chapter — is what you revise later.

## Study method for a high-volume subject

- **General before systemic, always** — inflammation, cell injury and neoplasia concepts are reused in every systemic chapter; mastering them first halves the difficulty of the rest
- **Learn by comparison tables** — nephritic vs nephrotic, ulcerative colitis vs Crohn's, benign vs malignant, exudate vs transudate: FMGE loves testing the differentiating point, so study in pairs
- **Morphology sessions** — regularly look at gross and microscopy images (your textbook's plates are enough) and practise naming the lesion and its three classic features
- **Mechanism chains** — for each disease, be able to say: cause → mechanism → morphology → clinical consequence. Questions are picked from somewhere along that chain
- **Question-driven revision** — after each system, solve topic-wise questions from a [question bank](/qbank/); pathology answers often hinge on one keyword in the stem, and only practice teaches you to spot it

## Revision plan

- **First pass:** general pathology thoroughly; systemic pathology system-wise with one-page compression sheets
- **Second pass:** comparison tables, marker and translocation lists, morphology images; a timed pathology test from the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/)
- **Final phase:** revise alongside medicine — revise a medicine system, then its pathology; finish with full mocks from the [grand test series](/tests/)

## Common mistakes that cost pathology marks

- **Attempting Robbins cover to cover in order** — this is the most common pathology failure. The book is a reference arranged for completeness, not a course arranged for learning; without prioritisation, students reach the exam with superb cell injury and untouched systemic pathology
- **Skipping the morphology** — pathology is a visual subject at heart. Students who never look at gross specimens and microscopy plates lose the image questions and, worse, lose the descriptive vocabulary ("bosselated", "bread-and-butter", "onion-skin") that text questions are built from
- **Studying diseases one at a time, never in pairs** — the exam's favourite move is to place two similar conditions side by side and ask for the differentiator. If you have never compared them, you will guess between them every time
- **Treating haematology as a small chapter** — blood is where pathology, medicine and physiology overlap most densely; anaemias and leukaemias deserve more time than their page count suggests

Q: How many pathology questions are there in FMGE?
A: Pathology carries 13 questions in the 300-question FMGE paper conducted by NBEMS, which has no negative marking and a pass mark of 150 out of 300.
Q: Is Robbins necessary for FMGE pathology?
A: Robbins is the reference standard and ideal for general pathology and high-yield systemic topics. Read it selectively and compress each chapter into your own one-page sheets for revision.
Q: Which pathology topics are most important for FMGE?
A: Cell injury, inflammation, neoplasia (markers, oncogenes, paraneoplastic syndromes), haematology, cardiovascular and renal pathology are consistently high-yield.
Q: How do I handle morphology and image questions in pathology?
A: Practise regularly with textbook gross and histopathology plates, learning to name the lesion plus its classic features — recognition is a trainable skill.
