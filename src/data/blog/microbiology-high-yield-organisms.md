If you revise **microbiology high yield organisms** properly, microbiology becomes one of the most scoring subjects in FMGE. The paper rarely asks long descriptions. It asks a classic association: an organism, the disease it causes, a characteristic clinical feature, or one laboratory clue that separates it from close options. FMGE is conducted by NBEMS — 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, no negative marking, pass mark 150 out of 300 — so every one-liner you can recall is a direct mark. This guide organises the important organisms by group, the way standard textbooks such as Ananthanarayan and Paniker present them, and the way the exam tests them.

## Microbiology high yield organisms: bacteria you must know

Start with the Gram-positive cocci and bacilli, then move to the Gram-negative organisms.

- **Staphylococcus aureus** — coagulase-positive, golden-yellow colonies; causes abscesses, osteomyelitis, infective endocarditis, scalded skin syndrome and toxin-mediated food poisoning and toxic shock syndrome
- **Streptococcus pyogenes** (Group A) — beta-haemolytic, bacitracin-sensitive; pharyngitis, scarlet fever, rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis
- **Streptococcus pneumoniae** — alpha-haemolytic, optochin-sensitive, bile-soluble, lancet-shaped diplococci with a capsule; the classic cause of lobar pneumonia and meningitis
- **Enterococcus** — grows in 6.5% salt and bile; a common cause of urinary infection and endocarditis
- **Bacillus anthracis** — large Gram-positive bacilli in chains, medusa-head colonies; cutaneous anthrax with a black eschar
- **Clostridium tetani** — drumstick appearance from terminal spores; tetanus from tetanospasmin, which blocks inhibitory neurotransmitter release
- **Clostridium botulinum** — botulinum toxin blocks acetylcholine release, causing flaccid paralysis; associated with canned food
- **Clostridium perfringens** — gas gangrene and food poisoning; double zone of haemolysis and the Nagler reaction
- **Corynebacterium diphtheriae** — Chinese-letter arrangement, Albert stain shows metachromatic granules; diphtheria toxin causes a pseudomembrane and myocarditis
- **Listeria monocytogenes** — tumbling motility at 25°C; meningitis in neonates and immunocompromised patients

For Gram-negative bacteria, learn the organism with its medium, toxin or clinical clue:

- **Neisseria meningitidis** and **Neisseria gonorrhoeae** — Gram-negative diplococci, oxidase-positive; meningococcal meningitis with petechial rash, and gonorrhoea respectively
- **Escherichia coli** — lactose-fermenting, metallic sheen on EMB agar; the commonest cause of urinary tract infection, and enterotoxigenic strains cause travellers' diarrhoea
- **Salmonella Typhi** — typhoid fever, rose spots, Widal test; chronic carriage in the gallbladder
- **Shigella** — non-lactose fermenter, very low infective dose; bacillary dysentery with blood and mucus in stool
- **Vibrio cholerae** — comma-shaped, oxidase-positive, rice-water stools; cholera toxin increases cyclic AMP, grown on TCBS agar as yellow colonies
- **Campylobacter jejuni** — curved rods, grows at 42°C; a common cause of bacterial diarrhoea, associated with Guillain-Barré syndrome
- **Helicobacter pylori** — urease-positive, spiral organism of the stomach; peptic ulcer disease and gastric carcinoma association
- **Pseudomonas aeruginosa** — blue-green pigment, fruity odour; infections in burns, cystic fibrosis and hospital settings
- **Haemophilus influenzae** — requires factors X and V, satellite phenomenon; meningitis and epiglottitis in children
- **Bordetella pertussis** — whooping cough, Bordet-Gengou medium
- **Mycobacterium tuberculosis** — acid-fast bacilli on Ziehl-Neelsen stain, Lowenstein-Jensen medium; tuberculosis, caseating granulomas
- **Mycobacterium leprae** — leprosy, cannot be grown on artificial media, seen in skin smears
- **Treponema pallidum** — syphilis; dark-ground microscopy, VDRL as a screening test and TPHA as a specific test
- **Leptospira** — leptospirosis with conjunctival suffusion and jaundice, associated with water contaminated by animal urine

## Viruses: structure, disease and one defining fact

Virus questions usually test whether the virus is DNA or RNA, enveloped or non-enveloped, its transmission route, or a pathognomonic feature.

- **Hepatitis A** — RNA virus, faeco-oral route, acute hepatitis, no chronic carrier state
- **Hepatitis B** — DNA virus, blood and sexual transmission; HBsAg is the first marker to appear, anti-HBs indicates immunity, HBeAg indicates high infectivity
- **Hepatitis C** — RNA virus, blood-borne; the commonest viral cause of chronic hepatitis and post-transfusion hepatitis in many settings
- **Hepatitis D** — defective virus that requires hepatitis B for replication
- **Hepatitis E** — RNA virus, water-borne; fulminant hepatitis in pregnancy is the classic association
- **HIV** — retrovirus with reverse transcriptase, targets CD4 T cells; diagnosis by ELISA-based testing algorithms and viral load by PCR
- **Rabies virus** — bullet-shaped RNA virus, Negri bodies in neurons; hydrophobia after an animal bite
- **Influenza virus** — segmented RNA genome; antigenic drift causes epidemics and antigenic shift causes pandemics
- **Dengue virus** — flavivirus transmitted by **Aedes aegypti**; thrombocytopenia and plasma leakage in severe disease
- **Measles virus** — Koplik spots, rash, and subacute sclerosing panencephalitis as a late complication
- **Mumps virus** — parotitis and orchitis
- **Rotavirus** — double-stranded RNA, wheel-shaped; a leading cause of infantile diarrhoea
- **Poliovirus** — faeco-oral transmission; flaccid paralysis from anterior horn cell involvement
- **Herpes simplex and varicella-zoster** — latency in sensory ganglia; multinucleated giant cells on Tzanck smear
- **Epstein-Barr virus** — infectious mononucleosis, atypical lymphocytes, heterophile antibody (Paul-Bunnell) test; associated with Burkitt lymphoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma
- **Human papillomavirus** — types 16 and 18 are associated with cervical carcinoma; types 6 and 11 with genital warts
- **Parvovirus B19** — slapped-cheek disease (erythema infectiosum) and aplastic crisis in haemolytic anaemia

Remember the smallest virus (parvovirus among the classic options), the largest (poxvirus), and that prions are infectious proteins with no nucleic acid — these are favourite one-liners.

## Fungi and parasites: small groups, repeated questions

Fungal questions focus on morphology and the immunocompromised host.

- **Candida albicans** — yeast with pseudohyphae, germ tube positive; oral thrush, vaginal candidiasis and systemic disease in immunocompromised patients
- **Cryptococcus neoformans** — encapsulated yeast, India ink preparation shows the capsule; meningitis in HIV infection, grown features highlighted by mucicarmine stain
- **Aspergillus** — septate hyphae with acute-angle (about 45°) dichotomous branching; aspergilloma in a pre-existing cavity and invasive disease in neutropenia
- **Mucor** — broad, ribbon-like, aseptate hyphae with right-angle branching; mucormycosis in diabetic ketoacidosis, involving sinuses and orbit
- **Dermatophytes** — Trichophyton, Microsporum and Epidermophyton cause tinea (ringworm) of skin, hair and nails
- **Dimorphic fungi** — mould in soil and yeast in tissue; Histoplasma is the classic example, found intracellularly in macrophages

For parasites, pair the organism with its stage, vector or diagnostic form:

- **Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium falciparum** — malaria transmitted by female Anopheles mosquito; falciparum causes cerebral malaria and blackwater fever, vivax forms hypnozoites in the liver causing relapses
- **Entamoeba histolytica** — flask-shaped amoebic liver abscess and amoebic dysentery; trophozoites may contain ingested red blood cells
- **Giardia lamblia** — falling-leaf motility, fat malabsorption and diarrhoea; trophozoite has a characteristic appearance with flagella
- **Leishmania donovani** — kala-azar with fever, splenomegaly and pancytopenia; amastigote (LD) bodies in macrophages, transmitted by the sandfly
- **Wuchereria bancrofti** — filariasis and elephantiasis, transmitted by Culex mosquito; microfilariae show nocturnal periodicity
- **Ascaris lumbricoides** — the commonest helminth infection; intestinal obstruction in heavy infection and Loeffler syndrome during lung migration
- **Hookworm (Ancylostoma duodenale)** — iron-deficiency anaemia from chronic blood loss
- **Taenia solium** — cysticercosis from eggs, including neurocysticercosis, a common cause of seizures
- **Echinococcus granulosus** — hydatid cyst, usually in liver and lung; dog is the definitive host
- **Trichomonas vaginalis** — motile trophozoite only, no cyst stage; frothy vaginal discharge

## How to revise organisms without forgetting them

Do not revise microbiology as long paragraphs. Build one table per group with four columns — organism, disease, diagnostic clue, and one special fact — and revise only the table in your second and third passes. Add culture media as a separate list: TCBS for Vibrio, Lowenstein-Jensen for tuberculosis, chocolate agar for fastidious organisms, and Sabouraud dextrose agar for fungi. Then test yourself immediately. Working through organism-wise questions in a [question bank](/qbank/) shows you which associations you truly recall and which you only recognise when you see the answer.

Q: Which bacteria are most important for FMGE microbiology?
A: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes and pneumoniae, E. coli, Salmonella, Shigella, Vibrio cholerae, Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Pseudomonas are among the most repeatedly tested bacteria.
Q: What is the difference between Aspergillus and Mucor on microscopy?
A: Aspergillus shows septate hyphae with acute-angle branching, while Mucor shows broad, aseptate, ribbon-like hyphae with right-angle branching.
Q: Which hepatitis virus is a DNA virus?
A: Hepatitis B is the DNA virus among the hepatitis viruses; hepatitis A, C, D and E are RNA viruses, and hepatitis D is defective and needs hepatitis B to replicate.
