An ECG strip is one of the few things in the FMGE paper that follows a completely fixed method, which is why revising **ecg basics fmge** examiners love is such good value. FMGE is conducted by NBEMS — 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, no negative marking, pass mark 150 out of 300 — and medicine questions regularly hand you a tracing and ask for the diagnosis, the artery involved, or the next step in a clinical stem. This guide gives you a fixed reading system, then the classic patterns the paper repeats.

## First, know the paper and the standard values

A standard ECG is recorded at **25 mm/s** paper speed and **10 mm/mV** calibration. That gives you the two numbers from which everything else is calculated:

- **1 small square** = 1 mm = **0.04 seconds** (40 ms)
- **1 large square** = 5 mm = **0.20 seconds** (200 ms)
- **Heart rate from large squares:** 300 divided by the number of large squares between two consecutive R waves (300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50 sequence)
- **Heart rate from small squares:** 1500 divided by the number of small squares between two consecutive R waves — more accurate, and the method to use in irregular rhythms by averaging several cycles

Normal values worth memorising cold: **PR interval 0.12–0.20 s** (3–5 small squares), **QRS duration under 0.12 s** (under 3 small squares), and **QT interval under 0.44 s** roughly, corrected for rate as QTc. The normal **cardiac axis** lies between −30° and +90°; lead I and lead aVF both positive means a normal axis, and you can sort most axis questions with just those two leads.

## The ECG basics FMGE keeps asking, in one reading order

Use the same five steps on every single ECG, in the exam and in practice, until it is automatic.

- **Rate** — calculate it by the 300 method; below 60 is bradycardia, above 100 is tachycardia
- **Rhythm** — is there a P wave before every QRS, is the rhythm regular, and are the P waves upright in lead II? Sinus rhythm needs a P before each QRS with a constant PR interval
- **Axis** — check leads I and aVF; left axis deviation suggests left ventricular hypertrophy or left anterior hemiblock, right axis deviation suggests right ventricular hypertrophy or pulmonary disease
- **Intervals** — PR (heart block), QRS width (bundle branch block or ventricular rhythm), QT (drugs, electrolytes, congenital long QT)
- **Waves and segments** — P wave shape, QRS morphology, ST segment, T wave and any U wave, lead by lead

Common wave facts the paper tests directly: a **tall peaked P wave (P pulmonale)** points to right atrial enlargement, a **wide notched P wave (P mitrale)** to left atrial enlargement. A **pathological Q wave** is wider than 0.04 s or deeper than one-third of the following R wave and suggests established infarction. **Left ventricular hypertrophy** by Sokolow-Lyon criteria needs the S wave in V1 plus the R wave in V5 or V6 to exceed 35 mm. In **right bundle branch block** look for an rSR' pattern — the classic "rabbit ears" — in V1 with a wide QRS; in **left bundle branch block** the QRS is wide with a broad, notched R in V6 and no normal septal q wave.

## Myocardial infarction: localisation is the favourite question

The single most repeated ECG task in FMGE is being shown ST elevation and asked which wall, and therefore which artery, is involved. Learn this table properly:

- **Inferior wall — leads II, III and aVF — right coronary artery** in most people; expect reciprocal ST depression in I and aVL, and be alert to bradycardia and heart block because the RCA usually supplies the AV node
- **Anterior wall — leads V3 and V4 — left anterior descending artery**
- **Septal — leads V1 and V2 — left anterior descending artery**
- **Lateral wall — leads I, aVL, V5 and V6 — left circumflex artery**
- **Anterolateral — I, aVL and V3–V6 — LAD or its diagonal branch / circumflex territory**
- **Posterior wall — tall R wave and ST depression in V1–V2** (the mirror image of infarction), confirmed with posterior leads V7–V9; usually the RCA or circumflex
- **Right ventricular infarction** — ST elevation in the right-sided lead V4R, classically with inferior MI; a clinical trap because these patients are preload-dependent

Evolution matters too: hyperacute T waves come first, then **ST elevation**, then pathological Q waves, then T-wave inversion as the ST settles. A **new left bundle branch block** with a compatible history is treated as an infarction equivalent in standard teaching. Contrast this with **pericarditis**, where ST elevation is diffuse and concave (saddle-shaped) across many leads with PR depression, and there are no reciprocal changes — a distinction the paper loves. Early repolarisation is the other benign mimic, common in young people, with concave ST elevation most obvious in the precordial leads.

Practise localisation until it is instant by working through tracing-based questions in the [question bank](/qbank/) — reading about MI patterns and spotting them under time pressure are two different skills.

## Heart blocks and the arrhythmias that actually get asked

Blocks are classified by where conduction fails, and the ECG differences are crisp enough to make these very fair questions:

- **First-degree AV block** — PR interval longer than 0.20 s, but every P wave is still conducted; no dropped beats
- **Second-degree block, Mobitz type I (Wenckebach)** — the PR interval lengthens progressively until a QRS is dropped, then the cycle repeats; usually at the AV node and relatively benign
- **Second-degree block, Mobitz type II** — constant PR interval with sudden, unpredictable dropped QRS complexes; usually below the AV node, often with a wide QRS, and more likely to progress to complete block
- **Third-degree (complete) heart block** — atria and ventricles beat independently; P waves march through at their own rate with no relation to the slower, regular ventricular escape rhythm
- **Atrial fibrillation** — the other great favourite: **irregularly irregular** rhythm, absent P waves, a wavy fibrillatory baseline; causes tested include hyperthyroidism, rheumatic mitral stenosis, hypertension and alcohol
- **Atrial flutter** — **saw-tooth flutter waves**, best seen in II, III and aVF, with an atrial rate around 300/min and often 2:1 conduction giving a ventricular rate near 150
- **Supraventricular tachycardia** — regular, narrow-complex tachycardia with hidden P waves; **ventricular tachycardia** is the wide-complex counterpart and is assumed in any broad-complex tachycardia with structural heart disease

## Electrolyte and drug changes: small section, easy marks

Electrolyte ECGs are short, stereotyped and asked again and again, so learn them as picture pairs:

- **Hyperkalaemia** — **tall, peaked, narrow-based T waves** first, then flattened or lost P waves, a widened QRS, and finally a sine-wave pattern; this is the sequence to quote
- **Hypokalaemia** — flattened T waves, **prominent U waves** and apparent QT prolongation (often really a long QU interval), with ST depression
- **Hypercalcaemia** — **short QT interval** is the headline finding
- **Hypocalcaemia** — **prolonged QT interval** due to a lengthened ST segment, with the T wave itself of normal width
- **Hypomagnesaemia** — prolongs the QT and predisposes to torsades de pointes, the twisting polymorphic ventricular tachycardia that is also associated with congenital long QT and many drugs
- **Digoxin effect** — downsloping, "reverse tick" ST depression with a shortened QT; remember this is effect, not toxicity, though digoxin toxicity can cause almost any arrhythmia, classically with yellow-tinted vision in the stem

Q: What is the fastest way to calculate heart rate on an ECG for FMGE?
A: Divide 300 by the number of large squares between two consecutive R waves. For irregular rhythms, divide 1500 by the number of small squares, averaged over several cycles.
Q: Which leads show an inferior wall MI and which artery is involved?
A: Inferior MI shows ST elevation in leads II, III and aVF, and the culprit is usually the right coronary artery, which also often supplies the AV node — hence the association with heart block.
Q: How do I differentiate Mobitz type I from Mobitz type II block?
A: In Mobitz I (Wenckebach) the PR interval lengthens progressively before a dropped beat. In Mobitz II the PR interval stays constant and QRS complexes drop suddenly, usually with a wide QRS.
Q: What are the ECG changes of hyperkalaemia?
A: Tall peaked T waves appear first, followed by flattening or loss of P waves, widening of the QRS complex, and finally a sine-wave pattern in severe cases.
