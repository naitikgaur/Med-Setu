Medicine is the centre of gravity of the FMGE paper. **Medicine for FMGE** carries **33 questions** — the largest share of any single subject — and its concepts spill into surgery, paediatrics, emergency questions and even pharmacology stems. It is also the subject students most often study badly: they open a giant textbook, read cardiology for three weeks, panic at the remaining systems, and end up with deep knowledge of one system and fragments of the rest. Medicine cannot be "finished" the way forensic medicine can; it has to be *organised*. This guide gives you the organisation — system-wise priorities, the question style to prepare for, the books that fit FMGE's depth, and a revision method for a subject you will revise in layers, not once.

## Medicine for FMGE: weightage and question style

FMGE is conducted by **NBEMS**: 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, **no negative marking**, pass mark **150 out of 300**. Medicine contributes 33 questions, and psychiatry (5), dermatology (5) and radiotherapy (5) are counted separately in the blueprint, which makes the medicine cluster even larger in practice. Question style:

- **Clinical vignettes** — a short case followed by the most likely diagnosis, the next investigation, or the best initial management step
- **"Investigation of choice" and "gold standard" questions** — a very common pattern across systems
- **ECG, imaging and lab-pattern questions** — recognising MI patterns, electrolyte derangements, ABG interpretation, classic X-ray and CT findings
- **Emergency medicine** — recognition and first steps in MI, stroke, DKA, status epilepticus, anaphylaxis, poisoning presentations

Notice: FMGE medicine tests *clinical decision points*, not textbook prose. Your preparation must be built around presentations and decisions.

## High-yield systems, in priority order

- **Cardiology** — coronary artery disease and MI, heart failure, valvular lesions and their murmurs, arrhythmias and ECG interpretation, infective endocarditis
- **Neurology** — stroke localisation and management, epilepsy, meningitis, Parkinson's, peripheral neuropathy, myasthenia gravis, dementia differentials
- **Respiratory medicine** — COPD vs asthma, pneumonia, tuberculosis, pleural effusion, ABG and respiratory failure
- **Endocrinology** — diabetes and its complications (DKA is a favourite), thyroid disorders, adrenal disorders, pituitary basics, calcium disorders
- **Nephrology** — AKI vs CKD, glomerular diseases (with pathology), electrolyte and acid–base disorders, dialysis basics
- **Gastroenterology and hepatology** — hepatitis, cirrhosis and portal hypertension, IBD, pancreatitis, GI bleeding approach
- **Haematology and infections** — anaemia approach, bleeding disorders, malaria, dengue, enteric fever, HIV essentials — overlapping with pathology and microbiology

If your time collapses, protect cardiology, neurology, respiratory and endocrine first — but plan from the start to cover all systems at least at "presentation + key investigation + first-line management" depth.

## Books at the right depth

- **Davidson's Principles and Practice of Medicine** — the best fit for FMGE depth: clinically organised, readable, and strong on presentations and management logic
- **Harrison's Principles of Internal Medicine** — the reference standard; use it for depth on systems you are weak in, not as a cover-to-cover read in a limited timeline
- **Kumar & Clark's Clinical Medicine** — a solid alternative core text if that is what you trained with
- **A good ECG and imaging source** — even a short, focused ECG primer pays for itself across cardiology and emergency questions

Consistency matters more than the label: one core text, read system-wise, with your own notes on decision points.

## The system-wise clinical method

- **Study by presentation, not only by disease** — chest pain, breathlessness, altered sensorium, fever, jaundice, oedema: for each, build the differential list and the discriminating features. Vignettes start from presentations
- **For every disease, fix a five-point grid** — classic presentation, investigation of choice, gold standard, first-line management, most-tested complication. If you can fill the grid, you can answer most FMGE framings of that disease
- **Learn ECG and ABG as skills** — set aside dedicated practice sessions; these are mechanical, learnable, and repeatedly tested
- **Integrate as you go** — when you study heart failure, revise its pharmacology the same day; when you study glomerulonephritis, revise its pathology. Integration is what converts five subjects into one understanding
- **Questions from week one** — medicine improves fastest by solving vignettes. Work system-wise sets from a [question bank](/qbank/) after each system, and study the explanations of your wrong answers properly — that is where the teaching is

## Revision plan

- **First pass:** system-wise reading with five-point grids; question set after every system; ECG/ABG skill sessions weekly
- **Second pass:** grids and wrong questions only; timed medicine tests from the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) to build speed on long vignettes
- **Final phase:** mixed clinical revision (medicine + surgery + paediatrics together), and full papers from the [grand test series](/tests/) — stamina for 300 questions is itself a trainable skill

## Common mistakes that cost medicine marks

- **Reading one system for a month** — depth in cardiology cannot compensate for untouched neurology. Medicine must be covered breadth-first at working depth, then deepened in layers; set a hard time budget per system and obey it
- **Studying diseases without their investigations** — students often know a disease's story but not its test of choice or gold standard, which is exactly the step the question asks about. The five-point grid exists to prevent this specific gap
- **Avoiding ECGs and ABGs** — these look technical and get postponed indefinitely, yet they are closed, learnable skills with repeated questions attached. Two focused weeks of practice closes them permanently
- **Only reading, never deciding** — medicine questions demand a decision under time pressure. If your preparation contains no timed vignette practice, the exam hall is the first place you attempt the actual skill being tested
