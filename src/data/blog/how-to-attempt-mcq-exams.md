Students preparing for the FMGE spend months learning medicine and almost no time learning how to attempt mcq exams — yet technique is where prepared students either collect the marks their knowledge deserves or leak them. Two candidates with identical knowledge can finish thirty marks apart because one had a method and the other had a hope. This guide covers the method: reading questions properly, **elimination**, the **two-pass system**, educated guessing in a no-negative-marking exam, and a simple way to budget your time across a part.

## How to Attempt MCQ Exams With a Method, Not a Mood

A method matters because the exam hall degrades improvisation. Under time pressure, an unstructured candidate reads every question the same way, lingers on hard ones out of pride, changes answers on vibes, and discovers the clock with forty questions left. A structured candidate runs a routine that was decided in advance, calmly, at a desk — and the routine holds when nerves rise.

The FMGE format rewards method particularly well: **300 MCQs, two parts of 150, +1 per correct answer, no negative marking, pass mark 150**, conducted by NBEMS as a computer-based test. No negative marking changes everything about guessing, as we will see. But first, the reading itself.

## Read the Question Like It Is Trying to Trick You — Because Sometimes It Is

Most silly mistakes are reading mistakes, not knowledge mistakes:

- **Read the stem fully before the options.** Cover the options if you can; form your own expected answer, then match it. Options are designed to seduce the half-read mind.
- **Hunt the negations and qualifiers.** "EXCEPT", "NOT", "least likely", "first step", "most common" — one missed word inverts the question. Slow down for exactly these words.
- **Use every clue in the stem.** Age, sex, duration, and one odd detail are rarely decoration; case-based questions usually include the discriminating fact on purpose.
- **Decide what the question is really asking** before debating options: diagnosis, investigation, or management are different questions wearing the same case.

## Elimination: Your Main Weapon on Hard Questions

On questions you cannot answer directly, **elimination** converts a guess into a probability. Work through the options and strike the ones that are definitely wrong — factually impossible, wrong age group, wrong system, internally inconsistent. Each genuine elimination raises the value of your eventual choice: from one-in-four blind, to one-in-three, one-in-two, or occasionally to the answer itself, which was hiding behind two absurd distractors.

Practical elimination habits:

- Eliminate in a fixed order and actually mark the strikes, so a return visit does not restart the debate.
- Beware absolute words — "always", "never", "only" — options containing them are often (not always) the false ones in clinical questions.
- If two options are near-identical, the answer is often one of them; if two options are opposites, likewise — the examiner is testing that exact distinction.
- When torn between two survivors, compare each against the stem's most specific detail. The option that explains *more* of the case is usually right.

## The Two-Pass Method and Honest Guessing

Divide each part into two passes. **Pass one:** move steadily, answer everything you are confident about, and for the rest, eliminate what you can, make your best current choice, and **mark the question for review** if your interface allows. Do not marry a hard question in pass one — its marks are worth exactly the same as the easy question forty places later. **Pass two:** return to the marked questions with your remaining time, calmer, and sometimes with memory jogged by later questions.

And the guessing rule, stated plainly: in the FMGE there is **no negative marking**, so a blank answer is a guaranteed zero while a guess is a chance at +1. **Never submit with unanswered questions.** Guess intelligently — eliminate first, then choose — but if time runs out, fill every remaining bubble with your best instinct. In the final minute, even uneliminated guesses are worth more than blanks.

## Budget Your Time With Simple Arithmetic

Each part has 150 questions. Whatever the part's total time on your screen, divide it by 150 and you get your average budget per question — in practice this works out to roughly **a minute a question** as a pacing guide. You will not spend a minute on each; easy ones take twenty seconds and bank time for the hard ones. The point of the average is a checkpoint habit: at regular intervals, compare questions completed against time elapsed. If you are behind, the fix is not panic — it is discipline: shorten pass-one debates, mark, and move.

A few pacing protections:

- Set a hard personal limit — if a question has consumed about twice your average budget and you are no closer, mark it and go.
- Keep the last few minutes sacred for pass two and for filling blanks, not for starting fresh battles.
- Practise the arithmetic in mocks until pacing is automatic; the [grand test series](/tests/) format, with the same two-part structure, is built for exactly this rehearsal.

## Practise Technique Where It Counts: In Mocks

Technique cannot be read into existence; it is built by repetition under realistic conditions. Sit full papers from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) with a timer, run two passes, force guesses instead of blanks, and afterwards audit not just *what* you got wrong but *how*: misread stem, failed elimination, time sink, or genuine knowledge gap. Each class of error has a different fix, and your marks rise fastest when you fix the class, not just the question.

Do this across several mocks and the method becomes reflex. On exam day you will not be hoping for a good performance — you will be executing a routine you have already performed many times.

Q: What is the two-pass method in MCQ exams?
A: In pass one, answer all questions you are confident about and mark the doubtful ones after eliminating what you can. In pass two, return to the marked questions with your remaining time. It prevents hard questions from stealing time owed to easy ones.
Q: Should I guess in the FMGE if I am unsure?
A: Yes. The FMGE has no negative marking, so a blank is a guaranteed zero and an educated guess can only help. Eliminate the options you know are wrong first, then choose between the survivors.
Q: How much time should I spend on each MCQ?
A: Divide the part's total time by its 150 questions — roughly a minute per question as a pacing average. Easy questions should take less, banking time for hard ones, and checkpoints during the part keep you honest.
Q: How can I reduce silly mistakes in MCQs?
A: Read the full stem before the options, watch for words like EXCEPT and NOT, form your own answer before matching, and audit your mock errors by type — misreading, weak elimination, time sinks, or knowledge gaps — so each gets its own fix.
