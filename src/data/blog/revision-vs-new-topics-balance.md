Every FMGE aspirant reaches the same fork in the final months: the syllabus still has untouched corners, but the topics you studied in month one are quietly evaporating. Getting the **revision vs new topics balance** right in this phase is worth more marks than any single subject, because the arithmetic is unforgiving — a topic studied once and never revised is, by exam day, barely better than a topic never studied, while a *new* topic added in the final weeks has almost no time to be revised at all. This guide gives you a phase-wise rule for the balance, and the reasoning behind it, so you can adapt it to wherever your preparation actually stands.

## Revision vs New Topics Balance: The Principle Underneath

Forgetting is not a character flaw; it is the default behaviour of memory. Material survives when it is **retrieved repeatedly at intervals** — which is precisely what revision is. New topics, meanwhile, have a hidden price tag: each one needs not just its first study, but two or three revisions before it is exam-safe. In the early months, time is plentiful and that price is affordable. In the final months, the calendar is the scarcest resource you own, and the same purchase is made with money borrowed from the revision of everything else.

So the balance shifts across the preparation like this:

- **Early phase (more than 4 months out):** roughly 70% new topics, 30% revision. Build coverage; revise weekly so nothing fully decays.
- **Middle phase (2–4 months out):** about 50/50. New coverage continues, but every completed subject enters a fixed revision rotation.
- **Final phase (last 6–8 weeks):** roughly 80% revision, 20% new — and that 20% is not random newness, as the next section explains.

## Which New Topics Still Deserve a Place in the Final Months

"Stop all new topics" is too crude — some untouched areas are high-yield and compact, and leaving them is leaving marks. In the final phase, admit a new topic only if it passes all three tests:

- **High weightage.** It belongs to a heavily tested subject or a frequently repeated theme. The NBEMS blueprint is public: Medicine, Surgery, Obstetrics & Gynaecology and Community Medicine carry the largest shares of the 300 questions, so gaps there cost more than gaps in five-mark subjects.
- **Compact.** It can be learned *and* revised at least twice in the time available. A two-hour topic qualifies; a two-week subject does not.
- **Connected.** It strengthens topics you already know — a drug class that completes a Pharmacology table, a syndrome that ties two Medicine chapters together. Connected knowledge sticks faster because it has hooks to hang on.

Anything failing a test goes on the honest sacrifice list. Writing that list is not defeat; every topper has one. The defeat is pretending you will cover everything and revising nothing properly.

## What Revision Should Actually Look Like

Revision in the final months is not re-reading your notes cover to cover — there is no time, and passive reading is the weakest form of review. Effective final-phase revision is **retrieval-led**:

- **Questions first.** Run topic sets from the [question bank](/qbank/) and let your errors choose what to re-read. Twenty questions will find your weak spots faster than two hours of hopeful highlighting.
- **Error notebook review.** The mistakes you have logged across months of mocks are a personalised syllabus of exactly what you get wrong. In the final weeks, this is the highest-value reading material you own.
- **Full mocks as revision audits.** A weekly paper from the [grand test series](/tests/) does triple duty: stamina, technique, and a fresh map of which subjects are leaking marks *now*, so next week's revision aims at live leaks rather than old fears.
- **Spaced micro-revision.** Short, frequent passes over tables, classifications and one-liners — the factual crust of subjects like Pharmacology, Microbiology and Community Medicine — beat one heroic weekend cram that fades by the next.

One practical scheduling pattern holds the balance without daily decision-making: **theme your days**. Give each day a lead subject for new or weak-area work in the morning blocks, when you are freshest, and reserve the later blocks for revision rotation — two previously studied subjects, question-led, every day. Across a week this naturally produces the final-phase ratio: mornings push the frontier slightly forward where it still matters, afternoons keep the whole territory behind you from going dark. It also removes the daily negotiation about what to study, which is itself a quiet drain in the high-pressure final weeks.

## A Simple Rule for the Last Eight Weeks

If you want one rule to tape above the desk: **no new topic after the final month begins, unless it passes all three tests above; every topic you keep must be touched at least three times before exam day — once learning, twice retrieving.** Count touches, not hours. A subject with three honest touches is safer than one with thirty hours spent in a single month and never revisited.

And when anxiety whispers that everyone else is covering more, remember what the exam actually samples: 300 questions drawn from the whole syllabus, pass mark 150. A well-revised 75% of the syllabus, retrieved confidently, clears that bar with room to spare. A thinly-spread 100%, half of it evaporated, often does not. Depth you can recall beats breadth you once saw.

Build your final phase around measurement: alternate revision blocks in the [question bank](/qbank/) with full papers from the [previous-year papers](/papers/), and let the error patterns — not panic — set each week's ratio. Q: When should I stop studying new topics before FMGE?
A: As a working rule, the final month should be almost entirely revision, and in the last six to eight weeks new topics should be admitted only if they are high-weightage, compact enough to revise twice, and connected to what you already know. Everything else goes on a deliberate sacrifice list.
Q: How many times should I revise each topic?
A: Aim for at least three touches per topic before exam day: the initial study plus two retrieval-based revisions spaced over time. In the final phase, revision should be question-led — topic sets and your error notebook — rather than passive re-reading.
Q: Is it better to cover the whole syllabus once or most of it thoroughly?
A: For a 150-out-of-300 pass mark, a thoroughly revised majority of the syllabus usually outperforms thin, unrevised full coverage, because unrevised material fades sharply by exam day. Weight your effort toward the high-weightage subjects in the NBEMS blueprint.
Q: How do I decide which new topic is worth adding late?
A: Apply three tests: is it high-weightage, is it compact enough to learn and revise twice in the time left, and does it connect to topics you already know? A topic failing any test is a poor final-month purchase.
