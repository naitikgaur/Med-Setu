Physiology is the subject that quietly decides how easy the rest of your FMGE preparation feels. **Physiology for FMGE** carries **17 questions** in the paper, but its real value is larger: cardiovascular, respiratory, renal and endocrine concepts reappear inside medicine, pharmacology and anaesthesia questions. The good news is that physiology rewards understanding over memory. If you genuinely understand a mechanism, you can derive the answer in the exam hall even when the question is framed in an unfamiliar way. This guide covers the weightage, the systems the paper favours, the right books, and a study method that builds concepts instead of notes you will forget.

## Physiology for FMGE: weightage and question style

The FMGE, conducted by **NBEMS**, has 300 MCQs split into two parts of 150, with **no negative marking** and a pass mark of **150 out of 300**. Physiology contributes 17 questions. Their style is distinctive:

- **Mechanism-based questions** — "what happens to X when Y changes" formats covering pressures, flows, reflexes and feedback loops
- **Graph and curve questions** — cardiac cycle, pressure–volume loops, oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve, spirometry tracings
- **Clinical physiology** — physiological basis of shock, heart failure, respiratory failure, acid–base disorders
- **Numerical and unit-based one-liners** — normal values, clearance concepts, lung volumes and capacities

Because questions test relationships between variables, last-minute mugging of physiology facts has a poor return. Concepts need a proper first pass.

## High-yield systems to prioritise

If time is short, these systems give the maximum marks per hour of study:

- **Cardiovascular physiology** — cardiac cycle, pressure–volume loop, cardiac output regulation, coronary circulation, ECG basics, haemodynamics and shock
- **Respiratory physiology** — mechanics of breathing, lung volumes, V/Q ratio, oxygen and carbon dioxide transport, hypoxia types, control of respiration
- **Renal physiology** — glomerular filtration, tubular handling of sodium and water, clearance, counter-current mechanism, acid–base balance
- **Endocrine physiology** — insulin and diabetes physiology, thyroid, adrenal cortex and medulla, calcium homeostasis, pituitary hormones
- **Neurophysiology** — membrane and action potentials, reflexes, sensory pathways, basal ganglia and cerebellum functions, sleep and EEG
- **Blood and GI physiology** — coagulation basics, haemoglobin, gastric and pancreatic secretions, GI motility and hormones

Notice how much of this list is also core medicine. An hour spent on renal physiology is simultaneously an hour spent on nephrology.

## Books that build concepts

- **Ganong's Review of Medical Physiology** — the classic concept text; dense but superb for mechanisms and clinical correlations
- **Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology** — unmatched for cardiovascular, respiratory and renal physiology explained from first principles; use it for the systems you find hard rather than cover to cover
- **Understanding Medical Physiology (Bijlani)** or your undergraduate notes — useful for a faster, exam-oriented pass once concepts are clear

Whichever text you choose, read actively: after each mechanism, close the book and explain it aloud in your own words. If you cannot explain it, you have not learned it — you have only seen it.

## A study method that makes physiology stick

- **Learn systems, not chapters** — take one system at a time and finish its physiology completely before moving on, because concepts inside a system interlock
- **Draw every curve yourself** — the oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve with its shift factors, the cardiac cycle with valve events, the pressure–volume loop in failure and stenosis: drawing converts passive reading into recall
- **Make cause–effect chains** — write sequences like "↓ venous return → ↓ stroke volume → ↓ cardiac output → baroreceptor response → …"; FMGE questions are built from exactly these chains
- **Anchor normal values** — a small, fixed list of values (pressures, volumes, clearances, hormone ranges) revised weekly until automatic
- **Solve questions early** — topic-wise practice from a [question bank](/qbank/) after each system shows you how examiners twist a concept, which is where most students lose marks they "knew"

Physiology also strengthens your pharmacology (drug actions are applied physiology) and your medicine, so front-load it in your timetable rather than leaving it for the end.

## Revision plan

- **First pass:** system-wise reading with self-drawn curves and cause–effect chains; topic-wise questions after each system
- **Second pass:** revise only your chains, curves and wrong questions; take a timed physiology test from the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/)
- **Final phase:** mixed revision with medicine and pharmacology, plus full mocks from the [grand test series](/tests/) to practise physiology under whole-paper pressure

Keep your physiology notes lean. In the last month, your curves and chains — not the textbook — are what you will revise from.

## Common mistakes that cost physiology marks

- **Highlighting instead of explaining** — physiology is only learned when you can state the mechanism in your own words; if your revision is re-reading highlighted lines, your exam performance will expose it
- **Skipping graphs because they look hard** — curves and loops are among the most repeated question formats in this subject; an afternoon spent drawing the pressure–volume loop and the dissociation curve until they are automatic is one of the best investments in the whole preclinical block
- **Studying physiology after the clinical subjects** — physiology is the language medicine and pharmacology are written in; learning it late means re-learning it inside every clinical chapter anyway
- **Ignoring the "what happens if" format** — examiners rarely ask what a value *is*; they ask what it *becomes* when something changes. Practise predicting the direction of change for every variable in every system

Q: How many physiology questions are there in FMGE?

Q: Ganong or Guyton — which is better for FMGE physiology?
A: Both are standard. Ganong is compact and concept-dense; Guyton explains cardiovascular, respiratory and renal physiology most clearly. Use one as your main text and the other for weak systems.
Q: Which physiology topics are most important for FMGE?
A: Cardiovascular, respiratory, renal and endocrine physiology are the most heavily emphasised, along with neurophysiology basics and acid–base balance.
Q: Why do I lose physiology marks even after studying?
A: Usually because questions test relationships between variables, not facts. Practising mechanism-based questions topic-wise trains exactly that skill.
