Good **clinical examination basics** turn a vague history into physical evidence. Examination is not a random collection of manoeuvres; it is a disciplined sequence performed the same way every time, so that signs are neither missed nor invented. Whether you are examining the chest of a breathless patient or presenting a case in a final-year viva, the examiner is really asking one question: does this student have a system? This guide gives you that system — preparation, general examination, then the four classic techniques applied system by system.

## Clinical Examination Basics: Prepare Before You Touch

More marks are lost before the examination starts than during it. Follow this routine every single time:

- **Wash or sanitise your hands** in front of the patient or examiner — visibly.
- **Introduce yourself**, confirm the patient's identity, and explain what you are about to do.
- **Take consent** — verbal consent is enough for routine examination, and it must be genuine, not a mumble while you reach for the patient.
- **Ensure privacy and dignity:** curtains drawn, only the area being examined exposed, and a **chaperone** offered for intimate examinations.
- **Position and expose properly:** most systemic examinations are done at 45 degrees for the chest and flat for the abdomen, with adequate light.

Then stand back. The first and most underused examination skill is simply looking at the patient from the end of the bed: Are they comfortable or distressed? Breathing easily or struggling? Connected to oxygen, drips, catheters or monitors? That ten-second survey often tells you the severity before any number does.

## The General Examination

Before any system, complete the **general examination**. Work through it in a fixed order:

- **Consciousness and comfort:** alert, drowsy, in pain, breathless at rest?
- **Build and nutrition:** well nourished, wasted, obese?
- **Pallor, icterus, cyanosis, clubbing, oedema and lymphadenopathy** — the classic six signs checked at the face, hands, neck and legs.
- **Vital signs:** pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature and oxygen saturation.
- **Hands and nails:** clubbing, splinter haemorrhages, tremor, palmar erythema, capillary refill.
- **Face, eyes, mouth and neck:** conjunctival pallor, scleral icterus, hydration, dentition, jugular venous pressure and neck nodes.

Present these findings first in any case. A student who reports "the patient is comfortable, afebrile, pulse 84 regular, no pallor or clubbing" has already sounded competent before reaching the main system. ## The Four Techniques, in Order

Every systemic examination follows the same four steps, and the order matters because later steps disturb what earlier steps would have shown.

**1. Inspection.** Look before you touch: shape, symmetry, movement, scars, swelling, discoloration, visible pulsations and respiratory pattern. Compare the two sides. Inspection alone can reveal the flail segment, the distended abdomen or the wasted limb.

**2. Palpation.** Feel with warm hands, gently first and deeper later, watching the patient's face. Assess tenderness, temperature, masses (site, size, surface, consistency, mobility), organ edges, pulses and thrills. Always ask about pain and examine the painful area **last**.

**3. Percussion.** Strike the middle finger of one hand with the middle finger of the other to generate a note: resonant over air-filled lung, dull over solid organs, stony dull over fluid, hyper-resonant over a pneumothorax, tympanitic over a gas-filled bowel. Percussion maps borders — of the liver, the heart and areas of consolidation — that palpation cannot.

**4. Auscultation.** Listen with the stethoscope: the diaphragm for high-pitched sounds such as normal breath and bowel sounds, the bell for low-pitched sounds such as the murmur of mitral stenosis. Listen in quiet, compare symmetrical points, and describe what you hear in standard words — vesicular or bronchial breathing, added sounds such as crackles or wheeze, murmur timing and radiation.

The one famous exception is the abdomen, where **auscultation comes before palpation and percussion**, because touching the abdomen first alters bowel sounds. State this exception confidently; examiners love it.

## Applying the Sequence System by System

For the **respiratory system**: inspect chest shape and movement, palpate trachea and expansion, percuss all zones comparing sides, auscultate for breath sounds and added sounds, and finish with vocal resonance if indicated. For the **cardiovascular system**: inspect, palpate the apex beat and thrills, then auscultate all valve areas with pulse timing — percussion has little role here, and saying so is correct. For the **abdomen**: inspect, auscultate bowel sounds, then palpate lightly and deeply, percuss for liver span and shifting dullness, and complete with hernial orifices and external genitalia only where appropriate and consented.

Whatever the system, examine the normal side first where possible, compare constantly, and never announce a sign you have not genuinely found. If you are unsure, say what you found and what it could mean — honesty about uncertainty is a clinical skill, not a weakness.

## Finishing Well

Thank the patient, help them dress or cover up, and wash your hands again. Then, before presenting, state what else you would do: complete the other systems, check the charts and observations, and order targeted investigations. A complete examiner also says "I would like to examine the other systems and review the temperature chart" — it signals that you see a patient, not a part.

Examination skill grows by examining many normal patients first; you cannot recognise abnormal until normal is boring. Q: What is the correct order of clinical examination?
A: Inspection, palpation, percussion, then auscultation. The abdomen is the exception: auscultation is done before palpation and percussion so bowel sounds are not altered by handling.
Q: What is included in the general examination?
A: Consciousness and comfort, build and nutrition, pallor, icterus, cyanosis, clubbing, oedema and lymphadenopathy, plus vital signs and a check of the hands, face, mouth and neck.
Q: Why is a chaperone important?
A: A chaperone protects both patient and examiner during intimate examinations, supports the patient's dignity and consent, and is standard professional practice.
Q: Which part of the stethoscope is used for low-pitched sounds?
A: The bell, used with light pressure — for example for the low-pitched diastolic murmur of mitral stenosis. The diaphragm is used for high-pitched sounds.
