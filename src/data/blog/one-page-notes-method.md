The **one page notes method** is simple to describe and surprisingly hard to do well: take one topic — heart failure, tuberculosis, the brachial plexus — and fit everything that matters about it onto a single page. Not a summary copied from a textbook, but a page you have *built* by deciding what earns space and what does not. That act of deciding is the whole point. When you compress, you are forced to understand the topic's structure, and a structured topic is far easier to recall in an exam hall than thirty pages of highlighted text.

## Why One Page Works Better Than Thirty

Most students revise by re-reading. Re-reading feels productive because the material looks familiar, but familiarity is not recall — and exams test recall. The one-page constraint attacks this problem in three ways.

- **It forces selection.** You cannot fit everything, so you must rank facts by yield: definitions, classifications, differentiating features, first-line treatments, classic presentations and favourite exam traps.
- **It forces organisation.** A page has no room for a rambling paragraph, so information naturally becomes tables, flowcharts, arrows and grouped lists — the formats memory handles best.
- **It makes revision fast.** In the final fortnight before an exam like FMGE, you may revise a subject three or four times. Thirty pages per topic makes that impossible; one page per topic makes it routine.

There is also an honest limitation: one-page notes are a *revision* tool, not a learning tool. Build them after you have studied a topic properly once. A page made from material you never understood is just a smaller thing you do not understand.

## The One Page Notes Method in Practice

Follow the same sequence for every topic and your pages will stay consistent, which matters when you are flipping through fifty of them under time pressure.

- **Start from questions, not the textbook.** Do a set of questions on the topic first — from a [question bank](/qbank/) or your notes — and note what is actually asked. Exam-setters return to the same angles repeatedly.
- **Draft the skeleton.** Write the topic's frame first: definition, causes or classification, clinical features, investigations, management. Five boxes, every time.
- **Fill with high-yield facts only.** Prototype examples, one-line differentiators ("painless vs painful", "upper vs lower lobe"), drug of choice, investigation of choice, most common cause, most common site — these phrases are the currency of MCQs.
- **Add a "traps" corner.** Reserve a small box for exceptions and commonly confused pairs: the atypical presentation, the contraindication everyone forgets, the one condition that breaks the rule.
- **Write it by hand or type it — but rewrite, don't copy.** The value is in reformulating. If a line is lifted word-for-word from a book, you have not processed it.

## What Belongs on the Page (and What Does Not)

A useful test for every line: *could this change which option I pick?* If yes, it belongs. If it is background colour — history of the disease, rare associations, long pathophysiology chains — it does not, however interesting it is.

**Belongs:** classifications with the one feature that separates each type; staging systems; drug doses only where dose questions are actually asked; side effects that are classic or dangerous; named signs; scoring systems; investigation and treatment of choice.

**Does not belong:** full paragraphs, rare syndromes mentioned once in a textbook chapter, anything you already know cold, and anything you have never once seen tested. Be ruthless. A page that is 90% full is better than a page crammed edge to edge in tiny writing — the white space is what lets your eye find things quickly during a fast revision pass.

## How to Use the Pages in Your Revision Cycle

Making the page is only half the method; the other half is how you revisit it. Cover the page and try to reproduce its skeleton from memory — the headings, the boxes, the traps corner. Where your mental copy has gaps, those gaps are exactly what you re-read. This is active recall applied to your own notes, and it pairs naturally with **spaced repetition**: revisit each page after a day, then a few days, then a week, lengthening the gap as recall gets reliable.

Before you file a finished page away, run one quality check: hand it to the only examiner who matters — yourself, tomorrow morning. Cover the page, state the topic aloud, and try to reconstruct its five boxes in order. If you can rebuild the skeleton without peeking, the page is well designed; its structure has become your structure. If you stall, the problem is usually layout, not effort — the page has no clear frame, or the traps corner is buried. Redraw it around the frame rather than adding more facts. A page is also allowed to be wrong, and should be corrected in pen whenever a question exposes an error or a missing exception; a one-page note that evolves across your preparation is worth three static ones, because every correction is a mistake you will not repeat in the hall.

In the last two weeks before the exam, stop making new pages. Switch entirely to cycling through the ones you have, interleaved with timed practice — [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) work well here because they tell you, subject by subject, which pages are not doing their job. If you keep missing questions on one topic, that page gets rewritten, not just re-read.

Q: How many one-page notes should I make for FMGE?
A: One per important topic rather than one per chapter — for most students that means 150 to 250 pages across the 19 subjects, weighted towards Medicine, Surgery, OBG, Pharmacology and Pathology. Quality and repeated use matter far more than the count.
Q: Should one-page notes be handwritten or typed?
A: Either works. Handwriting slows you down, which can help processing, but typed pages are easier to edit and reprint. The non-negotiable part is that you compose them yourself rather than copying or downloading someone else's.
Q: When should I stop making notes and only revise?
A: Roughly two to three weeks before the exam. After that, new pages rarely get the repeated passes that make them useful; your time returns more marks spent recalling existing pages and solving questions.
Q: Can one-page notes replace textbooks completely?
A: No. They are a revision layer built on top of one proper study pass. Use them to revise and to find gaps — then go back to the textbook only for the gaps, not for whole chapters.
