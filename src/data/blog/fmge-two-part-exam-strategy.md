A workable fmge two part strategy starts with a mental shift: stop picturing one enormous 300-question paper. The FMGE, conducted by **NBEMS** as a computer-based test, is **Part A: 150 questions**, then a break, then **Part B: 150 questions** — two separate performances, each worth 150 marks, with +1 per correct answer, **no negative marking**, and a pass mark of 150 out of the combined 300. Students who pace it as two exams finish both parts fresh enough to think. Students who pace it as one marathon usually fade somewhere in the second hundred. Here is how to run each part.

## FMGE Two Part Strategy for Part A and Part B

The strategy has four moving pieces: a per-part pace, a two-pass routine inside each part, a deliberate use of the break, and protection against the classic Part B fade. None of it requires talent — all of it can be rehearsed in mocks until it is automatic. Note also what the structure gives you: because the pass mark is on the *combined* total, a merely decent Part A is not a disaster. A strong Part B recovers it. The two-part format always leaves the exam winnable at the break, which is worth remembering when Part A feels rough.

## Pacing a Part: Checkpoints, Not Vibes

Each part stands alone, so pace it alone. Divide the part's time by its 150 questions and you get your average budget — roughly **a minute per question** as a working guide (this is simple arithmetic from the paper's structure, not a rule handed down by NBEMS). You will spend far less on easy questions and more on hard ones; the average exists to power **checkpoints**. At set intervals — say, every 50 questions — compare your question count with the clock. On pace means questions done roughly matching the fraction of time used. Behind pace means one adjustment only: tighten pass one, mark doubtful questions faster, and stop having long negotiations with single stems.

Two pacing errors to avoid:

- **The early crawl:** polishing the first thirty questions to perfection and bankrupting the last fifty. Marks are uniformly priced; question 140 pays the same as question 4.
- **The late panic:** discovering the clock with forty questions left and answering in a blur. Checkpoints exist precisely to make this impossible — you find out you are behind at question 50, when it is cheap to fix.

## Inside Each Part: The Two-Pass Routine

Run the same routine in both parts. **Pass one:** work steadily through all 150, answering what you know, and on doubtful questions eliminate the clearly wrong options, choose your current best, and **mark for review**. Give a stuck question a firm personal time limit — around twice your average budget — then mark and move; its mark is not worth three other questions' time. **Pass two:** return to marked questions in order, with the time your easy answers banked. You will find some now look obvious, partly because later questions jog memory and partly because you are no longer afraid of the clock.

Close each part with the guessing discipline the format demands: **no negative marking means no blanks**. Before submitting, sweep for unanswered questions and fill every one with your best educated guess. A part submitted with blanks is marks voluntarily surrendered.

## The Break Is a Tool — Use It Like One

The interval between parts is the only rest in a long exam; most students waste it on the one activity guaranteed to harm Part B: **replaying Part A**. Post-mortems in the corridor — yours or other candidates' — cannot change a submitted answer and reliably produce either false confidence or needless dread. Both are poison for the next 150.

Use the break to reset instead:

- Leave the hall area if permitted; change the visual field, stretch, walk.
- Eat something light and drink water — Part B is another full performance, and it will be run on whatever fuel you give it now.
- Do not discuss questions. If someone insists, move away politely.
- Re-enter with a single sentence in mind: "Part B is a fresh 150 marks." Because it literally is.

Students who protect the break consistently report that Part B feels like a new exam. Students who spend it grieving Part A carry 150 old questions into 150 new ones.

## Beating the Part B Fade

Fatigue in Part B is real but manageable. Its main causes are decision fatigue, the Part A replay, and running the second part on an empty stomach and a full bladder. Beyond the break habits above: keep your routine *identical* in Part B — same two passes, same checkpoints, same time limits. Routine is what carries you when freshness fades; improvisation is what fatigue eats first. If concentration dips mid-part, take ten seconds: sit back, breathe slowly with a longer exhale, look away from the screen, then restart with the next question. Small resets beat long stalls.

Also pace your *preparation* for this: if every mock you have ever taken was a single sitting of short sets, Part B will feel foreign no matter how much you know. Practise in the real shape — full 150 + 150 papers with a break — so your stamina is trained, not hoped for. ## Your Two-Part Checklist

- **Before Part A:** routine ready, checkpoints decided, time limit per stuck question set.
- **During each part:** two passes, mark freely, checkpoint every 50 questions, no blanks at submission.
- **At the break:** food, water, movement, zero question discussion, Part A mentally closed.
- **During Part B:** same routine, small resets when focus dips, final sweep for blanks.

Master these, and the two-part structure stops being an endurance threat and becomes what it actually is: two chances to perform, with a rest in between that your competitors are wasting.

Q: Is the FMGE pass mark calculated per part or combined?
A: Combined. The FMGE has 300 MCQs across Part A and Part B (150 each), and the pass mark is 150 out of 300 overall — a weaker Part A can be recovered by a strong Part B.
Q: How should I pace 150 questions in one part?
A: Divide the part's time by 150 for an average budget — roughly a minute per question as a guide — and check your position at regular checkpoints, such as every 50 questions, adjusting pass-one speed early rather than panicking late.
Q: What should I do in the break between Part A and Part B?
A: Reset: eat something light, drink water, move around, and do not discuss or replay Part A questions. Treat Part B as a fresh 150-mark paper.
Q: Should I change my strategy in Part B if Part A went badly?
A: No. Keep the identical two-pass routine and checkpoints. Because the pass mark is combined, a disciplined Part B can still carry you past 150.
