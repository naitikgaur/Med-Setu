Learning **how to read an ECG beginners** fear is mostly about order, not intelligence. Beginners stare at the whole tracing, spot one dramatic thing and miss the quiet finding beside it. Experienced clinicians do the opposite: they run the same checklist on every single ECG, in the same sequence, and let the abnormalities announce themselves. This article gives you that checklist. Work through it mechanically at first — speed and pattern recognition come later, built on hundreds of repetitions.

## First: Check the Paper, the Name and the Calibration

Before interpreting anything, confirm the tracing belongs to the patient in front of you and check the settings. Standard recording is a paper speed of **25 mm per second** and a calibration of **10 mm per millivolt**, shown by the calibration pulse at the start. At standard speed, one small square (1 mm) equals 0.04 seconds and one large square (5 mm) equals 0.2 seconds. If the speed or gain is non-standard — 50 mm/s is sometimes used — every measurement you make will be wrong unless you adjust. Also note the patient's symptoms at the time of recording: an ECG taken during chest pain means far more than the same tracing taken after the pain settled.

## How to Read an ECG Beginners Can Trust: Rate and Rhythm

**Rate.** Use the 300 method for regular rhythms: divide 300 by the number of large squares between two consecutive R waves. Three large squares means 100 beats per minute; five means 60. For irregular rhythms, count the QRS complexes in a long rhythm strip and multiply appropriately — a full 10-second strip multiplied by 6 gives the rate. The normal resting range is **60–100 beats per minute**; below is bradycardia, above is tachycardia.

**Rhythm.** Ask three questions. Is there a P wave before every QRS? Is every P wave followed by a QRS? Is the rhythm regular? If yes to all, with a normal PR interval, you are most likely looking at **sinus rhythm**. Then check the classic irregular patterns: an irregularly irregular rhythm with no clear P waves suggests atrial fibrillation; grouped beating suggests heart block; a sudden early beat suggests an ectopic. Name the rhythm before you move on — every later step depends on it.

Test your rhythm recognition repeatedly in the [question bank](/qbank/) and in cardiology sets within the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/), because rhythm strips are pure pattern practice.

## Axis and Intervals

**Axis.** The quick method uses leads I and aVF. If the QRS is predominantly positive in both, the axis is normal. Positive in I and negative in aVF suggests left axis deviation; negative in I and positive in aVF suggests right axis deviation; negative in both is an extreme axis. The normal axis lies roughly between **−30° and +90°**. Axis shifts support other findings — right axis deviation with right ventricular strain patterns, for instance — but rarely make a diagnosis alone.

**Intervals.** Measure them in the lead where they look longest:

- **PR interval:** 0.12–0.20 seconds (3–5 small squares). Longer means first-degree heart block; shorter suggests pre-excitation.
- **QRS duration:** normally under 0.12 seconds (3 small squares). A wide QRS means the ventricles are not being activated by the normal fast pathway — bundle branch block or a ventricular rhythm.
- **QT interval:** varies with rate and is corrected as QTc; a prolonged QTc (roughly above 450 ms in men and 470 ms in women) raises the risk of dangerous arrhythmias and is affected by drugs and electrolytes.

## P Waves, QRS Complexes and the ST Segment

Now walk the waveform itself. **P waves:** are they upright in lead II, uniform in shape, and normal in size? Tall peaked P waves suggest right atrial enlargement; wide notched P waves suggest left atrial enlargement. **Q waves:** small septal Q waves are normal in lateral leads, but deep or wide Q waves in a territory suggest previous infarction. **QRS height:** very tall complexes can indicate ventricular hypertrophy, judged against standard voltage criteria rather than by eye.

**ST segment and T waves** are where emergencies live. **ST elevation** in a territorial pattern with reciprocal depression is the signature of an acute occlusion heart attack until proved otherwise; **ST depression** and T-wave inversion suggest ischaemia or strain; tall, peaked T waves can be an early sign of both infarction and hyperkalaemia; flattened T waves with U waves suggest hypokalaemia. Always interpret ST changes with the story: the same millimetre of elevation means something different in a pain-free athlete and in a sweating patient with crushing chest pain. That clinical correlation is exactly what examiners test in the [grand test series](/tests/) and in case discussions on the ward.

## A Fixed Final Routine

Finish every ECG the same way:

- Compare with any **previous ECG** — new changes matter far more than old ones.
- Describe before you diagnose: "Sinus rhythm, 84 per minute, normal axis, normal intervals, with ST depression in V4–V6."
- State the **clinical implication** and the action: repeat ECG, cardiac monitoring, bloods including troponin where indicated, and urgent senior review for any worrying pattern.
- If you cannot interpret a tracing fully, say what you can see and escalate. An unrecognised abnormality that is escalated is safe practice; a guessed one is not.

Keep a personal file of interesting ECGs from the wards, cover the reports, and re-read them with this checklist monthly. Q: What is the normal heart rate on an ECG?
A: A normal resting heart rate is 60–100 beats per minute. Below 60 is bradycardia and above 100 is tachycardia, interpreted alongside the patient's condition.
Q: How is heart rate calculated from an ECG?
A: For a regular rhythm, divide 300 by the number of large squares between two R waves. For irregular rhythms, count QRS complexes over a 10-second strip and multiply by 6.
Q: What are the normal PR and QRS durations?
A: The PR interval is normally 0.12–0.20 seconds and the QRS duration is normally under 0.12 seconds. A longer PR suggests first-degree block; a wide QRS suggests bundle branch block or a ventricular origin.
Q: What does ST elevation usually indicate?
A: Territorial ST elevation with reciprocal change is the classic sign of an acute occlusive myocardial infarction and needs urgent senior and specialist assessment.
