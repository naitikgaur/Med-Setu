Ask a room of doctors which study design they trust most for deciding whether a treatment works, and the answer comes back instantly. Getting the **randomised controlled trial explained** properly — not just defined, but understood — is the single most valuable research skill a student can build, because the design's reputation rests on three or four specific mechanisms. Remove any one of them, and a "trial" quietly becomes something much weaker. An RCT, at its core, takes patients who are eligible and willing, assigns them **by chance** to receive either the new intervention or a comparison (existing treatment, placebo, or no added treatment), follows them forward, and compares outcomes between the groups.

## A Randomised Controlled Trial Explained: Why Randomise?

The deepest problem in treatment research is **confounding**: patients who receive a new treatment usually differ from those who do not — in age, severity, motivation, wealth, a hundred ways — and those differences, not the treatment, may explain their outcomes. Comparing treated and untreated patients in ordinary practice therefore compares *different kinds of people*, and no statistical adjustment can fix differences nobody measured.

**Randomisation** solves this elegantly. When allocation is by chance, the groups are alike on average in *everything* — measured and unmeasured, known and unknown — except the treatment. Any difference in outcomes can then be attributed to the intervention with far more confidence. This is why the RCT sits near the top of the evidence hierarchy for treatment questions: it is the only common design that controls for confounders nobody has even thought of.

Two safeguards protect the coin flip itself:

- **Allocation concealment** — the person enrolling patients cannot foresee the next assignment, so they cannot (consciously or not) steer sicker or healthier patients into a preferred group. Randomisation that can be predicted is randomisation that can be gamed.
- **A proper random sequence** — generated by computer or random-number tables, not by alternation, birth dates or record numbers, which are predictable and therefore not truly random.

In practice, randomisation is organised in ways that protect balance. **Block randomisation** allocates in small blocks (say, four or six patients) so the groups stay similar in size throughout recruitment, rather than drifting apart by chance early on. **Stratified randomisation** first divides patients by an important prognostic factor — age group, disease severity, centre — and randomises within each stratum, guaranteeing the groups match on that factor. Neither refinement changes the principle; both exist because "alike on average" is a promise that good trialists help chance to keep, especially in smaller trials where luck alone might leave the groups visibly unbalanced.

## Blinding: Keeping Expectations Out of the Results

Knowing who got the new treatment changes behaviour. Patients may report feeling better (or worse); doctors may treat, investigate or encourage groups differently; assessors may score outcomes generously for the favoured arm. **Blinding** breaks these channels of bias:

- **Single blind** — participants do not know their group
- **Double blind** — neither participants nor the treating/assessing team know
- **Open label** — everyone knows; sometimes unavoidable, as with surgery or exercise interventions

Where blinding is impossible, trials can still blind the **outcome assessors** and use objective outcomes (like mortality) that expectations cannot easily bend. A placebo control is the classic tool that makes participant blinding possible — it isolates the treatment's specific effect from the powerful effects of attention, expectation and ritual. Trial-reporting questions, including blinding and concealment, are staples of community medicine papers; you can practise them in our [question bank](/qbank/).

## Intention-to-Treat: Analyse As Randomised

Real patients misbehave — from the trial's point of view. They stop the drug, cross over to the other treatment, or disappear. **Intention-to-treat (ITT)** analysis keeps every participant in the group they were randomised to, regardless of what treatment they actually received. It sounds wasteful, but it protects the one thing randomisation bought: comparable groups. Analysing only those who complied ("per-protocol") reintroduces selection bias, because compliers differ systematically from non-compliers — they are usually healthier and more conscientious in *both* arms.

ITT also mirrors real practice, where prescribing a treatment includes the reality that some patients will not take it. Its conservative bias — crossovers dilute the measured effect toward no difference — is the acceptable price for an unbiased comparison.

## The Honest Limits of RCTs

RCTs deserve their crown, but the crown has conditions. They are **expensive and slow**, so they often enrol selected patients — younger, with fewer other diseases — limiting how far results generalise to the frail, elderly, real-world clinic. They are usually **too small and too short** to detect rare or late harms, which is why post-marketing surveillance exists. Some questions **cannot ethically or practically be randomised**: nobody will randomise people to smoke, and rare diseases may never fill a trial. For such questions, well-designed observational studies are not a poor substitute — they are the best evidence available, and the hierarchy must be read with that judgement.

Reading an RCT, then, is a checklist habit: Was allocation truly random and concealed? Who was blinded? Was follow-up complete, and analysis by intention-to-treat? Were the patients like mine? Trials that follow the **CONSORT** reporting standard answer these questions transparently — which is precisely what the standard was created for. The student takeaway: randomisation kills confounding, concealment protects randomisation, blinding blocks expectation bias, and intention-to-treat preserves the comparison. Q: Why is randomisation better than letting doctors choose who gets a new treatment?
A: Because choice introduces confounding — patients given a new treatment differ systematically from those who are not. Randomisation makes the groups alike on average in all factors, measured and unmeasured, so outcome differences can be attributed to the treatment.
Q: What is allocation concealment?
A: It is the protection of the randomisation sequence so that the person enrolling patients cannot predict the next assignment. Without concealment, enrolment can be consciously or unconsciously steered, destroying the balance randomisation was meant to create.
Q: What does intention-to-treat mean?
A: Participants are analysed in the group they were randomised to, regardless of whether they completed or even received that treatment. This preserves the comparability created by randomisation and reflects real-world prescribing.
Q: Can every clinical question be answered by an RCT?
A: No. RCTs may be unethical (randomising people to a harmful exposure), impractical (very rare diseases or very long-term outcomes), or too small to detect rare harms. In those situations, well-designed observational studies provide the best available evidence.
