Anki is a free, open-source flashcard program built around **spaced repetition**: it shows you each card just before you are likely to forget it, and schedules the next review further away each time you remember. Forgetting follows a steep curve in the first days after learning, and Anki's whole job is to interrupt that curve at the right moments. Interest in Anki for medical students has grown because the fit is natural — medicine has an enormous volume of facts that must survive for months, not days. But the app is only a scheduler. Whether it works depends almost entirely on the cards you feed it and whether you keep up with reviews.

## Anki for Medical Students: How the System Works

You build **decks** of flashcards. Each card has a front (a question or prompt) and a back (the answer). When a card is shown, you try to recall the answer, flip the card, and honestly grade how it went. Based on your grade, Anki schedules the card's next appearance — soon if you struggled, much later if it was easy.

Two ideas make this more powerful than shuffling paper cards. First, the schedule is **per card**, so strong material stops consuming your time while weak material keeps returning. Second, reviews accumulate gradually: a deck you feed a little each day produces a daily review load that stays manageable, as long as you do not flood it. That last condition is where most beginners fail, and we will come back to it.

## The Card-Writing Rules That Matter Most

Anki veterans repeat a few rules because they are earned from experience. The most important:

**One fact per card.** This is the golden rule, sometimes called the **minimum information principle**. A card asking "List the causes, features and treatment of heart failure" cannot be graded honestly — you will know half of it and stall on the rest. Split it: one card per cause group, one per key feature, one per drug. Small cards are fast to answer, easy to grade, and Anki can schedule each fact separately.

**Make the front a real question.** A front that reads "Heart failure — treatment" invites a vague mental wave. A front that asks "Which drug class is first-line for reducing mortality in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction?" forces a specific retrieval. Specific question, specific answer.

**Keep the back short.** The back should contain the answer and, at most, one line of context. If the back is a paragraph, you will start skimming instead of recalling.

**Add a cue when a fact floats free.** Isolated facts are hard to retrieve. A short hint on the front — the clinical setting, the system, a contrasting condition — gives memory a handle without giving away the answer.

## Cloze vs Basic Cards: Which to Use

Anki's two workhorse card types are **Basic** (a question on the front, an answer on the back) and **Cloze** (a sentence with a word or phrase blanked out, which you must supply).

- **Basic cards** suit questions with a clear answer: definitions, drug of choice, most likely diagnosis, the next step in management. Writing them takes thought, which is itself study.
- **Cloze cards** are faster to make from notes — take a sentence and blank the key term. They suit lists, pathways and values. Their weakness is that you can sometimes answer from the sentence's shape rather than real knowledge, so mix them with basic cards and questions.

A sane beginner split: cloze for rapid capture while studying a topic, basic cards for the facts that matter most and for anything you keep getting wrong.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

**Downloading a giant deck before learning the material.** A ready-made deck of twenty thousand cards is not a shortcut; flashcards consolidate learning, they do not replace the first proper study of a topic. Make cards from material you have already studied and understood.

**Making a card for every line.** If everything is a card, nothing is prioritised, and your daily reviews balloon into hours. Card the facts that are high-yield, forgettable or personally troublesome — not the whole chapter.

**Skipping review days.** Spaced repetition is a promise: a little every day. Miss a week and the queue returns as a mountain. On busy days, do the reviews and add **no new cards** — that keeps the system alive at minimum cost.

**Grading dishonestly.** Pressing "good" on a card you half-knew feels kind and is sabotage; the card disappears for weeks and the fact is not there in the exam. If you did not produce the answer before flipping, it was a miss.

**Only ever doing cards.** Flashcards hold facts; exams test application. Pair Anki with question practice — work a topic in the [question bank](/qbank/), and let the questions you miss tell you which new cards to write. The bank is often a better card-writing prompt than the textbook, because a missed question is proof of a gap.

## A Simple Way to Start

Pick one subject you are currently studying. Study one topic properly, then write fifteen to twenty small cards from it — no more. Do the reviews daily for two weeks, adding cards only from topics you have genuinely studied. By the end you will know whether the habit fits you, with a deck that is yours and a review load you control. Questions from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) can then show you which topics deserve decks next.

Q: Is Anki free for medical students?
A: Yes. Anki is a free, open-source flashcard program for desktop, and its scheduling algorithm is built on spaced repetition. You create your own decks or import shared ones.
Q: How many new Anki cards should I add per day?
A: Start small — around fifteen to twenty from a topic you have just studied. Every new card creates future reviews, so the right number is the one whose daily review load you can sustain even in a busy week.
Q: Should I use cloze or basic cards?
A: Both. Cloze cards are quick to make from notes and suit lists and pathways. Basic question-and-answer cards force more specific retrieval and suit key facts. Most students end up mixing them.
Q: Can Anki replace solving MCQs?
A: No. Anki maintains facts; MCQs train you to apply them under exam conditions and expose gaps. Use questions to find weak points, then write cards for the facts behind the questions you missed.
