Failing FMGE is a particular kind of setback: you have already finished medical school, the registration you need is one exam away, and the result says "not yet". Take the week you need. Then read this **fmge repeaters guide** for what it is — not consolation, but a diagnostic method. NBEMS publishes results every session and historically a minority of candidates pass, which means a failed attempt puts you in large, normal company. What separates candidates who clear on the next attempt is not more suffering. It is an honest answer to one question: **what, specifically, produced the last score?**

## The FMGE repeaters guide, step 1: diagnose before you open a single book

Most repeaters restart by re-reading everything, which quietly assumes the problem was "not enough reading". Sometimes it was. Often it was not. Before any new plan, write down your best analysis of the last attempt across five possible failure zones:

- **Coverage failure** — whole subjects or large areas never studied. Your score was capped before you entered the hall.
- **Conversion failure** — you studied the material but could not recognise or recall it in MCQ form. Plenty of reading, very little question practice.
- **Exam-behaviour failure** — questions left blank despite **no negative marking**, time mismanaged across Part A and Part B, panic in one section spilling into the other.
- **Revision failure** — studied early topics once, months before the exam, and never revisited them. By exam day they were gone.
- **Circumstance failure** — illness, work, family pressure, broken sleep in the final weeks. Real, and worth naming honestly, because the fix is logistics, not books.

Most failed attempts are a blend, but usually one zone dominates. That dominant zone is where your next attempt must be *structurally different* — not just longer.

## Step 2: Do not repeat the same preparation and call it a new attempt

The most dangerous sentence for a repeater is "this time I will do the same thing, but harder". If the system produced 132 last time, running it harder tends to produce something near 132. Change the system according to your diagnosis:

- **Coverage failure →** rebuild the plan by weightage, and finish the syllabus this time. Medicine (33 questions), Surgery (32), OBG (30) and Community Medicine (30) are non-negotiable; the full distribution — 17 each for Anatomy, Physiology, Biochemistry; 15 each for Paediatrics, Ophthalmology, ENT; 13 each for Pathology, Microbiology, Pharmacology; 10 each for Forensic Medicine and Radiology; 5 each for Psychiatry, Dermatology, Anaesthesia, Orthopaedics — decides your calendar.
- **Conversion failure →** flip the ratio. Questions first, reading second. Work topic-wise in the [question bank](/qbank/), and let wrong answers choose what you read.
- **Exam-behaviour failure →** train the exam itself: full papers under real timing until pacing and attempting all 300 are automatic.
- **Revision failure →** build revision cycles into the plan from week one — every topic revisited on a schedule, not "at the end".
- **Circumstance failure →** fix the logistics early: study hours protected, support arranged, sleep defended in the final month.

## Step 3: Start from a mock, not from chapter one

You are not a beginner; you have a base, even if it feels shaky. Prove where it stands. In your first week, sit a full paper from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) under real conditions and analyse it in detail. The eight-part analysis will show you, subject by subject, where you actually are — candidates are routinely surprised in both directions: weaker than feared in a subject they "covered", stronger in one they neglected. Build your timetable from that evidence, spending the most time where the marks are leaking, not where studying feels comfortable.

Then set a testing rhythm and keep it sacred: [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) as you re-cover each subject, and a full test from the [grand test series](/tests/) every one to two weeks. After each, classify errors as knowledge gap, silly mistake or failed guess — repeaters often discover their dominant bucket is silly mistakes and conversion, not knowledge, and that discovery alone can be worth the marks they were missing. Remember the target: the pass mark is **150 of 300**, and your job is to build a mock average safely above it.

## Step 4: Manage the repeater-specific traps

**The shame spiral.** Watching juniors register while you re-prepare is genuinely hard. Limit the inputs that feed it, keep a small circle that knows the plan, and measure yourself against your last mock score — the only comparison that contains information.

**Overcorrection.** Some repeaters respond by buying everything and attempting a superhuman schedule, then burn out by month two. A repeater's plan should be *smaller and more tested* than the first attempt, not bigger and more desperate.

**Comfort studying.** Re-reading your strongest subject feels productive and changes nothing. Your timetable should be visibly unfair — weighted toward weak, high-yield areas.

**Treating the attempt as a verdict on you as a doctor.** FMGE is a screening test with a fixed format; it measures preparation for that format. Thousands of competent doctors have needed more than one attempt at it. The result describes a preparation cycle, not a career.

## A realistic repeater timeline

With a base already in place, three to four focused months is a sensible frame: the first fortnight for diagnosis and the weakest big subjects, the middle for weightage-ordered re-coverage with daily questions, and the final month for revision cycles and weekly full mocks — no new material. Adjust the length to your diagnosis: a pure exam-behaviour failure may need less time; a coverage failure needs the full syllabus finished, however long that honestly takes.

You have one advantage no first-timer has: you know exactly what the exam feels like, and you have a real result to learn from. Use both. Begin this week with one full paper on MedSetu — free, no sign-up — read its analysis like a report on your last attempt, and let that, not guilt, write your next timetable. The [grand test series](/tests/) is there when you are ready to prove the new system works.

Q: I failed FMGE once. What should I do differently in my next attempt?
A: First diagnose why the last attempt fell short — coverage, conversion of knowledge into MCQ answers, exam behaviour, revision, or circumstances. Then change that specific part of your system instead of simply repeating the same preparation with more hours.
Q: How long should a repeater prepare for the next FMGE attempt?
A: With an existing base, three to four focused months is a common frame: diagnosis and weak subjects first, weightage-ordered re-coverage with daily questions next, and a final month of revision and weekly full mocks. A coverage failure may honestly need longer.
Q: Should repeaters re-read all 19 subjects from the start?
A: Usually not cover to cover. Start with a full mock test to find where marks are actually leaking, then re-cover subjects in weightage order, spending the most time on weak high-yield areas and testing each subject as you finish it.
Q: What is the most common fixable reason repeaters fall short?
A: A frequent pattern is conversion failure — plenty of reading but too little MCQ practice and revision, so knowledge does not turn into answers under exam conditions. Daily question practice, an error log and regular analysed mocks address it directly.
