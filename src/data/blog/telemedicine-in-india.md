For most of Indian medical history, a consultation legally meant a shared room — until **telemedicine in India** received its formal framework in 2020, when the Telemedicine Practice Guidelines were issued under the Indian Medical Council framework during the COVID-19 emergency. What had been a legal grey area overnight became regulated practice: any **Registered Medical Practitioner (RMP)** could consult remotely, within defined rules. The pandemic forced the change, but the reasons it endures are structural — a vast country, specialists concentrated in cities, and patients for whom a hospital visit costs a day's wages and a long journey. As a student, you will practise in this hybrid world; learn its rules as carefully as its technology.

## Telemedicine in India: The 2020 Guidelines Framework

The guidelines are best understood as an attempt to make remote care a real consultation — with the same duties — rather than a casual chat. Their framework-level provisions include:

- **Only RMPs may practise telemedicine**, and they must display their registration number in every interaction, prescription and invoice. Anonymity is not permitted on the doctor's side; the patient must know exactly who is treating them.
- **Identity and consent.** The patient's identity must be established. Consent is implied when the patient initiates the consultation, and must be explicitly recorded when the doctor, a caregiver or a health worker initiates it.
- **Modes are defined.** Consultations may occur by video, audio or text, and the guidelines treat them differently — video most closely approximates an in-person examination, while text-only consultation carries the greatest limitations and the most caution.
- **First consult versus follow-up are distinguished.** A follow-up for the same condition, within a defined window of an in-person visit, permits more than a first remote encounter with a new complaint, because the doctor already knows the patient and the diagnosis.
- **Prescribing is categorised.** The guidelines place medicines into lists by safety and by consultation type: some may be prescribed in any teleconsultation, some only on a first video consultation or in follow-up, and a prohibited category — including Schedule X and narcotic drugs — may never be prescribed remotely. Prescriptions must be proper signed prescriptions, not messages.
- **Records must be kept** of consultations, prescriptions and advice, just as for in-person care, and the same professional conduct, privacy and fee-transparency expectations apply. Emergencies must be directed to in-person care.

The framework's spirit matters more than its list numbers: remote care is real care, with real documentation, real accountability and real limits.

## What Telemedicine Does Well

- **Follow-up and chronic disease care.** Stable hypertension, diabetes reviews, medication adherence checks and report discussions translate well remotely, sparing patients repeated travel for five-minute reviews.
- **Access to scarce expertise.** A patient in a district town can reach a specialist opinion that geography previously denied them; tele-radiology and tele-pathology similarly move images and slides instead of people.
- **Triage.** Remote assessment can sort who needs urgent in-person care, who can safely wait, and who needs reassurance — a force multiplier for crowded systems.
- **Mental health and sensitive care.** Many patients speak more freely from home about psychological symptoms, sexual health or stigmatised conditions than across a crowded clinic desk.
- **Continuity during disruption.** Floods, epidemics and transport strikes no longer sever every doctor-patient link at once.

## Where Telemedicine Must Stop

The limits are clinical, not merely technical. You cannot palpate an abdomen, percuss a chest, test reflexes or smell ketones through a screen. Image and audio quality vary; patients may understate or misdescribe symptoms; the elderly and the poor — often those with most to gain — may have the weakest connectivity and digital literacy, so telemedicine can widen the very gaps it promises to close if it replaces rather than supplements physical services. Privacy depends on platforms and practices handling health data responsibly. And some encounters simply require presence: emergencies, first presentations of serious undifferentiated illness, and any situation where your examination would change the decision. Knowing when to say "this needs an in-person visit" is the core telemedicine skill.

## The Public System: Telemedicine at National Scale

India has also built telemedicine into its public infrastructure rather than leaving it to private apps alone. **eSanjeevani**, the Government of India's telemedicine service, runs in two broad modes: provider-to-provider consultations, where a community health officer or doctor at a rural health and wellness centre connects the patient to a doctor or specialist at a hub, and doctor-to-patient consultations for citizens directly. The first model deserves a student's attention because it solves the digital-divide problem structurally: the patient needs no smartphone or connectivity of their own — the health centre supplies the technology, a trained worker assists the examination, and the specialist's expertise travels instead. Alongside this, the **Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission** is building the rails for digital health generally — health IDs for citizens and interoperable records — so that a teleconsultation can, with consent, see the patient's prior reports rather than starting blind. These systems are evolving and uneven in reach, but their direction is clear: telemedicine as public infrastructure, not merely private convenience.

## Practising Well in the Hybrid Era

Treat the remote consultation with in-person discipline: verify identity, take a structured history, state the limits of what you can assess remotely, document fully, prescribe within the guideline lists, give explicit safety-net advice ("if the fever crosses this point, or breathing worsens, go to the nearest facility"), and arrange follow-up. Learn the technology your institution uses before you need it at midnight. Above all, remember that the screen changes the medium, not the medicine — the reasoning, honesty and record-keeping expected of you are identical.

Q: When did telemedicine become legal and regulated in India?
A: The Telemedicine Practice Guidelines were issued in 2020 under the Indian Medical Council framework, allowing Registered Medical Practitioners to consult remotely within defined rules on identity, consent, prescribing and records.
Q: Can any doctor practise telemedicine in India?
A: Only Registered Medical Practitioners, who must display their registration number in interactions and prescriptions. The same professional conduct and record-keeping duties apply as in-person care.
Q: Are there medicines that cannot be prescribed by teleconsultation?
A: Yes. The guidelines categorise medicines by consultation type and safety, and prohibit remote prescribing of Schedule X and narcotic/psychotropic drugs. Emergencies must be referred to in-person care.
Q: What is telemedicine best suited for?
A: Follow-up and chronic disease reviews, specialist access across distance, triage, mental health consultations and report discussions. It cannot replace physical examination when examination findings would change management.
