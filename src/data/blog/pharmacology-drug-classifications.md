**Pharmacology drug classifications** are the single most efficient revision tool in the subject, and learning to use them properly changes pharmacology from a memory burden into a system. Pharmacology carries 13 questions in the FMGE (300 MCQs, two parts of 150, no negative marking, pass mark 150), but its classifications reach far beyond those 13: drug-class questions appear inside Medicine, Psychiatry, Anaesthesia, OBG and Ophthalmology stems constantly. The students who struggle treat every drug as a separate fact. The students who score learn a small number of class frameworks, hang each drug on a framework, and reserve separate memory only for prototypes and exceptions. This article teaches that framework.

## Why classification works

A classification is a compression device. If you know that a drug belongs to a class, you inherit the class's mechanism, its main uses and most of its adverse effects without memorising them again. What remains genuinely drug-specific is small: the prototype's details, and the one or two ways each member differs from its class. Examination questions are built the same way — the majority test class-level knowledge, and the discriminating questions test exactly those member-specific exceptions. Study the way the paper is written and the two align.

## Pharmacology drug classifications: the three axes

Every useful classification sorts drugs along one of three axes. Knowing which axis a classification uses tells you what questions it can answer.

- **By mechanism of action** — *how* the drug works: enzyme inhibition, receptor blockade, ion-channel effects. Mechanism classifications predict effects and side effects, so they answer "which of the following" and adverse-effect questions
- **By site or anatomical system** — *where* it acts: autonomic, cardiovascular, central nervous system. Site classifications organise your revision by the system you are studying elsewhere
- **By therapeutic use** — *what it treats*: antihypertensives, antiepileptics, antidepressants. Use classifications mirror clinical stems, where the question starts from a patient and a condition

Strong revision uses all three: learn each class by mechanism, file it under its system, and practise retrieving it by use.

## The prototype method

For every class, choose the **prototype** — the best-known, most typical member — and learn it completely: mechanism, actions, uses, adverse effects, important interactions. Then learn every other member as a *difference from the prototype*: faster or slower, safer in pregnancy or forbidden, one extra use, one extra toxicity. Textbook examples of the pattern:

- **Beta blockers** — prototype propranolol (non-selective); then atenolol and metoprolol as the cardioselective difference, labetalol as the added alpha-blockade used in pregnancy hypertension, and the class cautions in asthma and diabetes as inherited properties
- **ACE inhibitors** — the class effects (dry cough, angioedema risk, teratogenicity) inherited by all members, with individual drugs differing mainly in pharmacokinetics
- **Local anaesthetics** — the ester–amide split (procaine versus lidocaine as prototypes), which predicts metabolism and allergy patterns

This converts a forty-drug chapter into one deep card and a page of differences.

## Exceptions: where the marks hide

Examiners love the drug that breaks its class's rule, because it separates framework-learners from list-memorisers. Keep a dedicated **exceptions list** and revise it separately. Standard textbook-level examples:

- **Sotalol** — a beta blocker that also has class III antiarrhythmic action
- **Tramadol** — an opioid analgesic with additional monoamine reuptake inhibition
- **Clozapine** — the atypical antipsychotic reserved for resistant schizophrenia because of agranulocytosis risk and monitoring
- **Metformin** — the antidiabetic that does not cause weight gain or hypoglycaemia when used alone
- **Aspirin** — the NSAID whose antiplatelet use at low dose outlives its analgesic role

Every "all of the following EXCEPT" question is an exceptions-list question. Build the list as you study each system rather than in a panic at the end.

## A worked routine for one classification

Take any class and process it in five steps. First, state the mechanism in one line. Second, name the prototype and its four facts. Third, list the members you actually need, each with its one difference. Fourth, write the class's shared adverse effects and contraindications once, at class level. Fifth, answer ten questions on the class immediately — from a [question bank](/qbank/) topic set, for instance — and let the wrong answers tell you which differences you have not yet fixed. Repeat the class on a spaced cycle. A classification processed this way takes under an hour and survives; a chapter read passively takes longer and does not.

## When classifications mislead

Use the framework critically. A classification predicts *most* of a drug's behaviour, not all of it: dose can change the effect (aspirin's antiplatelet and analgesic roles), one drug can sit in two frameworks at once (sotalol again), and newer members sometimes escape the class's classic toxicity while keeping its name. The safe habit is two-layer recall: answer from the class first, then pause and ask whether this specific drug is on your exceptions list. That two-second check is what converts framework knowledge into correct answers on the discriminating questions — and it is only possible if the exceptions list was built deliberately, class by class, during revision rather than hoped for.

## Connecting classifications across subjects

Because classes cross subject borders, revise them where they are used. Antihypertensive classes belong with Medicine's hypertension teaching; antipsychotic and antidepressant classes with [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) in psychiatry; anaesthetic agents with anaesthesia; glaucoma drugs with ophthalmology. When a clinical stem names a condition, your retrieval path should run condition → class → prototype → exception. Q: Why are drug classifications so important in pharmacology?
A: A classification compresses hundreds of drugs into a small number of class frameworks. Knowing a drug's class gives you its mechanism, main uses and most adverse effects at once, leaving only the prototype details and exceptions to memorise separately.
Q: What are the three ways drugs are classified?
A: By mechanism of action (how the drug works), by site or system (where it acts), and by therapeutic use (what it treats). Each axis answers a different kind of examination question.
Q: What is the prototype method?
A: Learn one typical member of each class completely — mechanism, uses, adverse effects, interactions — then learn every other member only as a difference from that prototype.
Q: How should I handle exceptions to a drug class?
A: Keep a separate exceptions list — drugs like sotalol, tramadol, clozapine and metformin that break their class's usual rules — and revise it on its own, because "except" questions are built from it.
