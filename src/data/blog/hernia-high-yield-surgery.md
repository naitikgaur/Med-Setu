## Hernia FMGE surgery basics: definitions the paper builds on

A **hernia** is the protrusion of a viscus, or part of a viscus, through an abnormal opening in the wall of the cavity containing it. Every hernia is described in the same three parts:

- **Sac** — the outpouching of peritoneum, with a neck, body and fundus
- **Coverings** — the layers of the body wall through which the sac passes
- **Contents** — what sits inside the sac: **omentum** (an omentocele or epiplocele), **intestine** (an enterocele), or sometimes a specific structure that gives the hernia its eponymous name

Named content traps: a **Richter hernia** contains only part of the bowel wall circumference, so it can strangulate without obstruction; a **Maydl hernia** contains a W-shaped bowel loop; a **sliding hernia** has a partly extraperitoneal viscus — caecum, bladder or sigmoid colon — forming part of the sac wall. A hernia is **reducible** if its contents return to the abdomen, **irreducible** if they cannot (because of adhesions or a narrow neck), **obstructed** if the bowel lumen is blocked but blood supply survives, and **strangulated** if the blood supply is cut off — the surgical emergency of this chapter.

## Anatomy of the inguinal canal: learn it once, use it everywhere

The **inguinal canal** is an oblique passage about 4 cm long in the lower abdominal wall, running from the deep inguinal ring to the superficial inguinal ring, above the medial half of the inguinal ligament. It transmits the **spermatic cord** in males and the **round ligament of the uterus** in females, along with the ilioinguinal nerve. Its anatomy is asked so often that you should be able to write it out from memory:

- **Deep (internal) inguinal ring** — an opening in the fascia transversalis, about 1.25 cm above the mid-point of the inguinal ligament (midway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the pubic symphysis); the inferior epigastric vessels lie medial to it — the single most useful landmark in this topic
- **Superficial (external) inguinal ring** — a triangular opening in the external oblique aponeurosis, just above and medial to the pubic tubercle
- **Anterior wall** — external oblique aponeurosis along its whole length, reinforced laterally by the internal oblique
- **Posterior wall** — fascia transversalis along its whole length, reinforced medially by the conjoint tendon
- **Roof** — the arched fibres of the internal oblique and transversus abdominis
- **Floor** — the inguinal ligament, with the lacunar ligament medially

**Hesselbach's triangle**, through which direct hernias emerge, is bounded medially by the lateral border of the rectus abdominis, laterally by the inferior epigastric vessels, and below by the inguinal ligament.

## Inguinal hernia: direct versus indirect, the classic comparison

Inguinal hernias are the commonest hernias overall and are far commoner in men. The indirect–direct distinction is probably the single most repeated hernia question in FMGE, so learn it as a contrast, not as two separate lists:

- **Indirect inguinal hernia** — enters through the deep ring, **lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels**, travels through the whole canal inside the spermatic cord coverings, and may descend into the scrotum; it results from a patent processus vaginalis, so it is the hernia of children and young adults, and it is the commonest type in both sexes
- **Direct inguinal hernia** — pushes forward through the posterior wall in **Hesselbach's triangle**, **medial to the inferior epigastric vessels**; it is a hernia of older men with weak abdominal muscles, rarely enters the scrotum, and rarely strangulates because its neck is wide
- **Clinical tests** — in the **ring occlusion test**, pressure over the deep ring (just above the mid-point of the inguinal ligament) after reduction controls an indirect hernia but a direct hernia still bulges medial to the finger; cough impulse, reducibility and whether the swelling is above the inguinal ligament complete the bedside picture
- **Coverings** — an indirect hernia that traverses the whole canal gains all three spermatic cord coverings: internal spermatic fascia (from fascia transversalis), cremasteric fascia (from internal oblique) and external spermatic fascia (from external oblique)

Treatment at textbook level: **herniotomy** (excision of the sac, the essential step in children), **herniorrhaphy** (posterior wall repair, classically Bassini), or **hernioplasty** with mesh — the tension-free **Lichtenstein repair** is the standard modern answer, with laparoscopic TEP and TAPP as minimally invasive options. ## Femoral, umbilical and incisional hernias

Each remaining type has facts the paper tests almost every cycle:

- **Femoral hernia** — protrudes through the femoral ring into the femoral canal, **below and lateral to the pubic tubercle** (an inguinal hernia is above and medial to it — the classic one-line distinction). It is **commoner in women**, though the commonest hernia in women overall is still the indirect inguinal hernia. The femoral ring is rigid and bounded by unyielding structures, so a femoral hernia has the **highest risk of strangulation** of the common hernias and often presents as an emergency
- **Umbilical hernia** — congenital umbilical hernias in infants usually close spontaneously by about 4–5 years of age and are observed; the adult **paraumbilical hernia** emerges through the linea alba just above or below the umbilicus, typically in multiparous, obese women, contains omentum, and does need repair. An **exomphalos (omphalocele)** is a different, congenital condition where viscera herniate through the umbilicus covered by a sac — do not let the options confuse the two
- **Incisional hernia** — herniation through a previous surgical scar, classically after wound infection, in obese or diabetic patients, or after emergency surgery; risk factors and prevention (sound closure technique, treating infection and cough) are the usual stem material, and large ones are repaired with mesh
- **Other named hernias** — **epigastric hernia** through the linea alba between xiphisternum and umbilicus, often just preperitoneal fat; **Spigelian hernia** through the semilunar line; **obturator hernia** in thin elderly women, which may present with pain along the medial thigh (Howship–Romberg sign); and **lumbar hernias** through the superior (Grynfeltt) or inferior (Petit) lumbar triangles

## Complications and the emergency stems

The complication sequence is a favourite clinical-vignette chain, and the order matters:

- **Irreducibility** — contents cannot be returned, from adhesions or a narrow neck; by itself, not an emergency
- **Obstruction** — the bowel in the sac is blocked: colicky pain, vomiting, distension and absolute constipation, but the blood supply is intact
- **Strangulation** — the blood supply is occluded at the neck: the swelling becomes tense, tender and painful, loses its cough impulse, and the patient may develop obstruction with toxicity; this demands **emergency surgery**, because gangrene and perforation follow
- **Incarceration** is the older term for an irreducible hernia in which obstruction or strangulation is impending — stems use it loosely to mean trapped contents

Exam traps: a strangulated hernia should never be pushed back by forceful taxis, because gangrenous bowel can be reduced into the abdomen — reduction en masse, where sac and contents are displaced together and strangulation persists unseen, is the named disaster. A tender, irreducible groin swelling with vomiting in an elderly woman is a femoral hernia until proved otherwise. Q: What is the key anatomical difference between direct and indirect inguinal hernia?
A: An indirect hernia enters the deep ring lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels and follows the canal; a direct hernia protrudes through Hesselbach's triangle, medial to the inferior epigastric vessels.
Q: Which hernia has the highest risk of strangulation?
A: The femoral hernia, because the femoral ring is narrow and bounded by rigid structures. Richter hernia can also strangulate without causing bowel obstruction.
Q: What are the boundaries of Hesselbach's triangle?
A: Medially the lateral border of rectus abdominis, laterally the inferior epigastric vessels, and inferiorly the inguinal ligament. Direct inguinal hernias emerge through it.
Q: What is the standard modern repair for an inguinal hernia?
A: The tension-free Lichtenstein mesh hernioplasty is the standard open repair; herniotomy alone is the essential operation in children, and TEP and TAPP are the laparoscopic options.
