Pathology is the language in which most of FMGE is written, and the **pathology high yield topics** — cell injury, inflammation and neoplasia — repay focused revision far beyond pathology's own questions. A fact about necrosis appears in surgery, a tumour marker appears in medicine, and a granuloma appears in microbiology. FMGE is conducted by NBEMS: 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, no negative marking, and a pass mark of 150 out of 300. Standard texts such as Robbins and Cotran present these three areas as the foundation of all disease, and the exam treats them the same way. Master the definitions, the classifications and the classic comparisons, and you collect marks across several subjects at once.

## Cell injury: reversible, irreversible and cell death

**Cell injury** occurs when a cell cannot adapt to stress. Causes include hypoxia (the commonest), physical agents, chemicals and drugs, infections, immunological reactions, genetic defects and nutritional imbalance. The key distinction the exam tests is reversible versus irreversible injury.

- **Reversible injury** — cellular swelling (hydropic change) and fatty change are the classic morphological signs; function recovers if the stimulus is removed
- **Irreversible injury** — marked by membrane damage, severe mitochondrial dysfunction and lysosomal rupture; the cell proceeds to death
- **Fatty change (steatosis)** — most often in the liver, from alcohol, obesity, diabetes and toxins; fat vacuoles in hepatocytes
- **Adaptations** — hypertrophy (increased cell size), hyperplasia (increased cell number), atrophy (decreased size), metaplasia (one adult cell type replaced by another, for example squamous metaplasia in the bronchi of smokers) and dysplasia (disordered growth, a precursor to carcinoma)

Cell death occurs by two main mechanisms, and their comparison is a guaranteed question area:

- **Necrosis** — unprogrammed death of groups of cells, always pathological, with inflammation; the cell swells and its membrane ruptures
- **Apoptosis** — programmed, energy-dependent death of single cells, physiological or pathological, with no inflammation; the cell shrinks, chromatin condenses, and apoptotic bodies are phagocytosed. Caspases are the key enzymes, and **Bcl-2** is anti-apoptotic

Learn the types of necrosis with one example each:

- **Coagulative necrosis** — architecture preserved; typical of ischaemic infarction in solid organs such as heart and kidney
- **Liquefactive necrosis** — tissue digested into liquid; brain infarction and abscesses
- **Caseous necrosis** — cheese-like appearance; the hallmark of tuberculosis, seen inside granulomas
- **Fat necrosis** — enzymatic, in acute pancreatitis, with chalky saponification; also traumatic fat necrosis of the breast
- **Fibrinoid necrosis** — in immune reactions involving blood vessel walls, as in vasculitis
- **Gangrenous necrosis** — usually a limb that has lost its blood supply, dry or wet

Pigments and calcification complete this section: **haemosiderin** is iron-storage pigment (stained by Perl's Prussian blue), lipofuscin is the wear-and-tear pigment of ageing, dystrophic calcification occurs in damaged tissue with normal serum calcium, and metastatic calcification occurs in normal tissue with high serum calcium.

## Inflammation: acute, chronic and granulomatous

**Inflammation** is the response of vascularised living tissue to injury. Acute and chronic inflammation differ in onset, duration, cells and outcome — a comparison table you should be able to reproduce from memory.

Acute inflammation has three major components: vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and emigration of leucocytes (mainly **neutrophils**). Its cardinal signs are redness, heat, swelling, pain and loss of function. Remember the sequence of leucocyte events:

- **Margination, rolling, adhesion and transmigration** — rolling is mediated by selectins, firm adhesion by integrins binding ICAM and VCAM
- **Chemotaxis** — movement towards C5a, leukotriene B4, IL-8 and bacterial products
- **Phagocytosis** — recognition, engulfment and killing; opsonins such as IgG and C3b coat the particle
- **Defects** — chronic granulomatous disease is a defect of the NADPH oxidase killing mechanism; leucocyte adhesion deficiency impairs adhesion

Chemical mediators are heavily tested. **Histamine** (from mast cells) causes early vasodilation and permeability. Prostaglandins mediate pain and fever, leukotrienes mediate chemotaxis and bronchospasm, and cytokines **TNF and IL-1** drive fever and the acute-phase response. Complement products C3a and C5a are anaphylatoxins, and C5a is a powerful chemotactic factor. Outcomes of acute inflammation are resolution, abscess formation, healing by fibrosis, or progression to chronic inflammation.

**Chronic inflammation** is prolonged inflammation with macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells, tissue destruction and attempted repair occurring together. The macrophage is the central cell. **Granulomatous inflammation** is a distinctive type in which activated macrophages form epithelioid cells and multinucleated giant cells. Causes include tuberculosis (caseating granuloma), leprosy, syphilis, fungal infections, sarcoidosis (non-caseating granuloma) and foreign bodies. Langhans giant cells, with nuclei arranged at the periphery, are classic in tuberculosis.

Healing follows two routes: **regeneration** in tissues with proliferative capacity, and **repair by fibrosis** (scar) elsewhere. Healing by first intention occurs in a clean incised wound; second intention involves more granulation tissue and wound contraction by myofibroblasts. Keloid is excessive collagen beyond the wound margin, unlike a hypertrophic scar.

## Pathology high yield topics: neoplasia essentials

**Neoplasia** means new growth; a neoplasm (tumour) grows uncoordinated with normal tissue and persists after the stimulus stops. This is the single most tested area in pathology, so learn it in layers.

First, benign versus malignant:

- **Benign tumours** — well differentiated, slow-growing, localised, encapsulated, do not metastasise; suffix **-oma** (lipoma, adenoma, leiomyoma)
- **Malignant tumours** — may be poorly differentiated or anaplastic, faster growth, local invasion and **metastasis**, which is the single most reliable feature of malignancy
- **Carcinoma** is malignancy of epithelial tissue; **sarcoma** is malignancy of connective tissue
- Exceptions to naming: melanoma, lymphoma, seminoma and hepatoma sound benign but are malignant; a **hamartoma** is a disorganised mass of tissue native to the site, and a **choristoma** is normal tissue in an abnormal site

Second, spread and staging. Carcinomas typically spread first by lymphatics, sarcomas typically by the bloodstream, and some tumours show transcoelomic seeding across body cavities. Common sites of blood-borne metastasis are liver, lung, bone and brain. **Grading** is the degree of differentiation under the microscope; **staging** is the extent of spread, expressed by the **TNM system**, and staging has greater clinical value than grading.

Third, carcinogenesis and tumour markers:

- **Proto-oncogenes** become oncogenes by gain of function — examples include RAS, MYC (Burkitt lymphoma, t(8;14)) and HER2 amplification in breast carcinoma
- **Tumour suppressor genes** act by loss of function — **RB** (retinoblastoma), **p53** (the most common genetic alteration in human cancers, Li-Fraumeni syndrome) and APC (familial adenomatous polyposis)
- **Chemical carcinogens** — aflatoxin B1 with hepatocellular carcinoma, asbestos with mesothelioma, vinyl chloride with hepatic angiosarcoma, aromatic amines with bladder carcinoma
- **Viral oncogenesis** — HPV 16 and 18 with cervical carcinoma, EBV with Burkitt lymphoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma, HBV and HCV with hepatocellular carcinoma
- **Tumour markers** — PSA for prostate, AFP for hepatocellular carcinoma and non-seminomatous germ cell tumours, CEA for colorectal carcinoma, CA-125 for ovarian carcinoma, CA 19-9 for pancreatic carcinoma, beta-hCG for choriocarcinoma, calcitonin for medullary thyroid carcinoma

Finally, **paraneoplastic syndromes** — effects of a tumour not due to its mass or metastasis. Classic pairs are SIADH and Cushing syndrome with small cell lung carcinoma, hypercalcaemia from PTHrP with squamous cell lung carcinoma, and migratory thrombophlebitis (Trousseau syndrome) with pancreatic carcinoma.

## How the exam tests pathology, and how to revise it

Pathology questions are usually one of three types: a definition or classification, a classic comparison (necrosis versus apoptosis, benign versus malignant, dystrophic versus metastatic calcification), or a clinicopathological correlation (a description that ends in a tumour marker, a pigment or a type of necrosis). Revise by tables and pairs, not by prose. After each topic, attempt questions immediately — topic-wise practice in a [question bank](/qbank/) fixes the comparisons, and timed [previous-year papers](/papers/) show you how often these same facts return in new wording.

Q: What is the difference between necrosis and apoptosis?
A: Necrosis is pathological death of groups of cells with swelling, membrane rupture and inflammation, while apoptosis is programmed death of single cells with shrinkage, caspase activation and no inflammation.
Q: Which is the most reliable feature of a malignant tumour?
A: Metastasis — the spread of tumour to sites physically discontinuous with it — is the most reliable feature distinguishing malignant from benign tumours.
Q: What is the difference between grading and staging of a tumour?
A: Grading is the microscopic degree of differentiation, while staging is the clinical extent of spread, usually by the TNM system; staging has greater prognostic value.
Q: Which tumour suppressor gene is most commonly altered in human cancers?
A: p53, on chromosome 17p, is the most commonly mutated tumour suppressor gene in human cancers; germline mutation causes Li-Fraumeni syndrome.
