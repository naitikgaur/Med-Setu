How you handle revision last 7 days before exam day decides whether months of preparation arrive in the hall with you. The final week tempts students into two opposite errors: frantic expansion — new books, new topics, other people's notes — or paralysed re-reading of everything at once. Both waste the week's real purpose. These seven days are for **consolidation**: strengthening what you have already learned, rehearsing the exam itself, and arriving rested. Here is a calm structure for doing exactly that.

## Revision Last 7 Days Before Exam: The Governing Rules

Three rules govern the whole week:

- **Nothing substantially new.** A brand-new large topic learned in the final week is learned shallowly, retained poorly, and paid for with the revision of five topics you already half-knew. Small gaps can be patched; new territories cannot be conquered.
- **Active over passive.** Recall, questions, and mistake review beat re-reading. In the last week especially, your time should produce answers, not just page turns.
- **Protect sleep like a subject.** Consolidation happens during sleep, and a tired brain retrieves slowly across 300 questions. The student who sleeps properly in the final week is revising while asleep; the one cramming at 3 a.m. is borrowing marks from exam day.

Keep the exam's shape in view all week: FMGE, conducted by NBEMS, 300 MCQs in two parts of 150 with a break between, +1 per correct answer, no negative marking, pass mark 150. Every plan below serves that shape.

## Days 7–5: Close the Loops

Use the first three days to finish what is unfinished — but only from *your own* materials and data:

- **Work your mistakes list.** Every error you have logged from mocks and the [question bank](/qbank/) is a mark you have already identified as winnable. Clear them topic by topic.
- **Revisit your weakest high-weight topics** — the ones your mock analyses keep flagging. In the final week, marks hide in known weaknesses, not in your strengths.
- **Patch, don't expand.** If a small, specific gap keeps costing questions — a table, a classification, a drug group — patch it now with a focused hour, then test it with questions.
- **Do one full mock early in this window**, ideally from the [previous-year papers](/papers/), and mine its analysis properly: topics, overtime questions, wasted attempts. Let its findings set the agenda for days 4–2.

End each day by writing the next day's short list. In the final week, a written plan is an anxiety treatment as much as an organiser.

## Days 4–2: Rehearse the Exam, Sharpen the Recall

Shift the balance from learning to performing:

- **One more full simulation, no more.** Sit a complete paper in the real two-part manner, at the real time of day, with the break used as you will use it. After this, your remaining mocks are done — late heavy simulations mostly add fatigue and shaky confidence.
- **Rapid-fire recall sessions:** flip through your own notes, flashcards, and marked pages, testing yourself rather than reading. Cover, recall, check, move on.
- **High-yield sweeps:** revise the compact, fact-dense material that MCQs love — classifications, triads, drug of choice, investigation of choice, commonest causes — from notes you have already made, not from a new source.
- **Re-attempt your bookmarked questions** so the topics you once found hard are re-met while there is still time to fix them.

Taper the total hours slightly across these days. You are an athlete in the last week before the race now: sharpness, not volume.

## Day 1 and Exam Morning: Arrive, Don't Cram

The day before the exam is for logistics and lightness:

- Confirm the centre location and travel time, and keep your admit card, ID, and permitted items ready the night before.
- Revise only lightly — a glance through your most-marked pages if it settles you — and stop at a fixed, early time.
- Eat normally, avoid heavy discussions comparing preparation, and sleep at your usual time. Do not try to force an unnaturally early night; a familiar routine works better.
- On the morning: a normal breakfast, no corridor cramming, and arrive with time to spare. Use the break between Part A and Part B to reset completely — whatever the first 150 held is finished business.

If anxiety spikes, return to what you know: you have rehearsed this exact format, you have a two-pass method, there is no negative marking so every question gets an attempt, and no single question can decide a 300-question paper.

## What to Stop Doing This Week

Stopping is half the plan. Stop: starting new books or new teachers' material; comparing progress with other students; all-night study; checking answers mid-mock; and chasing every rumoured "most important topic" list — your own mock data is a better guide than anyone's rumour. Also stop re-reading your strongest topics for comfort. Comfort is not a strategy in the final week; your remaining marks are in the weaknesses you have already identified.

Seven calm days will not transform your preparation — they are not meant to. They convert it: from knowledge in notebooks into marks in a hall, by a student who arrives rested, rehearsed, and hard to rattle.

Q: Should I learn new topics in the last 7 days before the exam?
A: Avoid substantially new, large topics. They are retained poorly and displace revision of material you already know. Patch small, specific gaps that keep costing you questions, and spend the week consolidating and rehearsing.
Q: How many mock tests should I take in the final week?
A: One or two full simulations early in the week, analysed properly, is enough. After that, shift to recall, mistake review, and high-yield sweeps — late heavy mocks mainly add fatigue.
Q: What should I do the day before the FMGE?
A: Sort logistics — centre, route, admit card and ID — revise only lightly, stop at a fixed early time, eat normally, and sleep at your usual time. Arrive rested rather than crammed.
Q: How should I use the break between Part A and Part B?
A: Treat Part A as finished. Eat something light, move around, avoid discussing questions, and start Part B as a fresh 150-question paper.
