**ENT for FMGE** carries **15 questions out of 300** in the NBEMS examination — two parts of 150 MCQs, no negative marking, pass mark 150. It is one of the most *organised* subjects in the syllabus: everything belongs to the ear, the nose and paranasal sinuses, or the throat and larynx, and within each region the examiners rotate through a short list of classic conditions. Students often under-prepare ENT because it feels minor next to Medicine or Surgery, but 15 questions from a finite list is exactly the kind of block that rewards a few focused days.

## ENT for FMGE: study by region, the way the paper asks

Divide the subject into three blocks and finish each with question practice before moving on. The ear block is usually the most question-dense, so start there.

## The ear: hearing loss and vertigo dominate

- **Hearing loss** — conductive versus sensorineural, and the tuning-fork tests (Rinne, Weber, absolute bone conduction) that separate them; this is the single most reliable ENT topic
- **Otitis media** — acute suppurative otitis media, chronic suppurative otitis media with its safe and unsafe types, and the complications examiners love, from mastoiditis to lateral sinus thrombosis
- **Otosclerosis and Ménière's disease** — the classic presentations: progressive conductive loss with a normal drum, and episodic vertigo with tinnitus and fluctuating hearing
- **Vertigo** — separating peripheral from central causes, BPPV and its positional testing and repositioning manoeuvres
- **Facial nerve** — its course through the temporal bone, levels of lesion, and Bell's palsy

Learn tuning-fork interpretation as a small grid — test result against type of loss — because several questions reduce to reading that grid correctly.

## The nose and sinuses

- **Epistaxis** — Little's area and Kiesselbach's plexus as the commonest site, first-aid measures, and when packing or cautery is needed
- **Sinusitis** — the sinuses, their drainage pathways, and complications of frontal and ethmoid disease
- **Deviated nasal septum, nasal polyps and allergic rhinitis** — standard presentations and management principles
- **Angiofibroma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma** — the adolescent boy with epistaxis, and the neck node with unilateral ear effusion; classic one-line stems

## The throat, larynx and airway

- **Tonsillitis and adenoids** — indications for tonsillectomy and complications such as peritonsillar abscess (quinsy)
- **Laryngeal disorders** — vocal nodules, laryngomalacia as the commonest cause of stridor in infants, and laryngeal cancer risk factors and presentation
- **Airway emergencies** — foreign bodies, epiglottitis, and the indications and anatomical landmarks of tracheostomy
- **Deep neck space infections** — Ludwig's angina and its airway threat

Airway questions test priorities — securing the airway comes before investigation — so learn them as action sequences.

## Books and study method

**Dhingra's Diseases of ENT** is the standard undergraduate text and maps closely to the FMGE syllabus. ENT is a subject of diagrams: the middle-ear cavity, the lateral wall of the nose, the laryngeal cartilages. Sketch the key diagrams once and label them; anatomy-adjacent facts (which nerve, which meatus, which wall) are asked directly. Revise with comparison tables — safe versus unsafe CSOM, conductive versus sensorineural loss — and short, frequent question sets rather than one long reading marathon.

## The questions students get wrong

Three traps recur in ENT. The first is **confusing the safe and unsafe types of chronic suppurative otitis media**: atticoantral (unsafe) disease, with its cholesteatoma and complication risk, is the one examiners build stems around, so learn its features — scanty foul discharge, attic or marginal perforation — against the benign tubotympanic type. The second is **referred otalgia**: earache with a normal ear examination points to shared nerve pathways from the teeth, tonsil, tongue base or larynx, and the question is really asking where the lesion hides. The third is **laterality and age clues**: unilateral symptoms in an older adult (a middle-ear effusion, a nasal mass, a neck node) are malignancy flags in ENT stems, while the same findings bilaterally in a child are usually benign. Reading for age and side before reading the options prevents most avoidable errors.

## Instruments, imaging and operative facts

Keep one short list for the factual tail: common instruments and their uses at recognition level, the imaging of choice for key conditions (CT for sinus and temporal bone disease, MRI for acoustic neuroma and inner-ear lesions), and the standard operations named in options — myringotomy, mastoidectomy types in outline, septoplasty, tonsillectomy, and the Caldwell–Luc approach as a historical-named favourite. Acoustic neuroma deserves its own lines: the cerebellopontine angle, earliest nerve involvement, and the investigation of choice. These one-liners are cheap to learn and appear steadily.

## A realistic study allocation

Plan two to three days for ENT in a full revision cycle. Day one belongs to the ear — hearing loss and its tests, otitis media in both forms, vertigo and the facial nerve. Day two covers the nose and sinuses in the morning and the throat and larynx in the afternoon, ending with airway emergencies. Day three is for consolidation: the comparison tables, the diagrams redrawn from memory, and a complete run of topic-wise questions with every error traced back to the page it came from. After that, ENT maintains itself through mixed practice — it appears inside full papers at its proper weight, and your tables need only a ten-minute refresh before each mock.
