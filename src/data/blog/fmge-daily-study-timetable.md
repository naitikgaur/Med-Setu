Most FMGE timetables fail for the same reason most diets fail: they are written for an ideal person on an ideal day. A real **fmge daily study timetable** has to survive a bad night's sleep, a family obligation and a low-motivation Tuesday — and still produce questions solved and topics revised. This article gives you the principles first, then two complete sample schedules: one for a full-time candidate and one for a candidate who is working or interning alongside preparation. Adapt the hours; keep the structure.

## Building your FMGE daily study timetable: the four blocks every day needs

Whatever your total hours, a productive day contains four kinds of work:

- **New study block** — the day's main topic, read actively with notes. Best placed at your sharpest hours.
- **Question block** — solving MCQs on what you studied, plus mixed questions. Non-negotiable, every day: the exam is 300 MCQs, and this is the only block that practises the exam itself.
- **Revision block** — revisiting earlier topics, marked questions and your error log. Short, daily, and what stops month one's work evaporating by month four.
- **Buffer and rest** — real breaks, one weekly lighter day, and a hard stop in the evening. A timetable without rest built in will take its rest anyway, as burnout.

Weightage should steer the week behind the timetable: Medicine (33 questions), Surgery (32), OBG (30) and Community Medicine (30) claim the most new-study blocks; smaller subjects rotate through fewer, focused sittings.

## Sample schedule 1: the full-time candidate (about 8 focused hours)

- **7:00–7:30** — Wake, morning routine. No phone-scrolling in bed; the first hour sets the day's tone.
- **7:30–8:00** — **Revision block:** yesterday's marked questions + error-log entries. Thirty minutes, every day, before anything new.
- **8:00–9:00** — Breakfast and a walk or light exercise.
- **9:00–12:00** — **New study block 1:** the main subject topic of the day (for example, cardiology within Medicine), notes open, phone in another room.
- **12:00–1:00** — **Question block 1:** topic-wise questions on the morning's study in the [question bank](/qbank/), in Practice mode so explanations teach as you go.
- **1:00–2:30** — Lunch and a genuine break — nap if that is your pattern.
- **2:30–4:30** — **New study block 2:** continue the same subject or take the day's second subject.
- **4:30–5:30** — **Question block 2:** mixed set in Exam mode — questions from earlier subjects, timed, to keep old material in play.
- **5:30–7:00** — Outside time. Non-negotiable.
- **7:00–8:30** — **Light block:** revise the day's notes into one-page summaries; update the error log; on test days this block is replaced by analysis.
- **Evening** — Dinner, family, entertainment. Hard stop on study by 9:30–10:00 so sleep is protected.

One day a week — Sunday for most — replace the new-study blocks with a full paper from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) or a test from the [grand test series](/tests/), and use the light block for its analysis. That is roughly eight focused hours on study days, sustained by the breaks rather than despite them.

## Sample schedule 2: the working candidate (about 4 focused hours)

If you are interning or working, your timetable must be smaller and fiercely consistent. Four good hours daily beat a fantasy ten-hour plan abandoned in week two.

- **6:30–7:30 (before work)** — **Revision + questions:** error log and marked questions first, then a short timed set. Morning-you is fresher than evening-you; spend that freshness on active work, not reading.
- **Work hours** — steal micro-revision where genuinely possible: one-page summaries on your phone during a commute or break. Do not count this in your four hours; treat it as a bonus.
- **7:30–9:30 (evening)** — **New study block:** one topic only, chosen from your weekly plan, read actively with notes. One topic finished properly beats three skimmed.
- **9:30–10:15** — **Question block:** topic questions on the evening's study, then stop. Protect the stop time — sleep is what converts today's study into tomorrow's memory.
- **Weekly off day** — one longer session: a [subject-wise test](/subjects/) for the subject you are currently covering, plus analysis, plus planning the next week's topics.

Working candidates should also plan on a longer total runway where possible, and weight weekends toward full papers in the final two months.

## Making any timetable survive contact with real life

- **Plan the week, execute the day.** Decide the week's subjects and topics on Sunday; each day then starts with zero decisions.
- **The two-hour rescue rule.** When a day collapses, do not write it off — do the revision block and one question block. Two honest hours keep the chain unbroken.
- **Same start time daily.** Willpower is unreliable; routine is not. Anchor the first block to a fixed time and a fixed place.
- **Track outputs, not hours.** Each night, note three numbers: topics closed, questions attempted, errors logged. If those numbers are healthy, the timetable is working — if not, adjust the blocks, not just the total.
- **Review monthly against mocks.** Your timetable serves your mock scores on the road to **150 out of 300** — not the other way round.

A timetable is a promise you make to a future version of yourself who will be tired and tempted. Keep the promise small enough to keep, structured enough to matter: revision first, deep study at your best hours, questions every single day, and a stop time you respect. Q: How many study blocks should an FMGE daily timetable have?
A: Four kinds of work cover a productive day: a new-study block at your sharpest hours, a daily question-solving block, a short revision block for earlier topics and errors, and genuine rest. The exact hours matter less than including all four.
Q: Can I prepare for FMGE while working or interning?
A: Yes, with a smaller, very consistent timetable — around four focused hours a day split between morning revision and questions, and an evening study-plus-questions block — plus a weekly test on your off day and a longer overall preparation window.
Q: What should I do on the weekly test day in my timetable?
A: Replace the new-study blocks with one full paper sat under exam conditions — 300 questions in two parts — and use the evening light block for detailed analysis of every error.
Q: What if I miss a day of my FMGE timetable?
A: Use the rescue rule: complete just the revision block and one question block, even if that is only two hours. Keeping the chain unbroken matters more than compensating with a marathon the next day.
