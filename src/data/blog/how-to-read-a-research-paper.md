The first time you open a journal article, it feels like a wall: dense columns, unfamiliar statistics, references to methods you have never been taught. Learning **how to read a research paper** is mostly learning what *not* to read — at least not first, and not in the printed order. Papers follow a standard structure called **IMRAD** (Introduction, Methods, Results, And Discussion), and once you know what each section is for, you can move through a paper strategically instead of heroically. This guide gives you a repeatable routine used by experienced readers.

## How to Read a Research Paper: The Three-Pass Method

**First pass — the five-minute skim.** Read the **title** and **abstract**, glance at the tables and figures, and read the last paragraph of the discussion. Your only goal: decide what kind of study this is (trial? cohort? review?), what question it asked, and whether it deserves more of your time. Most papers should stop here — that is not laziness, it is triage.

**Second pass — the structured read.** Now read properly, but in a deliberate order: the **methods** first, then the **results**, and the introduction and discussion last. This feels backwards until you realise why: the introduction and discussion are where authors *argue*; the methods and results are where they *show*. Judge the evidence before you let the authors interpret it for you.

**Third pass — the appraisal.** Revisit the paper asking whether you believe it and whether it applies to your patients or your exam topic. The rest of this article is about what to check in passes two and three.

## Interrogating the Methods

The methods section decides whether the paper deserves belief. Work through these questions:

- **What was the question?** A well-formed study states its population, intervention or exposure, comparison and outcome — the **PICO** elements. Vague questions produce uninterpretable answers.
- **What was the design, and does it fit the question?** A treatment question wants a trial; a prognosis question wants a cohort. Mismatches are a red flag.
- **How were participants selected?** Who was included and excluded, and from where? A study of volunteers from one private clinic may not describe your patients.
- **For trials: was allocation random and concealed, and who was blinded?** These are the bias-killers; if the paper is vague about them, be suspicious — good trials report them clearly, usually following the **CONSORT** checklist.
- **How many participants, and how many were lost?** Large losses to follow-up can quietly destroy a study's validity. Look for a flow diagram showing what happened to everyone who entered.
- **Were outcomes defined in advance and measured the same way in all groups?** Shifting outcomes and unequal measurement are classic sources of bias.
- **Was the study big enough to answer its question?** A trial that recruits too few participants may miss a real effect simply for lack of numbers — a "negative" result from an underpowered study proves very little. Reputable papers state how their sample size was calculated; silence on this point is itself information.

## Reading Results Without Being Fooled

In the results, tables and figures carry the real information; the text narrates it. Check whether the groups were actually comparable at the start (the famous "Table 1"), whether the analysis matches the design — for a trial, whether it was by **intention-to-treat** — and above all, look at the **size of the effect and its confidence interval**, not just whether it is labelled "significant."

Which brings us to the **p-value**. A p-value is the probability of seeing results at least as extreme as those observed *if there were really no effect* — it is a statement about the data under a hypothetical scenario, not the probability that the treatment works. By long-standing **convention**, results with p < 0.05 are called "statistically significant," but 0.05 is a customary threshold, not a law of nature: p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 describe nearly identical evidence. A tiny p-value with a trivially small effect is still a trivial effect, and a "non-significant" result in a small study may simply mean the study was too small to tell. The **confidence interval** is the more informative number — it shows the range of effects compatible with the data, telling you both size and precision at a glance.

## Reading the Discussion With Both Eyes Open

Now — and only now — read what the authors think it all means. Compare their claims against the results *you* just read. Honest discussions acknowledge limitations, compare with previous studies, and stay close to the data. Warning signs include conclusions stronger than the results, emphasis on a favourable subgroup when the main result was negative, and silence about obvious limitations. End by asking the applicability question: **were these patients like the ones I will treat, in a setting like mine?** If not, the paper may be valid and still not change your practice.

Fifteen focused minutes with this routine will extract more from a paper than an hour of cover-to-cover reading. Q: What does IMRAD stand for?
A: Introduction, Methods, Results, And Discussion — the standard structure of a research paper. Knowing what each section is for lets you read strategically: methods and results first, the authors' interpretation last.
Q: What is a p-value, in simple words?
A: The probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed if there were truly no effect. It is not the probability that a treatment works. The p < 0.05 threshold for "significance" is a long-standing convention, not a natural law.
Q: Why should I read the methods before the discussion?
A: Because the discussion is where authors argue for their interpretation. Reading methods and results first lets you judge the evidence yourself, so you can compare the authors' conclusions against what the data actually show.
Q: What should I check first in the results of a trial?
A: Whether the groups were comparable at baseline, whether the analysis followed the design (for trials, intention-to-treat), and the size of the effect with its confidence interval — rather than only whether the result is labelled significant.
