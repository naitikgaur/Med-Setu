The key to getting **immunotherapy cancer explained** right is one shift in thinking: most cancer drugs attack the tumour, but immunotherapy treats the patient's immune system instead, releasing it to attack the tumour itself. For decades, oncologists watched a frustrating paradox — the immune system clearly could recognise cancer, because rare patients' tumours shrank spontaneously, yet most cancers grew unchecked despite being surrounded by immune cells. The explanation turned out to be that tumours actively switch the immune system off. Immunotherapy switches it back on, and for some cancers — melanoma, lung cancer, Hodgkin lymphoma and others — it has changed outcomes that chemotherapy could not.

## Immunotherapy Cancer Explained: Why Tumours Survive Your Immune System

A healthy immune response has both accelerators and brakes. The brakes — called **immune checkpoints** — exist to protect you: after T cells are activated to kill infected cells, checkpoint signals switch them down so they do not destroy healthy tissue and cause autoimmunity. This is normal, essential physiology.

Cancer exploits it. Many tumours display the checkpoint ligand **PD-L1** on their surface. When PD-L1 binds the **PD-1** receptor on a T cell, the T cell receives a "stand down" signal and leaves the tumour alone — the cancer has, in effect, stolen the body's own off-switch and pointed it at the immune response. A second checkpoint, **CTLA-4**, acts earlier, during T-cell activation in lymph nodes. Tumours also hide by reducing antigen display, recruiting suppressive regulatory T cells, and creating a hostile local environment. Immunotherapy is the set of strategies that defeat this camouflage.

## Checkpoint Inhibitors: Releasing the Brakes

**Checkpoint inhibitors** are monoclonal antibodies that block these off-switches. Anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 antibodies prevent the tumour's stand-down signal from reaching the T cell; anti-CTLA-4 antibodies remove an earlier brake so more T cells get activated in the first place. The T cells then do what they were already capable of doing — infiltrate the tumour and kill malignant cells.

Features that make this class exam-worthy and clinically distinctive:

- **Durability.** Because treatment creates immunological memory, some responders stay in remission for years after treatment stops — a pattern rarely seen with chemotherapy.
- **Biomarkers matter.** Tumours with high PD-L1 expression, or with defective DNA mismatch repair (high microsatellite instability), tend to respond better, because they carry more abnormal antigens for T cells to recognise.
- **Not every cancer responds.** Tumours with few mutations and poor immune infiltration — sometimes called immunologically "cold" tumours, such as most pancreatic cancers — often resist checkpoint blockade. Turning cold tumours hot is a major research frontier, and claims here are still evolving.

## CAR-T Cells: Engineering the Soldier Instead

**CAR-T cell therapy** takes the opposite approach: rather than releasing existing T cells, it manufactures better ones. The concept, in sequence:

- T cells are collected from the patient's blood.
- In a laboratory, they are genetically engineered to express a **chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)** — a synthetic receptor whose outer part recognises a chosen tumour antigen (classically **CD19** on B-cell cancers) and whose inner part powerfully activates the T cell.
- The engineered cells are expanded and infused back into the patient, where they hunt antigen-bearing cells directly, without needing the tumour to present antigen normally.

CAR-T therapy has produced striking remissions in some relapsed B-cell leukaemias and lymphomas where other options had failed. It is established therapy for specific blood cancers; its role in solid tumours is still limited by antigen choice, trafficking into tumours, and toxicity.

## The Wider Immunotherapy Family

Checkpoint inhibitors and CAR-T cells get the attention, but they sit within a broader family worth mapping. **Monoclonal antibodies** against tumour antigens — rituximab against CD20 in B-cell lymphomas is the classic example — mark cancer cells for destruction and predate the checkpoint era. **Cytokine therapy** with high-dose interleukin-2 was an early, toxic proof that stimulating immunity could shrink some cancers. **Cancer vaccines** aim to teach the immune system new tumour antigens rather than release existing responses; therapeutic vaccines have had limited success so far, though personalised mRNA-based approaches are an active research area. **Oncolytic viruses** — engineered to infect and burst tumour cells, releasing antigens in the process — are approved for a narrow niche in melanoma. Knowing this map prevents a common exam error: immunotherapy is a strategy, not a single drug class, and each branch has its own evidence base and maturity.

## The Price of Releasing the Brakes: Immune Toxicity

Checkpoint inhibitors cause a side-effect pattern unlike chemotherapy's. With the brakes off, T cells can attack healthy organs, producing **immune-related adverse events**: colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, thyroiditis and hypophysitis, dermatitis, and rarely myocarditis. These are autoimmune diseases caused by treatment, and they are managed by pausing therapy and giving corticosteroids or other immunosuppression — the exact opposite of chemotherapy toxicity management. CAR-T therapy has its own signature risks: **cytokine release syndrome**, a potentially severe systemic inflammatory response, and neurological toxicity. Any student who can explain why a cancer drug causes colitis has understood the mechanism properly.

## What Immunotherapy Cannot Do

It is not a universal cure, and honest teaching should say so. A majority of patients with common solid tumours do not achieve lasting responses; treatment is expensive; biomarkers are imperfect; and resistance can develop. The established wins are real but specific — particular cancers, particular stages, particular biomarker profiles. That specificity is exactly what exams test.

Q: How do checkpoint inhibitors work?
A: They are antibodies that block inhibitory signals such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, which tumours use to switch T cells off. Blocking them restores the T cells' ability to kill cancer cells.
Q: What is CAR-T cell therapy?
A: A patient's own T cells are removed, engineered in a laboratory to express a synthetic receptor targeting a tumour antigen such as CD19, then infused back to hunt cancer cells.
Q: Why do checkpoint inhibitors cause colitis and thyroiditis?
A: Because releasing immune checkpoints can unleash T cells against healthy tissue too. These immune-related adverse events are autoimmune in nature and are treated with immunosuppression.
Q: Do all cancers respond to immunotherapy?
A: No. Cancers with many mutations and good immune infiltration respond best. Immunologically "cold" tumours, such as most pancreatic cancers, often resist checkpoint blockade.
