**MBBS in Russia** has drawn Indian students for decades: Russia has a long tradition of medical education, large established universities, and English-medium programmes that admit many international students each year. It remains one of the most considered destinations when government seats at home don't work out. But Russia is also a choice that rewards honest preparation — the climate is demanding, the local language matters more than brochures admit, and your degree only becomes an Indian licence after you clear the **FMGE**. Here is the full picture, without the sales layer.

## MBBS in Russia: Programme Structure

Medical programmes in Russia for international students generally run for **about 6 years**, structured along a specialist-degree pattern rather than the Indian MBBS semester layout:

- **Early years** focus on basic sciences — anatomy, biochemistry, physiology, histology — with increasing clinical subjects later.
- **Clinical years** move into teaching hospitals, where patient interaction becomes central.
- **Internship components** are built into the programme structure in several universities; confirm exactly how your chosen university organises its final phase, because the NMC's FMGL framework requires a **12-month internship at the same institution** (more on this below).
- **English-medium tracks** exist at many universities, but the depth of English teaching can vary by institution and by year of study — a point to verify with current students, not just admission letters.

Before anything else, remember the home rule: **qualifying NEET-UG is compulsory** for an Indian student who wants to practise in India after studying abroad.

## The English-Medium Question and the Russian Language

This is the most misunderstood part of studying in Russia. Your lectures may be in English, but your life — and much of your clinical learning — will not be:

- **Patients speak Russian.** During clinical rotations, taking a history and understanding a patient's complaints requires Russian. Universities teach Russian as a subject, and students who take it seriously get far more from their clinical years.
- **Daily life runs in Russian** — shops, transport, landlords, paperwork. Basic fluency within the first year makes everything easier.
- **Teaching quality varies.** Some departments teach international batches excellently in English; in others, students rely more on self-study with standard textbooks. Both kinds of students graduate — the difference shows up at FMGE time.

Treat Russian as a clinical skill, not a formality, and treat standard English textbooks as your academic anchor from the first semester.

## Climate and Student Life

Russian winters are severe by Indian standards — long, dark and far below zero in most university cities. Students adapt, but go prepared:

- **Budget attention for winter clothing** and choose accommodation with reliable heating; most Indian students live in university hostels, at least initially.
- **Indian food** is available in larger student communities, and self-cooking is common; hostel kitchens become a social centre for Indian batches.
- **Community** — Russia hosts large Indian student populations, which helps with everything from festivals to shared FMGE preparation.
- **Travel and currency** — the total cost of a Russian degree depends on the city, your housing and food choices, travel home, and exchange-rate movement. Costs vary widely between universities and cities, so verify current figures directly from the university and official sources rather than trusting any quoted "total package".

## The FMGL Rules and the FMGE Reality

Your Russian degree must fit the **NMC FMGL Regulations 2021** to be recognised in India: in essence, a course of at least 54 months, English as the medium, clinical training, and a 12-month internship at the same foreign institution — then **FMGE**, then a 12-month internship (CRMI) in India. Rules and related court proceedings evolve, so verify the current NMC text yourself before enrolling anywhere.

FMGE itself is set by **NBEMS**: **300 MCQs, two parts of 150, no negative marking, pass mark 150**. Historically only a minority of candidates pass in a session, and Russian graduates are no exception to that statistic — the gap is rarely knowledge of medicine alone; it is the mismatch between how you were taught and what the Indian syllabus emphasises. Students who study standard textbooks, keep the Indian syllabus alongside, and practise questions throughout the course close that gap steadily. Start building the habit with a subject-wise [question bank](/qbank/) and graduate to full timed [previous-year papers](/papers/) as you progress.

## Questions to Ask Before You Choose a Russian University

Because quality varies between institutions, your admission decision should rest on answers to specific questions — asked of the university and, separately, of students already there:

- **English teaching, year by year** — is every subject in your programme taught in English through the clinical years, or does teaching shift towards Russian later? Senior students answer this most honestly.
- **Russian language training** — how many hours of Russian does the curriculum include, and is it aimed at clinical communication or only everyday conversation?
- **Hospital affiliations** — which hospitals will your batch rotate through, and what is the patient load like in those departments?
- **Batch size and faculty** — how many international students sit in one lecture group, and who actually teaches them?
- **The internship year** — how exactly is the final internship organised, and will the university document it in the form Indian regulators expect?
- **Winter living** — what are hostel heating, room sharing and food arrangements like in the coldest months?

Write the answers down and compare universities on paper. A university that answers clearly is already telling you something good about itself; one that dodges these questions is telling you something too.

Preparing for FMGE from day one is what separates a smooth return from a stressful one: anchor every subject to the Indian syllabus while you study it in Russia, revise with questions weekly, and use free full-length practice like the MedSetu [grand test series](/tests/) so the 300-question format feels routine long before you sit the real exam.
