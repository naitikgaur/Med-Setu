Patients rarely judge a doctor by diagnostic brilliance they cannot see; they judge by how they were spoken to. Strong **doctor patient communication skills** are therefore not a soft extra — they change outcomes. Patients who feel heard give fuller histories, adhere better to treatment, complain less and return sooner when something is wrong. Students often assume communication is personality: you either have it or you do not. It is not. It is a set of learnable, structured behaviours, taught in clinical-methods courses worldwide, and this article breaks them into a consultation you can practise.

## A Structure for the Consultation: Calgary–Cambridge

The **Calgary–Cambridge framework** is the established teaching model used in medical schools internationally. It divides the consultation into five stages:

- **Initiating the session:** greet the patient, introduce yourself, confirm identity, and establish why they have come — then let them finish their opening statement without interruption. Studies of consultations show patients are often interrupted within the first half-minute; resist it, because the uninterrupted opening usually lasts only a minute or two and reveals the real agenda.
- **Gathering information:** combine your biomedical questions with the patient's perspective — their **ideas, concerns and expectations** (ICE). "What were you hoping I could do today?" uncovers the fear behind the symptom.
- **Physical examination:** explained, consented and narrated as you go.
- **Explanation and planning:** described in detail below.
- **Closing the session:** summarise, agree the plan and safety-net — "if the fever crosses this point or the pain changes like this, come back or call."

Running this structure visibly — signposting with phrases like "now I'd like to examine you, then we'll discuss what it means" — makes a short consultation feel thorough instead of rushed.

## Doctor Patient Communication Skills in Practice: Listening and Empathy

Listening is a technique, not a pause between your questions. Practise its components deliberately:

- **Open questions first:** "Tell me about the pain" before "Is the pain sharp?" Funnel from open to closed as the consultation proceeds.
- **Active listening signals:** eye contact, nodding, and short encouragers; put the pen and screen down for the first minute.
- **Reflection and summarising:** repeat the patient's key words back — "the dizziness frightens you most" — to confirm understanding and invite correction.
- **Naming emotion:** empathy is mostly acknowledgement. "I can see this has been a worrying few weeks" costs five seconds and changes the temperature of the room.
- **Silence:** after an emotional statement or bad news, wait. The urge to fill silence with premature reassurance ("don't worry, everything will be fine") is strong and usually wrong.

Also attend to the non-verbal channel: sit down at the patient's level, face them, and match your expression to the content. A cheerful face delivering a serious result reads as indifference. Cultural sensitivity matters too — forms of address, family involvement in decisions, language and health beliefs differ across India's patients, and asking respectfully ("who would you like present while we discuss this?") is always safer than assuming.

## Explaining Clearly and Deciding Together

Most misunderstanding is jargon, not intelligence. Translate relentlessly: not "your haemoglobin is low" but "the oxygen-carrying part of your blood is lower than normal — that is why you feel tired and breathless". Give information in **small chunks and check understanding** as you go (the chunk-and-check method), use simple drawings for anatomy, and invite the patient to explain the plan back in their own words — the **teach-back** method — framed as your test, not theirs: "I want to check I explained that well; how will you take this medicine at home?"

Then decide **with** the patient. Shared decision-making means presenting reasonable options with their benefits, burdens and uncertainties, asking what matters most to the patient, and agreeing a plan they can actually follow — a cheaper medicine taken daily beats a superior one abandoned in a week. Document the discussion, especially where a patient declines a recommended test or treatment after understanding the consequences. ## Breaking Bad News

Sooner or later you will watch a senior — and then be the doctor — telling someone a diagnosis is cancer, a pregnancy is lost, or a relative has died. A widely used structure is **SPIKES**: **S**etting (privacy, seated, enough time, right people present), **P**erception (ask what the patient already understands), **I**nvitation (ask how much detail they want), **K**nowledge (give the news plainly, in small pieces, after a warning shot — "I'm afraid the result is not what we hoped"), **E**mpathy (respond to the emotion, allow silence and tears), and **S**trategy and summary (agree the next steps before they leave). Never give bad news standing in a corridor, never hide behind euphemisms the patient cannot decode — "growth" when you mean cancer helps no one — and never leave without a concrete next step and a point of contact.

## Difficult Moments

Angry patients, relatives demanding answers, and consultations through an interpreter are part of real practice. The transferable principles: listen to the complaint fully before defending anything, acknowledge the feeling ("you've waited four hours and you're in pain — that's frustrating"), separate the person from the problem, explain what you **can** do, and involve seniors early. With an interpreter, speak to the patient, not the interpreter, in short sentences, and check understanding at the end. And when you make a mistake or a delay occurs, honest explanation with an apology for the experience is both ethical practice and, consistently, what patients say they wanted most.

Q: What is the Calgary-Cambridge framework?
A: An established teaching model that structures a consultation into five stages: initiating the session, gathering information, physical examination, explanation and planning, and closing the session.
Q: What does ICE mean in a consultation?
A: The patient's Ideas, Concerns and Expectations — what they think is wrong, what they fear, and what they hope the doctor will do — explored alongside the medical history.
Q: What is the teach-back method?
A: After explaining, you ask the patient to repeat the plan in their own words, framed as a check on your explanation, to confirm real understanding.
Q: What does SPIKES stand for?
A: Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Empathy, and Strategy and summary — a widely used structure for breaking bad news.
