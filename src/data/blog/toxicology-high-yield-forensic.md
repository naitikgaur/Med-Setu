Toxicology is one of the most scoring areas in the paper, because **toxicology fmge forensic medicine** questions are usually built on a single identifying feature, a named antidote, or a medico-legal duty. The examiner rarely asks for long management protocols. Instead, you are given a farmer with pinpoint pupils, a child who has swallowed kerosene, or a patient with blue-green vomitus, and asked to name the poison or the correct next step. If you revise poisons as feature-and-antidote pairs, these questions become quick marks in a 300-question paper where every correct answer counts and there is no negative marking.

## Toxicology FMGE Forensic Medicine: How the Exam Tests Poisons

Most questions follow three predictable patterns. The first is **recognition from a clinical vignette**: a distinctive sign such as garlic odour, cherry-red blood, or hot dry skin points to one poison. The second is **antidote matching**: name the poison and pick its specific antidote from options that usually include related but wrong drugs. The third is **contraindication and duty**: what must not be done, such as gastric lavage in corrosive poisoning, or what the doctor is legally bound to do in a medico-legal case.

A practical way to revise is to group poisons rather than read them alphabetically:

- **Poisons with pinpoint pupils:** organophosphates and opioids — separated by secretions, bowel sounds and response to the antidote
- **Poisons with dilated pupils:** dhatura and other anticholinergic agents
- **Poisons damaging a single target organ:** paracetamol and the liver, methanol and the eye, copper sulphate and the blood
- **Poisons where the stomach must be left alone:** corrosives and kerosene

Working through the [question bank](/qbank/) by topic after reading each group helps fix these contrasts, because the distractors in real questions are almost always the neighbouring poison in the same group.

## Classic Plant, Chemical and Household Poisons

**Organophosphate poisoning** is the single most tested poison in this section. These insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase, so acetylcholine accumulates at muscarinic, nicotinic and central sites. The classic picture is **miosis**, salivation, lacrimation, urination, defaecation, bronchial secretions with wheeze, bradycardia, muscle fasciculations and weakness, confusion and convulsions. A **garlic odour** of the breath is a favourite clue. The specific antidotes are **atropine**, which blocks the muscarinic effects, and **pralidoxime**, an oxime that reactivates the enzyme if given before ageing occurs. Carbamate poisoning looks similar but is shorter acting, and oximes are generally not used for it.

**Dhatura** poisoning is the mirror image. Its alkaloids block muscarinic receptors, producing the anticholinergic syndrome: **dilated pupils**, hot dry flushed skin, dry mouth, urinary retention, tachycardia, hyperthermia and delirium with muttering and picking at imaginary objects — often remembered as blind, hot, dry, red and mad. **Physostigmine** is the named antidote for severe anticholinergic delirium.

**Copper sulphate** poisoning is recognised by **blue-green vomitus**, abdominal pain, haemolysis, jaundice, and renal damage. It is a metallic irritant poison, and chelating agents such as **penicillamine** are named in textbooks as part of management. **Carbon monoxide** deserves a place in every revision list: it binds haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen, produces **cherry-red** skin and blood, headache, giddiness and coma, and the treatment of choice is **high-flow oxygen**.

## Drug Overdoses and Their Antidotes

Drug overdose questions are pure antidote recall, and they appear again and again.

- **Opioids:** the triad of **coma, pinpoint pupils and respiratory depression**. The antidote is **naloxone**. A history of injection marks or dependence often completes the vignette.
- **Paracetamol:** early symptoms are deceptively mild — nausea and vomiting — followed after a day or two by **centrilobular hepatic necrosis**, the most important cause of acute liver failure in overdose. The antidote is **N-acetylcysteine**, which replenishes glutathione.
- **Benzodiazepines:** drowsiness and respiratory depression, milder than opioids. The antagonist is **flumazenil**, though textbooks caution against its routine use because it can precipitate seizures in dependent patients.
- **Methanol:** covered below, but note that its antidotes, **fomepizole** and ethanol, work by blocking the enzyme that creates its toxic metabolites.

The examiner's favourite trick is to offer the antidote of a similar poison as a distractor — naloxone against flumazenil, or atropine against physostigmine. Revise them as opposing pairs and the confusion disappears. ## Snake Bite, Methanol and Corrosives

**Snake bite** questions divide neatly by snake family. **Neurotoxic** bites — cobra and krait — cause ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, bulbar weakness and respiratory paralysis, often with minimal local signs; a krait bite at night with abdominal pain is a classic stem. **Haemotoxic** bites — the vipers — cause local swelling, blistering, spontaneous bleeding, incoagulable blood and renal failure. The specific treatment for systemic envenomation is **anti-snake venom**, along with supportive care. Incisions, tight tourniquets and sucking the wound are condemned practices that appear as wrong options.

**Methanol** is metabolised to formic acid, which damages the retina and produces a severe metabolic acidosis. The patient complains of visual blurring described as a **snowfield vision**, which can progress to permanent blindness. Treatment rests on **fomepizole** (or ethanol where fomepizole is unavailable), correction of acidosis, and haemodialysis in severe cases.

**Corrosives** — strong acids and alkalis — burn the gastrointestinal tract on contact. Alkalis tend to injure the oesophagus more, acids the stomach, with risk of perforation and later stricture. Two rules are tested relentlessly: **emesis is contraindicated** because the corrosive burns again on the way up, and **gastric lavage is contraindicated** because the tube can perforate the damaged wall. The same no-lavage rule applies to **kerosene**, where the danger is aspiration and chemical pneumonitis rather than systemic absorption.

## Medico-Legal Duties of a Doctor in Poisoning Cases

Forensic medicine marks are also earned outside the poison list. In India, a suspected poisoning case is a **medico-legal case**, and the treating doctor has clear duties:

- **Treat first.** Saving life takes priority over paperwork; legal formalities must never delay emergency care.
- **Inform the police.** Suspected homicidal or otherwise medico-legal poisoning must be intimated to the police, and the doctor should record the details of the case carefully and contemporaneously.
- **Preserve evidence.** Vomit, gastric lavage fluid, blood, urine, and any suspected container or leftover material should be collected, sealed, labelled and handed over with a proper chain of custody, so the samples remain admissible.
- **Do not wash away evidence.** The stomach wash and soiled clothing are evidence, not waste, until the police and forensic process are satisfied.
- **Maintain confidentiality with limits.** In a medico-legal case, information relevant to the investigation must be shared with the authorities despite the usual duty of confidence.

The **dying declaration** is the other high-yield topic. It is a statement made by a person about the cause of their death, admissible in court when the maker dies. Ideally a magistrate records it. If the patient's condition does not allow waiting, the doctor may record it, after certifying that the patient is **compos mentis** — conscious, oriented and fit to make a statement. It should be taken in the patient's own words, in the presence of witnesses, without leading questions or prompting, and signed and forwarded promptly. Questions usually test exactly these points: who should record it, the doctor's certification of fitness, and the rule that no oath is administered.

Q: What is the antidote for organophosphate poisoning?
A: Atropine is the main antidote for the muscarinic effects, and pralidoxime, an oxime, is used to reactivate acetylcholinesterase before ageing occurs.
Q: Why is gastric lavage contraindicated in corrosive poisoning?
A: Because the corrosive has already damaged and weakened the oesophageal and stomach wall, and passing a tube or inducing vomiting risks perforation and further burns.
Q: What are the classic signs of opioid poisoning?
A: The classic triad is coma, pinpoint pupils and respiratory depression. The specific antidote is naloxone.
Q: What must a doctor do in a suspected medico-legal poisoning case in India?
A: Treat the patient first, inform the police, preserve samples such as vomit, lavage fluid, blood and urine with proper sealing and labelling, and document the case carefully.
