Every medical student knows the feeling: you studied renal physiology thoroughly in March, and by June it feels like someone else read that textbook. The **forgetting curve study** — some of the oldest experimental work in psychology — says this is not a personal failure. Forgetting follows a predictable pattern: memory for newly learned material drops steeply at first, then levels off, and most of the loss happens in the first hours and days after learning. The encouraging half of the same research is that the curve is not fixed. Each well-timed review makes the next decline slower.

## What the Forgetting Curve Study Actually Found

In the 1880s, Hermann Ebbinghaus tested his own memory using lists of nonsense syllables — material chosen precisely because it had no meaning to lean on. He measured how much re-learning effort ("savings") remained at different intervals after first learning. The pattern he found has been replicated many times since, including in a well-known 2015 replication by Murre and Dros that closely matched his original curve.

Three findings matter for a medical student:

- **Decay is fastest early.** The drop in the first day is far steeper than the drop across the following week. Material you do not touch again within 24–48 hours is largely gone.
- **Reviewing resets and flattens the curve.** After each successful review, forgetting restarts from a higher point and proceeds more slowly. This is the basis of **distributed practice** — spreading study of a topic across several sessions beats cramming the same total time into one.
- **Meaningful material is forgotten more slowly than nonsense syllables.** Ebbinghaus deliberately used meaningless material. Connected, understood clinical knowledge decays more slowly — but it still decays, and the shape of the curve is the same.

A caution, because this topic attracts exaggeration: precise claims like "you forget exactly 70% in 24 hours" come from those nonsense-syllable experiments and should not be quoted as your personal forgetting rate for medicine. The *pattern* — steep early loss, flattened by review — is the robust, useful part.

## Why 19 Subjects Make the Curve Worse

FMGE covers 19 subjects and 300 questions, and most students study in long blocks: three weeks of Anatomy, then three weeks of Physiology. Blocked study feels thorough, but it collides head-on with the forgetting curve. By the time you finish your third subject, the first has had six untouched weeks to decay.

The second problem is interference. Similar material studied close together — drug names in Pharmacology, organisms in Microbiology, lesions in Pathology — blurs at the edges, and questions in the exam are designed to probe exactly those edges. The fix is not more total hours; it is **contact frequency**. A subject you touch briefly every week decays far less than a subject you mastered once and abandoned.

## Beating the Curve: A Schedule That Works

You do not need an app or a complicated system. You need three habits built around how memory actually behaves.

- **Review within a day.** After studying a topic, spend ten minutes the next day recalling it — closed book, writing or saying what you remember. This single early review does more than any other.
- **Use expanding intervals.** Review again after a few days, then a week, then two to three weeks. If recall fails at any point, shorten the next interval for that topic. Let your performance, not the calendar alone, set the spacing.
- **Review by testing, not re-reading.** The **testing effect** is one of the most reliable findings in learning research: retrieving an answer strengthens memory more than seeing the answer again. Reviews of the evidence, such as Dunlosky and colleagues' 2013 analysis of study techniques, rate practice testing and distributed practice as the two highest-utility strategies — and re-reading and highlighting among the lowest.
- **Interleave subjects.** Mix two or three subjects in a week rather than finishing one completely. Short, repeated contact across subjects is exactly what a 19-subject syllabus demands.

To make this concrete, take one topic — say, heart failure, studied properly on Monday. On Tuesday, spend ten minutes writing everything you remember about it on a blank page, then check and repair. On Friday, do it again, this time as fifteen questions. The following week, one mixed set that includes heart failure among older topics; the week after, it appears inside a full paper. Four contacts, perhaps ninety minutes in total, spread across a month — and the topic is still alive at the end of it, whereas a single three-hour Sunday session would have left it to decay untouched for weeks. Multiply that pattern across your subjects and you have the entire system: nothing exotic, just every topic revisited shortly before you would have lost it.

## Turning This Into an FMGE Routine

A practical weekly shape: study new material in the mornings, and reserve a fixed daily slot — even 45 minutes — purely for scheduled reviews of older topics, done as questions rather than reading. Sit [previous-year papers](/papers/) regularly, because they sample all 19 subjects at once and show you honestly which older subjects have decayed. Between full papers, short sets from a [question bank](/qbank/) aimed at the subjects you studied two and six weeks ago are the cheapest curve-flattening tool you have.

Expect the first review of an old topic to feel bad. That feeling is information: it tells you the interval was too long, and the next one should be shorter. Students who avoid old topics because reviewing them feels discouraging get the worst of the curve; students who schedule the discomfort flatten it.

Q: How quickly do we forget what we study?
A: Forgetting is fastest in the first hours and days after learning, then slows down. Exact percentages quoted online come from Ebbinghaus's nonsense-syllable experiments and don't transfer precisely to meaningful medical material — but the steep-early-drop pattern is consistent across replications.
Q: What is the best interval for revising a topic?
A: A reliable pattern is: review the next day, then after a few days, then after one to two weeks, expanding the gap while recall stays strong. If you fail to recall, shorten the next interval for that topic.
Q: Is re-reading my notes a good way to review?
A: It is one of the weakest. Research comparing study techniques rates practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice as high-utility, and re-reading and highlighting as low-utility. Test yourself first; read only to fill the gaps testing reveals.
Q: Does cramming ever work against the forgetting curve?
A: Cramming can carry you through an exam held the next day, but the material decays almost as fast afterwards. For FMGE, where early subjects are examined months after you study them, spaced review is not optional — it is the whole game.
