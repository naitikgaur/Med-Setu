Every FMGE session, a share of candidates clear the exam in their first attempt while others — often with similar medical knowledge — return for a second or third. The difference is rarely intelligence. It is a set of behaviours, visible again and again in candidates who finish the job in one go. This **fmge first attempt strategy** lays them out plainly, so you can copy the behaviours rather than admire them. The context is worth remembering: NBEMS publishes results each session, and historically a minority of candidates pass — which means the default outcome is failure, and first-attempt success comes from doing specific things differently.

## The FMGE first attempt strategy: start with the target — 150, engineered

First-attempt clearers treat FMGE as what it is — a **pass-mark exam**. The paper is **300 MCQs, +1 per correct answer, no negative marking, pass at 150**. There is no rank to chase and no cutoff race. That changes planning completely: the goal is not "know everything" but "build a reliable 170–190 in mocks so exam-day variation cannot pull you under 150". Every decision — subject order, hours, mock frequency — is judged against that one number.

## They study by weightage from day one

The official distribution is public, and first-attempt candidates actually use it:

- **Medicine 33, Surgery 32, OBG 30, Community Medicine 30** — 125 questions, prepared first and deepest
- **Anatomy, Physiology, Biochemistry 17 each; Paediatrics, Ophthalmology, ENT 15 each**
- **Pathology, Microbiology, Pharmacology 13 each; Forensic Medicine 10; Radiology 10**
- **Psychiatry, Dermatology, Anaesthesia, Orthopaedics 5 each** — small syllabi, collected deliberately as cheap marks

Nobody clears FMGE by accident while ignoring where the questions come from. Weightage-first planning means that even an incomplete preparation — and every preparation feels incomplete — is incomplete in the low-yield corners, not in Medicine.

## They solve questions from the first week

The most common repeater pattern is months of pure reading followed by a shocked first mock test. First-attempt candidates invert it: questions begin almost immediately, topic by topic, in the [question bank](/qbank/) alongside reading. Solving questions does three jobs at once — it reveals what examiners actually ask, it exposes weak topics while there is still time to fix them, and it builds the recognition speed a 300-question paper demands. By exam day, they have answered thousands of questions; the format holds no surprises.

## They take full mocks early — and analyse them properly

Most candidates delay full papers until they feel "ready", which usually means the final fortnight. First-attempt candidates sit a full paper from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) within the first month, knowing the score will be poor, because an early baseline is information, not judgement. From there, mocks run on a rhythm — monthly, then fortnightly, then weekly — using a structured [grand test series](/tests/) in the later phase.

The real differentiator is the analysis. After every test, each wrong answer is classified:

- **Knowledge gap** — I did not know this → goes into the revision list
- **Silly mistake** — I knew this and still missed → a speed/attention problem, fixed by pacing practice
- **Failed guess** — a fair attempt under no negative marking → ignored, by design

Two candidates can score identically in a mock and be in completely different positions, depending on which of these buckets their errors fall into. First-attempt candidates know their buckets.

## They revise in cycles, not in one heroic pass

Material read once in month one is gone by month five unless it is revisited. Candidates who clear in one attempt schedule revision as they go: a topic is revised a few days after first study, again after a few weeks, and again in the final month. Their notes get shorter each cycle — full notes become one-page summaries become fact lists. In the last month they are revising *their own distilled material*, not re-opening textbooks, and they protect that final month from new content entirely.

## They keep the operation boring

First-attempt candidates are unspectacular about logistics, and it pays. They know their eligibility documents are in order well before the application window matters, they read the NBEMS information bulletin themselves instead of relying on forwarded summaries, and they treat sleep in the final fortnight as part of preparation rather than a reward after it. None of this earns a single mark directly — but a missing document, a misunderstood instruction or a wrecked final week can erase marks that months of study built. The candidates who clear in one go rarely have a dramatic story to tell. Their preparation looks, from outside, almost dull: steady hours, regular tests, honest logs. That dullness is the strategy.

## They manage the exam, not just the syllabus

First-attempt candidates rehearse exam behaviour in every mock: same start time, two parts with a break, a per-block time sense, and the discipline of **attempting all 300 questions** — flagging the doubtful, returning, and making educated attempts on everything left, because a blank answer is the only guaranteed zero in this paper. They also sit [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) as they finish each subject, so weaknesses are caught at subject level months before the full mocks would reveal them.

None of this requires genius, expensive material or fourteen-hour days. It requires starting early enough, planning by weightage, testing from the beginning, analysing honestly and revising in cycles. Q: What is the best strategy to clear FMGE in the first attempt?
A: Plan backwards from the pass mark of 150 out of 300: study subjects in weightage order, solve questions from the first week, take full mock tests early and regularly, analyse every error, and revise in repeated cycles instead of one final pass.
Q: When should I take my first FMGE mock test?
A: Within the first month of preparation. An early mock is a baseline measurement that shows your weak subjects while there is still time to fix them — not a verdict on your readiness.
Q: Do toppers study all 19 subjects equally for FMGE?
A: No. They weight their effort by the official distribution — Medicine, Surgery, OBG and Community Medicine first — while still covering small subjects like Psychiatry and Dermatology, whose compact syllabi offer cheap marks.
Q: How many questions should I attempt in the FMGE exam?
A: All 300. FMGE has no negative marking, so an attempted question can only gain or hold marks, while a blank answer is a guaranteed zero.
