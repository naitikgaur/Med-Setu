**Radiology for FMGE** carries **10 questions out of 300**, and it is the only subject officially split into two halves: **Radiodiagnosis 5 questions and Radiotherapy 5 questions** in the NBEMS blueprint. The paper has two parts of 150 MCQs, no negative marking, and a pass mark of 150. But the 10-question figure understates radiology's real presence. Imaging appears inside Medicine, Surgery, Orthopaedics, Paediatrics and OBG questions constantly — a chest X-ray in a medicine stem, a CT in a surgical emergency. Studying radiology well therefore pays twice: once in its own 10 questions, and again every time an image decides a question in another subject.

## Radiology for FMGE: radiodiagnosis and the classic signs

Radiodiagnosis questions are recognition questions. Each pairs an image or a described finding with a diagnosis. Build a sign-to-diagnosis list and revise it as a list:

- **Chest imaging** — lobar consolidation, pleural effusion with its meniscus, pneumothorax, the bat-wing of pulmonary oedema, miliary mottling
- **Abdominal imaging** — free air under the diaphragm, air-fluid levels in obstruction, the coffee-bean sign, Rigler's signs, and the barium-study appearances of the classic lesions
- **Skeletal imaging** — the fracture patterns of orthopaedics plus tumour signs such as sunburst appearance and onion-peel reaction
- **Neuroimaging** — CT appearances of extradural versus subdural haematoma (biconvex versus crescent-shaped), and the hyperdense middle cerebral artery sign
- **Contrast studies and named signs** — string sign, thumbprinting, apple-core lesion, and other one-line associations examiners reuse

For every sign, attach the *modality*: which investigation shows it, and which investigation is the investigation of choice for that condition. "Investigation of choice" is the most repeated radiology question pattern across all subjects.

## Modalities and their principles

Know the basic physics and safety facts at undergraduate level: how X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound and nuclear medicine differ, their classic contraindications (metallic implants and pacemakers for MRI, contrast caution in renal impairment and allergy), and radiation protection basics — the ALARA principle and the most radiosensitive tissues and life stages. Pregnancy and imaging is a recurring theme that links radiology with OBG.

## Radiotherapy: the other five questions

The Radiotherapy half is smaller and more factual:

- **Principles** — the difference between external beam therapy and brachytherapy, common sources and isotopes, and the idea of fractionation
- **Radiosensitivity** — which tumours are radiosensitive and which organs are dose-limiting, taught as standard lists
- **Common clinical applications** — radiotherapy's role in cancers of the cervix, breast, head and neck and prostate, usually asked at the level of "which modality is preferred" rather than dose details
- **Side effects** — acute versus late effects and the classic long-term risks

Keep this half list-based; it does not need deep reading, but it does need one careful pass, because students who ignore it donate five questions.

## Study method

Radiology is learned by looking. Work through images in sets, naming the finding aloud in one line before reading the options. Use a standard undergraduate radiology text or your final-year notes for the sign lists, and interleave radiology questions with the parent subjects — chest imaging with Medicine, skeletal imaging with Orthopaedics. Short daily image drills beat a single long session, because recognition speed is the skill being tested.

## Investigation of choice: think in pairs

The cleanest way to hold this material is as condition–investigation pairs, because that is the exact shape of the questions. For each major condition in the clinical subjects, note the **first** investigation and the **best or definitive** investigation separately — stems exploit the difference. A suspected perforation starts with an erect chest X-ray; a stable head injury is characterised by CT; soft-tissue and tendon questions point to MRI; the first test in pregnancy is almost always ultrasound because it avoids ionising radiation. When you revise any clinical topic, add its imaging pair to the same page. Over a full revision cycle this builds several hundred pairs without a single separate radiology marathon, and it is precisely the knowledge the Radiodiagnosis five — and the hidden imaging questions elsewhere — are testing.

## Ultrasound, nuclear medicine and interventional basics

Round out the subject with three short blocks. **Ultrasound**: its strengths (gallstones, obstetric imaging, free fluid, guided procedures), the FAST examination in trauma, and Doppler principles in outline. **Nuclear medicine**: the classic scans and their headline uses — bone scan for metastases and occult infection, thyroid scans, V/Q scanning in pulmonary embolism when CT is unsuitable, and PET's role in oncology staging. **Interventional radiology** at recognition level: angioplasty and stenting, embolisation for bleeding, and image-guided drainage and biopsy. Each block is a page at most, and together they cover the questions that fall outside the classic-sign list.

## A realistic study allocation

Radiology does not need a block of its own in your timetable; it needs a habit. Attach twenty minutes of image practice to the end of each clinical study day — chest images after Medicine, skeletal images after Orthopaedics, neuroimaging after Surgery — and give the Radiotherapy lists and radiation-protection facts two short sittings of their own. By the end of a revision cycle you will have accumulated more radiology practice than a single weekend block would give you, and it will be indexed by the conditions it belongs to, which is how the paper will ask for it.
