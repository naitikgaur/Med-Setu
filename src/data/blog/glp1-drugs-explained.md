Few drug classes have moved from pharmacology textbook to public conversation as fast as this one, so getting **GLP-1 drugs explained — semaglutide** and its relatives — properly matters for every medical student. These medicines mimic a natural gut hormone, **glucagon-like peptide-1**, and are established prescription treatments for type 2 diabetes; some are also approved for chronic weight management in defined patient groups. They are genuinely effective drugs and genuinely misused ones. Your job as a future doctor is to understand the physiology, the benefits, and the risks — and to resist both the hype and the dismissal.

## GLP-1 Drugs Explained: Semaglutide Starts With a Gut Hormone

After you eat, cells in the intestine release GLP-1. It is one of the **incretin** hormones, and it coordinates the body's response to incoming glucose in several ways at once:

- **It increases insulin release** from pancreatic beta cells — but only when blood glucose is elevated. This glucose-dependence is the elegant part: the signal strengthens when sugar is high and fades as it falls.
- **It suppresses glucagon**, the hormone that tells the liver to release stored glucose, further reducing hepatic glucose output when it is not needed.
- **It slows gastric emptying**, so food leaves the stomach more gradually and glucose enters the blood more smoothly.
- **It acts on appetite centres in the brain**, increasing satiety — people feel full sooner and think about food less.

Natural GLP-1 survives only a couple of minutes before the enzyme **DPP-4** destroys it. The drugs in this class are engineered versions that resist DPP-4 and last far longer. **Semaglutide** (known to many patients by its brand name Ozempic in its diabetes formulation) is a once-weekly example; others in the class include liraglutide and dulaglutide, and newer agents such as tirzepatide act on both GLP-1 and a second incretin, GIP. All are prescription-only medicines, most given by injection, with an oral semaglutide formulation also available.

## What Benefits Are Established

At the level of established evidence — stated qualitatively, as responsible teaching should — the class lowers blood glucose and HbA1c effectively in type 2 diabetes, produces clinically meaningful weight reduction in people with obesity, and certain agents have shown **cardiovascular outcome benefits** in high-risk patients with type 2 diabetes, which is why guidelines position them prominently for patients with established cardiovascular disease. Because insulin release is glucose-dependent, these drugs carry a **low risk of hypoglycaemia when used alone** — a real advantage over sulfonylureas and insulin, though the risk returns when they are combined with those agents. Anyone quoting you exact percentages or promised kilograms for an individual patient is selling something; individual responses vary widely, and weight typically returns if treatment stops.

## The Risks and Cautions Students Must Know

- **Gastrointestinal effects are the commonest problem** — nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, reflux and constipation, especially while starting and escalating. They are the main reason patients discontinue.
- **Gastroparesis and gut slowing** matter clinically: these drugs are used cautiously, or avoided, in severe gastrointestinal motility disorders, and they matter before surgery and anaesthesia because a "fasted" stomach may not be empty.
- **Pancreatitis** has been reported; new severe abdominal pain on these drugs deserves proper evaluation.
- **Gallbladder disease** risk increases with rapid weight loss by any method, including this one.
- **Lean mass loss.** Weight lost on any powerful appetite suppressant includes some muscle, which is why protein intake and resistance exercise are part of responsible prescribing, especially in older patients.
- **Thyroid tumour warning.** Rodent studies showed medullary thyroid carcinoma with this class; the drugs are contraindicated in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or MEN 2 syndrome, even though a human causal link has not been established.
- **Misuse is a real clinical problem.** Off-label cosmetic use by people without obesity, unregulated online supply, and compounded imitations all create patients you will meet. These are not lifestyle accessories; they are potent endocrine drugs.

## A Useful Contrast: DPP-4 Inhibitors

Examiners love pairing this class with its quieter cousins, the **DPP-4 inhibitors** (such as sitagliptin). Both exploit the incretin pathway, but differently: GLP-1 agonists supply pharmacological levels of a long-lasting GLP-1-like signal, whereas DPP-4 inhibitors merely block the enzyme that destroys the patient's own GLP-1, gently raising natural levels. The consequences follow predictably from the mechanism — DPP-4 inhibitors are oral, weight-neutral and well tolerated but modest in glucose-lowering power, with no established cardiovascular outcome benefit and no meaningful weight loss. If you can explain why blocking a breakdown enzyme cannot match supplying the hormone itself at high, sustained levels, you understand incretin pharmacology rather than merely listing it — and you can also predict why only the agonists, not the inhibitors, slow gastric emptying enough to cause prominent nausea.

## Prescribing Principles, Not Prescriptions

This article deliberately gives no doses, because dosing belongs to guidelines, formularies and the individual patient's assessment — kidney function, other medicines, retinopathy status, pregnancy plans (these drugs are stopped before planned pregnancy) and more. What you should carry into practice is the framework: a GLP-1 agonist is a long-term metabolic therapy for defined indications, started and monitored by a clinician, combined with nutrition and activity, and reviewed like any chronic treatment. If a patient asks you about these medicines, that conversation belongs with their treating doctor, with you explaining mechanism and trade-offs honestly.

Q: What is GLP-1 and what does it do?
A: Glucagon-like peptide-1 is an incretin hormone released from the gut after eating. It boosts glucose-dependent insulin release, suppresses glucagon, slows gastric emptying and increases satiety.
Q: Why do GLP-1 drugs rarely cause hypoglycaemia on their own?
A: Because their insulin-releasing effect is glucose-dependent — it is strong when blood glucose is high and fades as glucose falls. The risk rises when they are combined with insulin or sulfonylureas.
Q: What are the commonest side effects of semaglutide?
A: Gastrointestinal effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, reflux and constipation, especially during initiation and dose escalation. They are the leading cause of discontinuation.
Q: Can anyone take GLP-1 drugs for weight loss?
A: No. They are prescription-only medicines approved for defined groups, with contraindications such as medullary thyroid cancer history or MEN 2. Unsupervised or cosmetic use is unsafe and should be discussed with a doctor.
