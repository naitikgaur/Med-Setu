"How many times to revise before exam day?" is one of the most common questions medical students ask — and one of the most misleading. It assumes the number of passes is what fixes a topic in memory, as if the third reading of a chapter unlocks something the second did not. Memory does not work by counting exposures. A topic is revised enough when you can **retrieve** it — accurately, completely and without the book — and it stays that way until the exam. For an easy topic that might take two passes. For a dense, confusing one it might take six. The count is the output, not the target.

## How Many Times to Revise Before the Exam? The Honest Answer

Here is the truthful version: **revise a topic as many times as it takes for retrieval to become fluent, spaced at widening intervals, with the last pass close to the exam.** No study or textbook establishes a magic number of revisions that guarantees retention for everyone, and any source that promises one — "revise exactly five times and you will never forget" — is selling certainty the science does not offer.

What the science does support is *how* those revisions should look:

- **Spaced, not stacked.** Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve shows memory fading fastest in the first days after learning. Revisions placed at expanding intervals — soon after learning, then days later, then weeks later — flatten that curve far better than the same number of revisions crammed together. Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated distributed practice among the highest-utility study techniques.
- **Retrieval, not re-reading.** The testing effect shows that pulling material from memory strengthens it more than re-exposure. A "revision" in which you only look at your notes barely counts; a revision in which you test yourself and check counts fully.
- **Performance decides.** The stop signal is not a tally — it is two or three consecutive occasions, days apart, on which you can recall the topic and answer questions on it correctly.

## A Practical Pattern That Works for Most Topics

If you want a working default instead of a magic number, use this rhythm and let performance adjust it:

**Pass 1 — within a day or two of first studying.** Short and mostly testing: close the book, write what you remember, fix the gaps. This pass does the heaviest lifting because it catches the topic before the forgetting curve does.

**Pass 2 — a few days later.** Test again, this time including questions. Topics that survive pass 2 confidently can move to a longer interval.

**Pass 3 — after two to three weeks.** By now the review should be fast. Spend the time only on what still fails: the stubborn values, the exceptions, the differentials you still swap.

**Final pass — in the last weeks before the exam.** Compressed notes only: your tables, your mistakes list, the volatile facts. This pass is about freshness, not learning.

Easy topics will graduate after pass 2 or 3. Hard topics — the ones you keep failing — get extra passes inserted wherever they are needed. That is the system working correctly: **revision count follows weakness, not a timetable.**

## Signs a Topic Needs Another Revision

- You recognise the notes but cannot reproduce them with the page covered
- You answer questions on the topic correctly but slowly, or only after eliminating three options
- You confuse it with a neighbouring topic — the classic sign that the two need a comparison table and a joint revision
- Your last attempt at it was more than a few weeks ago and the exam is near

The most reliable test is questions under honest conditions. Working a topic set in the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) or the [question bank](/qbank/) tells you more about whether a topic is "done" than any number of readings: if the marks are there, the revision worked; if they are not, the topic goes back into the queue regardless of how many times you have "revised" it.

## What About the Topics You Revised and Still Forget?

Some forgetting is normal and even useful — a fact that has faded slightly and is then retrieved makes a stronger memory than one reviewed while still fresh. But if a topic collapses completely every time, the problem is usually upstream: it was never understood, only transcribed. Re-study that one topic from the book once, properly, then restart its revision cycle with shorter intervals. Also check the format of your revision: students who switch a failing topic from re-reading to self-testing are often surprised how quickly the same number of passes starts working.

## Count Fluency, Not Passes

So stop counting revisions and start counting **successful retrievals**. A topic you have tested yourself on, correctly, three times across a month is in far better shape than a topic you have read six times in a week. Let your question scores keep the tally.

Q: Is revising a topic three times enough before the exam?
A: For a topic you understood well and can still retrieve fluently, three spaced, test-based revisions may be plenty. For a weak or dense topic, it will not be. Judge by retrieval and question scores, not by the count.
Q: Should all revisions be the same length?
A: No. Revisions should shrink. The first review after learning is the longest; later passes focus only on what still fails, and the final pass uses compressed notes — tables, values and your mistakes list.
Q: What if I revise a topic and still forget it in the test?
A: Usually it was recognised, not retrieved, during revision. Switch that topic to self-testing with feedback, shorten the interval before the next pass, and check whether it was ever properly understood in the first place.
Q: How close to the exam should the last revision be?
A: Every important topic deserves a final light pass in the last few weeks, using compressed notes. The goal of that pass is freshness — bringing already-learned material back to the surface, not learning anything new.
