Of all the **bls cpr basics** a student learns, these are the ones that must work at 3 a.m., under stress, without thinking. Basic Life Support is the immediate response to **cardiac arrest** — the moment the heart stops pumping effectively — and survival is decided in the first few minutes, usually before any senior doctor or specialist team arrives. This article explains the concepts at awareness level: how arrest is recognised, what the chain of survival is, and what high-quality CPR involves. It is **not** a certification and cannot replace one: BLS is a physical skill that must be learnt and assessed hands-on, for example on an AHA (American Heart Association) course or your institution's equivalent, and renewed regularly.

## The Chain of Survival

Survival from cardiac arrest depends on a sequence of actions, each enabling the next — the **chain of survival**:

- **Early recognition and call for help:** spot the collapse, shout for help, and activate the emergency response (in hospital, the arrest call; outside, the local emergency number — 112 in India).
- **Early CPR:** immediate chest compressions keep oxygenated blood moving to the brain and heart.
- **Early defibrillation:** an AED or manual defibrillator shock, delivered fast, is the definitive treatment for the shockable rhythms.
- **Effective advanced life support:** drugs, airway management and skilled team care once the arrest team arrives.
- **Post-resuscitation care:** intensive care, treating the cause, and rehabilitation.

A chain breaks at its weakest link. For a student, the first three links are yours: recognise, call, compress, and get the AED. Everything else in this article serves those actions.

## Recognising Cardiac Arrest and Starting

The recognition rule taught worldwide is simple: a person who is **unresponsive and not breathing normally** is in cardiac arrest until proved otherwise. Occasional gasping (agonal breathing) is **not** normal breathing and must not delay you. The sequence:

- **Check response:** shout and shake the shoulders firmly.
- **Shout for help** and send someone for the AED and to make the emergency call — or make the call yourself on speakerphone if alone.
- **Open the airway** (head tilt, chin lift) and look, listen and feel for breathing for **no more than 10 seconds**.
- If not breathing normally, **start chest compressions immediately**. Trained rescuers also check the carotid pulse, but never for longer than 10 seconds, and lay rescuers are not expected to — uncertainty means start compressions.

Do not waste minutes looking for monitors, blood pressures or a senior before starting. Compressions first; everything else fits around them. These first-response principles connect with the emergency topics tested across the [question bank](/qbank/) and the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/).

## Bls Cpr Basics: High-Quality Chest Compressions

Compression quality is measured, and the standard teaching values for adults are precise:

- **Position:** heel of the hand in the **centre of the chest** (lower half of the sternum), other hand on top, arms straight, shoulders directly over the hands.
- **Rate:** **100–120 compressions per minute**.
- **Depth:** **5–6 cm** in an adult — hard enough to pump, not so deep as to injure unnecessarily.
- **Recoil:** let the chest come fully back up between compressions; leaning on the chest between pushes reduces filling.
- **Continuity:** minimise interruptions. Every pause — for breaths, rhythm checks or swapping rescuers — drops the pressure you have built up. Swap rescuers about every 2 minutes to fight fatigue, with the briefest possible pause.

Rescue breaths are combined with compressions by trained rescuers at a ratio of **30 compressions to 2 breaths** in adults; if you are untrained or unwilling, continuous compression-only CPR is far better than doing nothing. In hospital, a bag-valve-mask with oxygen replaces mouth-to-mouth. The honest truth students should hear early: real compressions are physically hard, tiring within two minutes, and feel nothing like the classroom — another reason hands-on practice on a manikin with feedback is irreplaceable.

## The AED: Let the Machine Guide You

An **automated external defibrillator** is designed for exactly your situation: switch it on and follow its voice prompts. Attach the pads to the bare chest as drawn on them (one below the right clavicle, one on the left side below the armpit), keep compressions going while pads are placed, then stand clear when the machine analyses and when it advises a shock. Resume compressions immediately after the shock or after a "no shock advised" message, and continue until the arrest team takes over or the patient shows clear signs of life. AEDs are safe: the device will not deliver a shock to a rhythm that should not be shocked.

## Your Role as a Student, and Getting Certified

In a real arrest you may be first on scene or the fifth pair of hands. Both matter. Useful student roles include performing compressions in rotation, fetching and operating the AED, timing and scribing (recording what happened and when — invaluable afterwards), bringing the arrest trolley, and guiding the team to the patient. Know where your ward's arrest trolley and AED live **before** the arrest happens; that thirty-second orientation is a genuine life-saving habit.

Then get properly trained. Enrol in a recognised hands-on BLS course — the AHA BLS Provider course is the common standard in Indian medical colleges, and many institutions run equivalent certified programmes — and refresh it as required, because skills decay measurably within months. Q: What is the chain of survival?
A: Early recognition and calling for help, early CPR, early defibrillation, effective advanced life support, and post-resuscitation care — each step enabling the next.
Q: What are the correct compression rate and depth for adults?
A: Standard teaching is 100–120 compressions per minute at a depth of 5–6 cm, with full chest recoil and minimal interruptions.
Q: How do you recognise cardiac arrest?
A: The person is unresponsive and not breathing normally. Occasional gasping (agonal breathing) counts as not breathing normally and should not delay starting CPR.
Q: Can reading about BLS certify me?
A: No. BLS is a hands-on skill; certification requires an assessed practical course such as an AHA BLS Provider course, with regular renewal.
