Every student thinks they know how to make notes for medical exams: read the chapter, copy the important points, revise them before the test. In practice, most notes fail at the final step. They are too long to reopen, too similar to the textbook to add anything, and written in a way that makes revision feel like reading the chapter all over again. Good notes are not a summary of the book. They are a **revision tool** — built for the stressed, short-on-time version of you who will use them in the last weeks before the exam.

## How to Make Notes for Medical Exams: Start From the Revision, Not the Chapter

Before writing a single line, ask one question: *when I revise this topic at 11 pm two days before the exam, what will I actually need?* You will need the facts that are asked repeatedly, the comparisons you keep confusing, the values and criteria you cannot afford to mix up, and the traps that examiners set again and again. You will not need the paragraph of background physiology you already understand.

This is why note-making should usually come **after** a first reading, not during it. Read the topic once for understanding. Then close the book and write down, from memory, what matters. Writing from memory does two things: it forces you to select instead of copy, and it quietly tests you — the gaps you notice while writing are exactly the gaps the exam will find.

## What Belongs in Your Notes (and What Does Not)

A useful rule: if a point would change your answer to a question, it belongs in your notes. If it would not, it probably does not.

**Write down:**
- **High-yield facts** — the ones that appear in questions year after year
- **Comparisons** — diseases, drugs or investigations you confuse with each other, in table form
- **Numbers** — normal values, doses, durations, diagnostic criteria and cut-offs
- **Exceptions** — "most common cause is X, *except* in children, where it is Y"
- **Your own mistakes** — every question you get wrong in practice points to a line that should be in your notes

**Leave out:**
- Full paragraphs copied from the textbook — you already own the textbook
- Background you genuinely understand and never get wrong
- Rare details with no history of being asked, unless your syllabus demands them
- Beautiful colour-coding that takes longer than the studying itself

Notice what is on the "write down" list: your own mistakes. Notes built partly from your errors are personal in a way no printed summary can be, because they record the difference between what the book says and what *you* actually retain.

## A Structure That Survives the Whole Year

Structure matters because revision is a search problem. In the final weeks you may have twenty minutes for a topic, and your notes must let you find the one fact you need in seconds.

- **One topic, one unit.** Keep each topic in its own page or file so you can revise topics independently and in any order.
- **Headings that match questions.** "Causes", "Clinical features", "Investigations", "Treatment" — the same skeleton examiners use.
- **Tables for anything comparative.** Side-by-side columns beat paragraphs whenever two things must be distinguished.
- **Short lines, not sentences.** A note is a trigger for memory, not an essay. If a line needs a comma-heavy explanation, you have not understood it well enough yet — go back to the book for that line only.
- **Leave white space.** You will add lines after every mock test. Notes that cannot grow become outdated by your second revision.

## Write Notes Your Future Self Can Test From

The strongest study methods share one feature: they make you **retrieve** information instead of just looking at it. Research on the **testing effect** shows that pulling an answer out of memory strengthens that memory far more than re-reading it, and Dunlosky and colleagues (2013), reviewing the evidence on study techniques, rated practice testing among the highest-utility methods — and re-reading among the lowest.

So write notes that can be tested from. Turn headings into questions: instead of writing the features of a disease as a paragraph, list them so you can cover the page and recall them. Leave the answer column of your tables blank on a photocopy or second version. When you revise, cover, recall, then check. A page of notes used this way stops being reading material and becomes a self-test — which is precisely why it works.

## The Revision Test: If You Never Open It, Rewrite It

Be honest about the notes you already have. If a set of notes has sat untouched for two months, the problem is not your discipline — it is the notes. They are too long, too dense or too boring to use. Compress them. A topic you first wrote in eight pages should survive in two by the second revision and in half a page by the final one. That shrinking is not loss; it is the visible record of learning. What remains at the end — the stubborn facts, the exceptions, the numbers — is your true last-minute material.

Q: Should I make handwritten notes or digital notes for medical exams?
A: Either works. Handwriting is slower, which forces selection, but digital notes are easier to search, edit and compress over repeated revisions. Choose the format you will actually reopen, and keep one consistent system.
Q: How long should my notes for one topic be?
A: Short enough to revise in the time you will realistically give that topic in the final weeks — often two pages or less for a standard topic, shrinking with each revision. If revising them feels like re-reading the chapter, they are too long.
Q: Should I copy notes from toppers or printed summaries instead of making my own?
A: Use them as a reference for what to include, but write your own. The act of selecting and rephrasing is part of the learning, and only your notes will contain your own mistakes and confusions.
Q: When should I make notes — while reading or after?
A: After a first full reading of the topic. Writing while reading usually becomes copying; writing after reading forces you to select what matters and reveals what you did not actually understand.
