For every student heading abroad, one fact should be pinned above the desk from day one: your degree makes you eligible, but the **fmge after mbbs abroad** is what makes you a doctor in India. Too many students treat the Foreign Medical Graduate Examination as a problem for their future selves, and meet it unprepared after six years of studying a different curriculum. This article describes the exam exactly as it is, why foreign graduates find it demanding, and the calm, multi-year plan that turns it from a threat into a formality.

## FMGE after MBBS abroad: what the exam actually is

The FMGE is a licensing examination conducted by **NBEMS** (the National Board of Examinations in Medical Sciences) for Indian citizens and OCIs who hold a primary medical qualification from abroad. Its structure is fixed and knowable:

- **300 multiple-choice questions** in a single day, split into two parts of 150
- **No negative marking** — attempting every question costs nothing
- **Pass mark: 150 out of 300**
- Questions drawn from the **Indian undergraduate medical curriculum**, across the 19 subjects from Anatomy to Community Medicine, with the clinical subjects carrying the largest weight

Notice what this means. The exam is not a mystery and it is not designed to be failed; it is designed to confirm that a returning graduate meets the standard of an Indian MBBS graduate. Half the marks, with no penalty for wrong answers, is a very achievable bar for a student who has prepared deliberately. The students who struggle are overwhelmingly those who begin preparing after they return.

## Why the exam feels hard for foreign graduates

Understanding the difficulty honestly helps you plan against it. Three gaps do most of the damage:

**The curriculum gap.** Your college abroad teaches medicine well, but not necessarily the Indian syllabus — the emphasis, the guidelines, the disease patterns and the style of questions differ. Subjects like Community Medicine, with its India-specific programmes and statistics, can be almost absent abroad.

**The pattern gap.** The FMGE asks Indian-style clinical MCQs: one-line stems, image-based questions, and "most likely diagnosis" reasoning at speed. A student can know the medicine and still lose marks to unfamiliarity with the format.

**The time gap.** Six years abroad, then a few months of panic preparation, compresses a 19-subject syllabus into an impossible window. The knowledge was never the main problem; the calendar was.

Every one of these gaps closes easily if you start early — and barely at all if you start late.

## The plan: prepare from the first year, lightly and steadily

The strategy that works is not heroic. It is simply early.

**Years 1–2: align the basics.** As you study Anatomy, Physiology and Biochemistry abroad, glance at how the same topics are asked in India. Solve topic questions alongside your college work — an hour a week is enough to build the habit and the map of what matters.

**Years 3–4: add the clinical bridge.** Pathology, Pharmacology, Microbiology and then the clinical subjects: solve questions topic by topic, and start noting India-specific areas — national programmes, Indian guidelines, local disease patterns — in a single running document.

**Final years: shift to full papers.** Move to timed, full-length practice in the real format: 150-question parts, then full 300-question days. Analyse every attempt subject-wise, and let the analysis, not your mood, choose what you revise next.

**After returning: consolidate, don't start.** If the earlier years were used well, the post-return period becomes revision and mock refinement rather than a first reading of 19 subjects.

This is also why your choice of college matters to your FMGE outcome: strong clinical training abroad plus steady Indian-pattern practice is the combination that clears comfortably. Our [resources](/resources/) section collects planning guides for each stage of this timeline.

One habit deserves special emphasis because it costs nothing and changes everything: **analyse every mock you attempt**. Do not just note your score. Record which subjects lost you marks, which questions you guessed, and which topics you have now missed twice. A simple notebook or spreadsheet, maintained across your student years, becomes the most personalised revision plan in existence — it is written by your own performance, not by a stranger's timetable. Students who keep this record arrive at their final year already knowing their weak subjects by name, and their last months become targeted repair instead of general panic.

## Habits that separate clearers from repeaters

Across every session, the pattern repeats. Students who clear tend to have solved questions continuously for years, revised the same core material multiple times, and treated mock analysis as the main study tool. Students who repeat attempts tend to have read passively, changed materials repeatedly, and sat the exam hoping familiarity would substitute for practice. The FMGE rewards **retrieval and revision**, not exposure.

One more practical point: eligibility paperwork deserves the same early attention as study. Your foreign programme must satisfy the FMGL Regulations 2021, and your documents will be scrutinised when you apply. Keep every certificate, transcript and internship document safe and organised from the beginning, and always follow the current official NBEMS information bulletin for the session you apply to.

Q: What is the FMGE and who conducts it?
A: The FMGE is the licensing examination for Indian citizens and OCIs with a primary medical qualification from abroad, conducted by NBEMS. It has 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, no negative marking, and a pass mark of 150 out of 300.
Q: When should I start preparing for the FMGE if I study abroad?
A: From your first year. Light, steady Indian-pattern practice alongside your college subjects — topic questions early, full timed papers in the final years — closes the curriculum and pattern gaps while they are still small. Starting after returning compresses 19 subjects into a few months.
Q: Why do foreign graduates find the FMGE difficult?
A: Mainly three gaps: the Indian curriculum emphasis differs from many foreign programmes, the Indian MCQ style is unfamiliar without practice, and preparation is often left until after graduation. All three are planning problems, and all three are solvable with early, consistent work.
Q: What happens after I pass the FMGE?
A: You complete a 12-month CRMI internship in India and then apply for permanent registration with your state medical council, after which you can practise medicine in India.
