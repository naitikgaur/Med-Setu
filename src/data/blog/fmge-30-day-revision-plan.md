The final month before FMGE is not for learning medicine — it is for converting what you have already studied into marks. Candidates who keep reading new material in the last 30 days usually walk in knowing more and scoring less. This **fmge revision plan last 30 days** gives you a day-by-day structure: which subjects to revise on which days, when to sit mocks, and how the final week should taper so you peak on exam day, not a week before it. If you want a shorter overview first, the [resources](/resources/) section has revision checklists too — this plan is the full day-by-day version.

## FMGE revision plan: the rules of the last 30 days

- **No new subjects, no new books.** Your material now is your notes, your marked questions and your error log.
- **Revision follows weightage.** Medicine (33 questions), Surgery (32), OBG (30) and Community Medicine (30) get the most days; the 5-mark subjects get focused single sittings.
- **One full mock every week**, sat under real conditions — 300 questions, Part A and Part B with a break, timed.
- **Every mock produces homework.** The analysis of a test is worth more than the test itself.
- **Attempt-everything discipline.** With **no negative marking** and a pass mark of **150 out of 300**, leaving blanks is the one unforced error you must eliminate this month.

## Days 1–7: the big four, second pass

**Day 1–2 — Medicine.** Revise system-wise from your notes. Spend the second afternoon on your Medicine error-log entries only. Solve 100 mixed questions in Exam mode in the [question bank](/qbank/) each evening.

**Day 3–4 — Surgery.** Classifications, staging, investigations of choice, most-common lists. These fact-clusters are what Surgery questions are made of.

**Day 5 — Obstetrics & Gynaecology.** Labour, emergencies, antenatal care, contraception — one intense day, notes plus marked questions.

**Day 6 — Community Medicine.** Epidemiology, screening, vaccines, programmes, nutrition. SPM facts decay fast; this is why it sits close to the exam in every cycle of this plan.

**Day 7 — Mock 1.** A full paper from the [previous-year papers](/papers/), sat at exam time. Analyse it in the evening: every wrong answer labelled knowledge gap, silly mistake or failed guess. The knowledge gaps become Days 8–14's revision list.

## Days 8–14: the middle band

**Day 8 — Paediatrics.** Milestones, immunisation, common childhood illnesses, neonatology basics.
**Day 9 — Ophthalmology.** Cataract, glaucoma, refractive errors, eye emergencies.
**Day 10 — ENT.** Ear, nose and throat high-yield topics and their classic presentations.
**Day 11 — Pathology.** General pathology plus your weakest systemic topics from Mock 1.
**Day 12 — Microbiology.** Organism–disease pairs, sterilisation, hospital infections.
**Day 13 — Pharmacology.** Drug classes, mechanisms, adverse effects — revise from tables and your marked questions.
**Day 14 — Mock 2.** This time use a fresh test from the [grand test series](/tests/). Compare the analysis with Mock 1: are the same topics still leaking marks? Those topics are now your highest priority in the building.

## Days 15–21: pre-clinicals and the small subjects

**Day 15 — Anatomy.** Neuroanatomy and your marked regions only.
**Day 16 — Physiology.** CVS, respiratory, renal — the systems the paper returns to.
**Day 17 — Biochemistry.** Pathways, enzymes, molecular biology, vitamins.
**Day 18 — Forensic Medicine.** Legal procedures, poisons, injuries — a compact, scorable day.
**Day 19 — Radiology + Psychiatry.** Imaging signs and investigation-of-choice; psychiatric disorders and their first-line management concepts.
**Day 20 — Dermatology + Anaesthesia + Orthopaedics.** Three 5-mark subjects in one day: common skin conditions and their descriptions, anaesthetic drugs and monitoring basics, fractures and orthopaedic emergencies.
**Day 21 — Mock 3.** Full paper, real timing. By now your score should be sitting at or above 150 with some consistency. If a subject is still dragging, Day 22 is reserved for it.

## Track the four mocks as one story

Do not treat each mock as an isolated event. Keep one simple table — mock number, total score, and marks lost per subject — and update it after every test. Across four mocks this table tells you three things no single test can: whether your total is **trending toward a safe margin above 150** or wobbling around it, which subjects leak marks *repeatedly* rather than once, and whether your silly-mistake count is falling as your pacing settles. If the same subject appears in the loss column three times, it does not matter that you "revised" it — the revision method for that subject is not working, and Days 22–24 should attack it through questions and error-log entries rather than another passive reading.

## Days 22–30: error-log week and the taper

**Day 22 — weakest subject day.** Whatever Mock 3 exposed, revised from notes and errors only.
**Days 23–24 — rapid fact sweep.** Classifications, normal values, most-common lists, programme and vaccine details, drug adverse-effect tables. Short, fast, repeated.
**Day 25 — Mock 4, your final full test.** Sat exactly like the real exam, same start time, same break pattern.
**Day 26 — final analysis.** Revise only the errors from Mock 4. Then close the error log — it has done its job.
**Days 27–28 — light revision.** Skim your one-page summaries and fact lists. Two short sessions a day, nothing heavy, nothing new.
**Day 29 — logistics.** Admit card, documents, centre route, exam-day timing. Lay everything out. Stop studying by evening.
**Day 30 — exam day.** Reach early. In each part: first pass for what you know, flag the doubtful, return, and finish by attempting everything that remains — a blank answer is the only guaranteed zero in a no-negative-marking paper.

Thirty days of disciplined revision will not add new knowledge — it will make your existing knowledge show up on the scoresheet, which is the entire point. Set up the month today: book your four mock days first using the [grand test series](/tests/) and past papers, then let the subject rotation fill the days between them.

Q: Should I study new topics in the last 30 days before FMGE?
A: No. The final month works best as pure revision — your notes, marked questions and error log — plus weekly full mock tests. New material at this stage usually displaces revision of higher-yield topics.
Q: How many mock tests should I take in the last 30 days of FMGE?
A: One full mock a week — four in total — each sat under real exam conditions (300 questions, two parts, timed) and each followed by a detailed error analysis.
Q: Which subjects should I revise closest to the FMGE exam?
A: Fact-heavy subjects decay fastest, so keep Community Medicine, Pharmacology tables and rapid fact lists in the later cycles, while the big clinical subjects — Medicine, Surgery and OBG — anchor the first week.
Q: What should I do on the day before FMGE?
A: Keep it light: check your admit card and documents, confirm the centre and reporting time, skim only your shortest summaries, and sleep early. No full tests and no new material.
