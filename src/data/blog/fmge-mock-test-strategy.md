A good **fmge mock test strategy** answers three questions that students usually leave to chance: how many mocks should I take, when should I take them, and what exactly should I do with the result? Mock tests are not a score-prediction ritual. They are a training tool — the only tool that simultaneously trains knowledge retrieval, pacing, stamina and decision-making under the real format: **300 MCQs, Part A 150 and Part B 150, +1 per correct answer, no negative marking, pass mark 150**. Used casually, mocks waste entire days and demoralise. Used with a system, they become the engine of the last marks that separate a pass from a near miss. This article gives you that system.

## Why an FMGE Mock Test Strategy Matters More Than You Think

The FMGE compresses **19 subjects** into a single day. Most candidates who fall short do not fall short on one subject; they fall short on breadth, speed and consistency. A student can know Medicine well and still lose the exam in Biochemistry one-liners, Community Medicine programmes, or simple fatigue in the second part. Only full-length mocks expose those weaknesses honestly, because topic tests and reading both flatter you: they test what you studied recently, in a rested state, without time pressure. A mock removes all three comforts at once.

Mocks also train a skill that is invisible until exam day: **sustained decision-making**. Three hundred questions means three hundred small judgements — answer, eliminate, guess, move on. That judgement tires like a muscle, and like a muscle it improves with progressive training.

## How Many Mocks Should You Take?

There is no magic number, and anyone who gives you one without asking about your stage is guessing. Work backwards from your timeline instead:

- **Long preparation (several months available):** begin with one diagnostic mock, then one full mock every one to two weeks during the main study phase, increasing to one or two per week in the final revision phase.
- **Short preparation (a few weeks left):** prioritise frequency over volume of new reading — a mock every two to three days, each followed by a full review, teaches more now than fresh chapters read once.
- **Final week:** taper. One or two last mocks early in the week, then light revision. Do not take a mock the day before the exam; arrive rested.

The binding constraint is never the number of mocks available — MedSetu alone offers 12 [previous-year papers](/papers/) and a 30-test [grand test series](/tests/), which is more full papers than most students can review properly. The constraint is **review capacity**. A mock you do not analyse is worth a fraction of one you do. If your schedule forces a choice, take fewer mocks and review each one completely.

## When to Start: The Three Stages

### Stage one — the diagnostic mock

Take your first full mock early, even if you feel unprepared — especially if you feel unprepared. Its job is not to judge you; it is to draw the map. Which subjects are already safe? Which collapse? Do you finish Part A comfortably or rush the last thirty questions? Every later decision — subject order, daily targets, revision cycles — should trace back to this attempt.

### Stage two — mocks as checkpoints

During the main study phase, treat each mock as a checkpoint on specific repairs. You identified Pharmacology as weak; you studied it for two weeks; the next mock tells you whether the repair worked. Change one or two things between mocks, not ten, so you can see what actually helped.

### Stage three — mocks as rehearsal

In the final phase, the mock becomes a dress rehearsal of exam day itself: same start time, same break pattern between parts, no phone, no pausing. You are no longer mainly learning content; you are automating behaviour so that nothing on the real day is new except the questions.

## How to Analyse a Mock: The Part Most Students Skip

Analysis should take at least as long as the attempt felt tiring — and it should be structured, not a scroll through wrong answers. Work through these layers:

- **Score by subject.** Which subjects are below the level the blueprint demands of them? A big subject below par costs more than a small one.
- **Error classification.** Separate every lost mark into: did not know, knew but misread, knew but chose wrong between two options, or ran out of time. Each class has a different cure, and most students discover their errors are not all knowledge gaps.
- **Time analysis.** Where did the minutes go? Questions that took three times the average and still went wrong are doubly expensive.
- **Attempt pattern.** With no negative marking, unattempted questions are pure loss. Count them; the target is zero.
- **Guess audit.** Mark the questions you guessed. A right guess is a hidden gap; a wrong guess on a topic that repeats is a priority fix.

However you do it, end every analysis by writing **three concrete repairs** — not "study Anatomy more", but "revise brachial plexus and skull foramina; both cost marks twice".

## Common Mock-Test Mistakes

- **Taking mocks only when feeling ready.** Readiness is the output of mocks, not the entry ticket.
- **Comparing scores across different sources.** A recall paper, a grand test and a subject test measure different things; track your trend within one series.
- **Reviewing only wrong answers.** Lucky guesses and slow correct answers are also data.
- **Changing your whole plan after one bad mock.** One attempt is one sample; look for patterns across two or three.
- **Ignoring Part B.** Fatigue errors in the second part are real; train for them by always practising in full two-part sittings, using the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) for shorter sessions only when time genuinely does not allow a full paper.

## A Simple Weekly Template

In the main phase: five days of study with topic questions, one full mock, one review-and-repair day. In the final phase: alternate mock days and repair days, with the mock always under full exam conditions. Protect sleep in both phases — a tired brain converts known answers into wrong ones, and no strategy survives exhaustion.

Mocks are where preparation becomes performance. Pick your series, start with a diagnostic attempt on MedSetu's [grand test series](/tests/), and let each analysis — not anxiety — decide what you study next.
