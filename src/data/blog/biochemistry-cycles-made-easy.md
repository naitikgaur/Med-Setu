Biochemistry feels vast until you realise the exam keeps returning to the same pathways with the same three questions: where does it happen, which step controls it, and what goes wrong when an enzyme fails? Good **biochemistry cycles revision** is therefore not about redrawing every arrow from memory. It is about fixing, for each pathway, its **site** (organ and cell compartment), its **rate-limiting step**, its **key regulation**, and its **clinical links**. Learn those four anchors for glycolysis, the TCA cycle, gluconeogenesis, glycogen metabolism, the HMP shunt, the urea cycle and fatty acid metabolism, and most pathway questions become direct recall.

## Biochemistry cycles revision: the map in one view

Before the details, fix the geography, because site alone answers many one-liners:

- **Cytosol only** — glycolysis, glycogen synthesis and breakdown, HMP shunt, fatty acid synthesis
- **Mitochondria only** — TCA (Krebs) cycle, fatty acid oxidation (beta-oxidation), most of the urea cycle's first steps
- **Both cytosol and mitochondria** — gluconeogenesis, urea cycle, haem synthesis
- **Liver is the metabolic hub** — it is the main site of gluconeogenesis, glycogen storage, the urea cycle and fatty acid synthesis; muscle stores glycogen but lacks **glucose-6-phosphatase**, so it cannot release free glucose into blood
- **Red blood cells** depend entirely on glycolysis (no mitochondria) and on the HMP shunt to protect against oxidative damage

A useful habit: whenever you revise a pathway, say its compartment out loud. Examiners love pairing a cytosolic pathway with a mitochondrial distractor.

## Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis: opposite directions, different controls

**Glycolysis** breaks glucose into pyruvate in the **cytosol of all cells**. Its key facts:

- **Rate-limiting enzyme: phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1)**, which converts fructose-6-phosphate to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate
- Other irreversible, regulated steps are **hexokinase/glucokinase** and **pyruvate kinase**
- **Regulation:** PFK-1 is activated by **fructose-2,6-bisphosphate** and AMP, and inhibited by ATP and citrate. Insulin favours glycolysis; in the liver, glucokinase is induced by insulin
- Net gain from one glucose: **2 ATP** (substrate-level phosphorylation), plus pyruvate for the TCA cycle or lactate under anaerobic conditions
- **Clinical links:** **pyruvate kinase deficiency** causes chronic haemolytic anaemia because red cells cannot make enough ATP; hexokinase deficiency is a rarer cause of haemolysis

**Gluconeogenesis** makes new glucose from lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acids, mainly in the **liver** (and kidney cortex in prolonged fasting), split between **mitochondria and cytosol**.

- It bypasses the irreversible glycolysis steps using **pyruvate carboxylase** (the first and key regulated enzyme), **phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK)**, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase and glucose-6-phosphatase
- **Regulation:** glucagon and cortisol promote it, insulin suppresses it; fructose-2,6-bisphosphate inhibits fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, which is why the two pathways are reciprocally controlled and never fully run together
- **Clinical link:** **glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency (von Gierke disease, glycogen storage disease type I)** blocks the final step of both gluconeogenesis and glycogen breakdown, causing fasting hypoglycaemia with hepatomegaly

## TCA cycle and glycogen metabolism

The **TCA (Krebs) cycle** runs in the **mitochondrial matrix** of aerobic cells. Acetyl-CoA from carbohydrates, fats and amino acids is oxidised to carbon dioxide, generating NADH and FADH2 for the electron transport chain.

- **Rate-limiting enzyme: isocitrate dehydrogenase**; citrate synthase and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase are the other regulated steps
- **Regulation:** the cycle is driven by substrate supply and pulled by demand — ADP activates, while ATP and NADH inhibit its key enzymes. It has no hormonal control of its own; it responds to the cell's energy state
- Per acetyl-CoA it yields **3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP and 2 CO2**
- **Clinical links:** the cycle itself has few classic inherited defects asked in exams, but **pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency** starves it of acetyl-CoA from glucose and presents with lactic acidosis and neurological features; thiamine deficiency impairs pyruvate dehydrogenase and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase

**Glycogen metabolism** happens in the **cytosol**, mainly of **liver and muscle**.

- **Synthesis:** **glycogen synthase** is the key enzyme, activated by insulin and glucose-6-phosphate; a primer protein, glycogenin, starts the chain
- **Breakdown:** **glycogen phosphorylase** is rate-limiting, activated by glucagon (liver) and adrenaline (muscle); the debranching enzyme and glucose-6-phosphatase (liver only) complete glucose release
- **Clinical links:** **Pompe disease (type II)** is lysosomal acid maltase (alpha-1,4-glucosidase) deficiency; **McArdle disease (type V)** is muscle phosphorylase deficiency, causing exercise intolerance and cramps; **Hers disease (type VI)** is liver phosphorylase deficiency

## HMP shunt, urea cycle and fatty acid metabolism

The **hexose monophosphate (HMP) shunt** runs in the **cytosol**, especially in liver, adipose tissue, adrenal cortex, red cells and lactating mammary gland. It makes **NADPH** (for reductive synthesis and antioxidant defence) and **ribose-5-phosphate** (for nucleotides) — not ATP.

- **Rate-limiting enzyme: glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)**, activated by NADP+ and insulin, inhibited by NADPH
- **Clinical link:** **G6PD deficiency** is the classic exam favourite — oxidative stress from fava beans, infections or drugs such as primaquine causes haemolysis with **Heinz bodies** and bite cells, because red cells cannot regenerate reduced glutathione

The **urea cycle** disposes of ammonia in the **liver**, starting in **mitochondria** and continuing in the **cytosol**.

- **Rate-limiting enzyme: carbamoyl phosphate synthetase-1 (CPS-1)**, activated by **N-acetyl glutamate**
- **Clinical links:** **ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency** is the commonest urea cycle disorder (X-linked) and causes severe hyperammonaemia in the newborn; CPS-1 deficiency presents similarly. Hyperammonaemia is neurotoxic, so these questions often arrive as a vomiting, lethargic neonate

**Fatty acid oxidation** occurs in the **mitochondrial matrix**; long-chain fatty acids enter via the **carnitine shuttle**, whose **carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1 (CPT-1)** is the rate-limiting step, inhibited by malonyl-CoA.

- **Clinical link:** **medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (MCAD) deficiency** is the commonest fatty acid oxidation defect, presenting with fasting hypoketotic hypoglycaemia
- **Fatty acid synthesis** is the mirror image in the **cytosol** of liver and adipose tissue: **acetyl-CoA carboxylase** is rate-limiting (activated by citrate and insulin, inhibited by glucagon and palmitoyl-CoA) and makes malonyl-CoA, and the multifunctional **fatty acid synthase** complex builds palmitate using NADPH from the HMP shunt

## How to revise cycles without forgetting them

Pathways fade quickly if you only read them, so revise actively:

- **One pathway, one card** — site, rate-limiting enzyme, two regulators, one deficiency. Seven pathways fit on seven cards
- **Pair opposites together** — glycolysis with gluconeogenesis, glycogen synthesis with breakdown, fatty acid synthesis with oxidation; the exam tests the contrasts
- **Attach a disease to every enzyme** — a deficiency is the most common clinical stem for a pathway question
- **Draw once, recall many times** — sketch the pathway skeleton from memory, then check; do not copy it neatly again and again
- **Mix your practice** — timed [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) force you to retrieve these facts among questions from every other subject, which is how the FMGE paper actually presents them

Remember the exam frame: FMGE has **300 MCQs in two parts of 150**, no negative marking, and a pass mark of **150 out of 300**, conducted by NBEMS. Biochemistry one-liners are among the most predictable marks in that paper — if your anchors are fixed.

Q: What is the rate-limiting enzyme of glycolysis?
A: Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1), which converts fructose-6-phosphate to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. It is activated by fructose-2,6-bisphosphate and inhibited by ATP and citrate.
Q: Which enzyme deficiency causes haemolysis with Heinz bodies?
A: Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, the rate-limiting enzyme of the HMP shunt. Oxidative stress from fava beans, infections or drugs like primaquine triggers the haemolysis.
Q: What is the commonest urea cycle disorder?
A: Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency, an X-linked disorder causing severe hyperammonaemia, typically presenting in the newborn period.
Q: Which glycogen storage disease affects muscle phosphorylase?
A: McArdle disease (type V). Muscle glycogen phosphorylase deficiency causes exercise intolerance, cramps and myoglobinuria, while von Gierke disease (type I) is glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency.
