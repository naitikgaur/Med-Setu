Few tables in paediatrics are asked as reliably as **developmental milestones paediatrics** questions. The FMGE examiner does not expect you to know obscure research norms; the paper sticks to the standard textbook ages found in Ghai Essential Pediatrics, and asks them in the simplest possible way — at what age does a child sit without support, say the first meaningful word, or develop a pincer grasp? These are fast, factual marks, but only if your ages are exact. A month here or there is the difference between the right option and the nearest distractor, so this table deserves deliberate, repeated revision.

## Developmental Milestones Paediatrics: The Four Domains

Development is assessed in four domains, and questions may come from any of them:

- **Gross motor:** control of the large muscles — neck holding, sitting, standing, walking
- **Fine motor and adaptive:** hand skills — grasping, transferring objects, pincer grasp, scribbling
- **Language:** hearing, understanding and speech — cooing, babbling, first words, sentences
- **Personal and social:** interaction and self-care — social smile, stranger anxiety, waving bye-bye, dressing

Two general principles help you reason in the exam hall. Development proceeds **cephalocaudally**, from head to toe, which is why neck control comes before sitting and sitting before walking. It also proceeds from **mass activity to specific responses** — the young infant grasps with the whole palm before the refined pincer grasp appears. A child is judged against the age by which most normal children achieve a skill, and a delay in one domain alone means something different from a **global developmental delay**, where two or more domains are affected. Practising these ages inside the [question bank](/qbank/) quickly shows which domain the examiners favour — in practice, gross motor and language carry the most questions.

## Gross Motor Milestones From Birth to Two Years

Gross motor is the most heavily tested domain, and the sequence matters as much as the ages.

- **3 months:** neck holding — the baby holds the head steady when held upright
- **4 months:** rolls over beginning, head lag disappears when pulled to sit
- **5 months:** rolls over completely, from prone to supine and back
- **6 months:** sits with support, in a tripod position leaning on the hands
- **6–8 months:** sits without support steadily
- **8 months:** crawling — the baby moves on the abdomen and then on hands and knees
- **9 months:** stands with support, pulls up holding furniture
- **10 months:** cruising — walking sideways while holding onto furniture
- **12 months:** stands without support and walks a few steps, the classic first birthday milestone
- **15 months:** walks well, creeps upstairs
- **18 months:** runs, walks upstairs holding a rail with both feet on a step
- **2 years:** walks up and down stairs one step at a time, kicks a ball

Notice the tidy ladder: neck at 3 months, sitting at around 6 to 8 months, standing at 9 to 12 months, walking at about 12 months. If a stem describes a normal 9-month-old who cannot walk, that is not delay — walking is a 12-month skill, and examiners test this boundary often.

## Fine Motor and Adaptive Milestones

Fine motor milestones follow the maturation of hand control, from the primitive grasp reflex of the newborn to precise finger work.

- **3 months:** hands mostly open, brings hands together in the midline, follows objects across the midline
- **4 months:** reaches for objects, grasps with the whole palm — the **palmar grasp**
- **5 months:** bidextrous reach, grasps objects voluntarily
- **6 months:** transfers objects from one hand to the other, radial palmar grasp
- **7 months:** sits and manipulates objects, uses a raking movement
- **9 months:** **pincer grasp** develops — first immature, using the side of the index finger, then mature with finger and thumb tips
- **12 months:** releases objects on request, gives toys to others, tries to build a tower
- **15 months:** builds a tower of 2–3 cubes, scribbles spontaneously
- **18 months:** builds a tower of 3–4 cubes, copies a vertical stroke later, feeds self with a spoon
- **2 years:** builds a tower of 6–7 cubes, turns pages one at a time

The **pincer grasp at 9 months** is the single most repeated fine motor fact in the paper. Pair it with its gross motor partner at the same age — standing with support — and you have two answers anchored to one number.

## Language Milestones: From Cooing to Sentences

Language questions test both sound production and vocabulary size, and the ages form a neat doubling pattern in the second year.

- **1 month:** turns head to sound, startles to loud noise
- **3 months:** **cooing** — vowel sounds made in response to being spoken to
- **6 months:** **monosyllables** — sounds like ma, ba, da without meaning
- **9 months:** **bisyllables** — mama, dada, baba, still without specific meaning
- **10 months:** understands "no", responds to own name
- **12 months:** speaks **1–2 words with meaning**, true mama and dada for the parents
- **15 months:** **jargon** — fluent babbling in the child's own language, follows simple commands
- **18 months:** vocabulary of about 10–20 words, points to body parts
- **2 years:** vocabulary of around 100 words or more, joins 2–3 words into simple sentences, refers to self by name
- **3 years:** full sentences, knows name, age and gender, asks constant questions

The distinctions examiners love are fine ones: monosyllables at 6 months versus bisyllables at 9 months, and babbling versus the first word **with meaning** at 12 months. A child saying "mama" at 9 months is babbling; the same word used specifically for the mother at 12 months is speech. Testing yourself on these near-identical pairs in the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) is the quickest way to stop losing marks on them.

## Social and Personal Milestones, Red Flags

Social milestones are fewer but very frequently asked, because they screen for interaction and attachment.

- **2 months:** **social smile** — smiles back in response to a face or voice
- **3 months:** recognises the mother, anticipates being picked up
- **6 months:** enjoys mirror play, distinguishes familiar faces
- **7–8 months:** **stranger anxiety** appears, the baby clings to the mother with unfamiliar people
- **9 months:** waves **bye-bye**, plays peek-a-boo
- **12 months:** comes when called, cooperates in dressing, indicates wants
- **18 months:** imitates adults, feeds self, kisses parents
- **2 years:** parallel play alongside other children, dry by day in many children
- **3 years:** interactive play, shares toys, dresses with help

Finally, learn the **red flags** — ages by which a milestone must appear, failing which the child needs evaluation. No neck holding by 4 months, no sitting without support by 8 months, no standing by 12 months, no walking by 18 months, no single meaningful word by 18 months, and loss of previously acquired skills at any age are all warning signs. The **social smile at 2 months** is the earliest and most tested social fact, and its absence in an older infant is itself a red flag. Exam questions on red flags usually describe a child just past one of these limits and ask for the next step, which is referral for developmental assessment.

Q: At what age does a child develop a social smile?
A: A social smile appears at 2 months, when the baby begins to smile back in response to a face or voice.
Q: At what age does the pincer grasp develop?
A: The pincer grasp develops at around 9 months, beginning as an immature grasp and maturing to a neat finger-thumb grasp by about 12 months.
Q: By what age should a child walk independently?
A: Most children walk a few steps by about 12 months and walk well by 15 months. Not walking by 18 months is a red flag needing evaluation.
Q: What is the difference between monosyllables and bisyllables in development?
A: Monosyllables such as ma and ba appear at about 6 months, while bisyllables such as mama and dada appear at about 9 months, initially without specific meaning.
