Nineteen subjects is the number that frightens FMGE candidates before they even begin. It sounds like nineteen separate exams. It is not: it is one exam of **300 questions** in which the subjects share concepts, reinforce each other, and — crucially — carry wildly different weights. This guide on **how to study 19 subjects fmge** shows you how to group the subjects, sequence them by marks, and pace the work so you reach the exam with your energy intact. Burnout is not a character flaw; it is usually a planning failure, and planning is fixable.

## How to study 19 subjects for FMGE: see four groups, not nineteen silos

**Group 1 — Pre-clinical: Anatomy (17), Physiology (17), Biochemistry (17).** 51 questions. These are foundations: structures, mechanisms, pathways. Study them for their high-yield cores — neuroanatomy, CVS and respiratory physiology, metabolic pathways — not like a first-year student with a year to spare.

**Group 2 — Para-clinical: Pathology (13), Microbiology (13), Pharmacology (13), Forensic Medicine (10).** 49 questions. The bridge subjects. Pathology is the language Medicine and Surgery speak; pharmacology repeats inside every clinical subject you will ever study.

**Group 3 — Clinical majors: Medicine (33), Surgery (32), Obstetrics & Gynaecology (30), Paediatrics (15).** 110 questions. The heart of the paper and the heart of your timetable.

**Group 4 — Clinical minors and specials: Community Medicine (30), Ophthalmology (15), ENT (15), Radiology (10), Psychiatry (5), Dermatology (5), Anaesthesia (5), Orthopaedics (5).** 90 questions — and note that Community Medicine sits here by timetable logic, not by size: at 30 questions it is a giant wearing a minor's clothing.

Grouped this way, the syllabus is four projects, not nineteen. Each group also studies differently: pre-clinicals reward concept maps, para-clinicals reward tables and matching drills, clinicals reward case-style question practice.

## Sequence by weightage, connect by logic

Do not study the groups in college order. A marks-first sequence that also respects how subjects connect:

1. **Medicine first** — the largest subject, and the one that makes Pathology and Pharmacology make sense when you return to them.
2. **Surgery + Orthopaedics together** — trauma, fractures and surgical emergencies overlap heavily.
3. **OBG** — self-contained, high-yield, quick to complete relative to its 30 questions.
4. **Community Medicine** — fact-based; park it mid-plan and again near the exam, because its facts decay fastest.
5. **Pathology, Microbiology, Pharmacology** — now easier, because clinical study has shown you why they matter.
6. **Paediatrics, Ophthalmology, ENT** — the 15-mark band, each compact enough for a focused block.
7. **Anatomy, Physiology, Biochemistry** — high-yield cores only, unless your timeline is long.
8. **Forensic Medicine, Radiology, Psychiatry, Dermatology, Anaesthesia** — the small-subject sweep: 40 questions from the shortest syllabi, ideal for the phase when your stamina for new material is fading.

Wherever two subjects genuinely connect — Physiology with Medicine, Pharmacology with everything — studying them adjacently means each one revises the other for free.

## The anti-burnout operating system

**Cap the day, vary the work.** Burnout grows in monotonous marathons. Alternate reading blocks with question blocks in the [question bank](/qbank/), and never study one subject for an entire day unless it is a deliberate finishing push. Two subjects a day is a good default rhythm.

**Test each subject as you close it.** Finishing a subject should end with evidence, not a feeling. A [subject-wise test](/subjects/) after each subject gives you a score, a list of leaks, and the psychological reward of a completed milestone. Nineteen small victories sustain motivation better than one distant exam.

**Revise in cycles or lose the early subjects.** The cruellest FMGE experience is reaching month five and finding month one's Anatomy gone. Prevent it structurally: every topic gets a first revision within days and a second within weeks, and your weekly schedule always includes mixed questions from earlier subjects. Your early subjects stay warm with surprisingly little time — a daily mixed set does most of the work.

**One lighter day a week, non-negotiable.** Rest is when memory consolidates and motivation refills. Candidates who schedule rest take it guilt-free and return sharper; candidates who don't, take it anyway — as collapse, at a worse time.

**Watch for the real warning signs.** Persistent dread of starting, re-reading the same page, practice scores falling despite more hours — these mean the system needs a change (smaller blocks, more questions, more sleep), not more pressure.

## A note on resources

You do not need nineteen sets of material. For each subject you need exactly three things: one source to learn from (your MBBS textbooks — Harrison, Robbins, Ganong, Park and their companions — used as reference), one place to practise (topic-wise questions, solved alongside reading, not after), and one record of your errors. More sources than that usually means less studying, spread thinner. As your preparation matures, full papers from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) and the [grand test series](/tests/) take over as the main instrument, because they test all 19 subjects in the proportions the real exam uses — and their analysis tells you which of your nineteen needs the next hour.

Nineteen subjects, one paper, a pass mark of 150: the candidates who manage this without burning out are the ones who turned the syllabus into a sequence, kept questions and revision running daily, and rested on schedule. Start by grouping your own plan today — and take the first subject's test on MedSetu when you close it, so your system begins with evidence instead of hope.

Q: In what order should I study the 19 subjects for FMGE?
A: A weightage-first sequence works well: Medicine, Surgery with Orthopaedics, OBG, Community Medicine, then Pathology, Microbiology and Pharmacology, then Paediatrics, Ophthalmology and ENT, the pre-clinical high-yield cores, and finally the small subjects — Forensic Medicine, Radiology, Psychiatry, Dermatology and Anaesthesia.
Q: How can I remember early subjects while studying later ones for FMGE?
A: Build revision cycles into the plan: revise each topic within days and again within weeks, and include a daily mixed question set from earlier subjects. Small, regular contact keeps early material warm without full re-reading.
Q: Which FMGE subjects should be studied together?
A: Pairs with real overlap: Surgery with Orthopaedics, Physiology before or alongside Medicine, and Pharmacology alongside the clinical subjects, since drug questions recur inside Medicine, Anaesthesia and Psychiatry.
Q: How do I avoid burnout during FMGE preparation?
A: Cap daily hours at a sustainable level, alternate reading with question practice, take one lighter day a week without guilt, protect sleep, and treat falling practice scores or persistent dread as signals to change the system — smaller blocks, more active work — rather than to add pressure.
