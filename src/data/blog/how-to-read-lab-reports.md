Every ward round begins with the same ritual: someone opens a report and reads out numbers. Learning **how to read lab reports CBC** panels, liver tests and kidney tests turn on is not about memorising ranges — it is about knowing what each test measures, recognising patterns, and understanding that a flag is a question, not a diagnosis. Laboratories mark values H or L against a reference range, but about one in twenty results from a healthy person falls outside any given range by statistics alone. This guide teaches the pattern-based approach clinicians actually use. It is educational only and is not a way to diagnose yourself or any individual patient.

## Start With the Patient, Not the Printout

Before reading a single value, ask four questions. Who is this patient, and what question was the test meant to answer? When was the sample taken, and was it taken correctly — a haemolysed sample, for instance, falsely raises potassium? What do the **previous results** show, because a trend usually matters more than one value? And do the numbers fit the clinical picture, or is a result so unexpected that a laboratory or sampling error is more likely than a new disease? A creatinine that doubles overnight in a well patient, or a potassium incompatible with life in a patient chatting comfortably, should be repeated before it is believed.

Remember also that reference ranges differ between laboratories and methods. Always read the range printed on that report rather than reciting one from memory.

## How to Read Lab Reports CBC: The Complete Blood Count

The **CBC** describes the three cell lines. Read it in order:

- **Haemoglobin (Hb):** the oxygen-carrying protein. Low Hb is **anaemia** — then look at the indices to classify it. Typical adult ranges are roughly 13–17 g/dL in men and 12–15.5 g/dL in women, varying by laboratory.
- **MCV (mean corpuscular volume):** average red cell size. Low MCV (microcytic) most commonly reflects iron deficiency or thalassaemia trait; high MCV (macrocytic) suggests vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol, or certain drugs.
- **Total WBC count and differential:** raised neutrophils broadly suggest bacterial infection or inflammation; raised lymphocytes are more typical of viral infections; eosinophilia points towards allergy or parasitic disease. A very low count raises concern for marrow suppression or severe sepsis.
- **Platelets:** low platelets (thrombocytopenia) increase bleeding risk and have causes from immune destruction to marrow failure and consumption; high platelets are often reactive to inflammation, infection or iron deficiency.
- **ESR and CRP** (often reported alongside): non-specific markers of inflammation. They support a clinical suspicion and track response; they do not name the disease.

The skill is combining lines: microcytic anaemia with low platelets and a high WBC is a different problem from an isolated microcytic anaemia. ## Liver Function Tests: Patterns, Not Just Numbers

The standard **LFT** panel mixes markers of liver cell injury, bile flow and true synthetic function — separating them is the whole game.

- **ALT and AST (transaminases):** enzymes released when liver cells are injured. Marked elevation (many times the upper limit) suggests acute hepatitis — viral, drug-induced or ischaemic. Modest elevation is seen in fatty liver and many chronic conditions. AST also comes from muscle and heart, so it is less liver-specific than ALT.
- **ALP and GGT:** ALP rises when bile flow is obstructed (and also from bone and in pregnancy); GGT rises with obstruction and with alcohol and enzyme-inducing drugs. A predominantly ALP/GGT rise is a **cholestatic** pattern; a predominantly ALT/AST rise is a **hepatocellular** pattern.
- **Bilirubin:** high bilirubin causes jaundice. Broadly, unconjugated elevation suggests overproduction (haemolysis) or impaired conjugation, while conjugated elevation suggests obstruction or liver cell dysfunction.
- **Albumin and PT/INR:** these reflect what the liver **makes**. Low albumin and a prolonged prothrombin time indicate impaired synthetic function and carry more prognostic weight than enzyme levels — though albumin also falls with inflammation, malnutrition and protein loss.

## Renal Function Tests and Electrolytes

**RFTs** centre on **urea and creatinine**, interpreted with electrolytes and the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Creatinine is produced by muscle and cleared by the kidneys, so it rises as kidney function falls — but it also depends on muscle mass, so a small elderly patient can have significant impairment with a "normal-looking" creatinine, which is why eGFR is reported. Urea rises with dehydration, high protein intake and gastrointestinal bleeding as well as kidney failure. The pattern of the rise, urine output and urinalysis separate pre-renal causes (such as volume depletion) from intrinsic kidney disease and obstruction.

Of the electrolytes, **potassium** demands the most respect: both high and low values can disturb cardiac rhythm, and high potassium with ECG changes is an emergency. **Sodium** disturbances are usually disorders of water balance rather than salt, and are corrected slowly and carefully. Calcium, phosphate and bicarbonate complete the picture in chronic kidney disease and acid–base disorders.

## A Five-Step Routine for Any Report

- Confirm the patient, the date and the test requested.
- Read every value against the printed range; note which are flagged and by how much.
- Group the flags into a **pattern** (hepatocellular or cholestatic; microcytic or macrocytic).
- Compare with previous results and with the clinical findings.
- Decide the action: repeat, monitor, investigate further, or escalate — and document your reasoning.

Laboratory fluency is built by reviewing real reports on rounds and asking "what pattern is this?" every time. Q: What does a CBC test include?
A: A complete blood count reports haemoglobin, red cell indices such as MCV, total and differential white cell counts, and platelet count — a summary of all three blood cell lines.
Q: What is the difference between hepatocellular and cholestatic LFT patterns?
A: A hepatocellular pattern shows predominantly raised ALT and AST (liver cell injury), while a cholestatic pattern shows predominantly raised ALP and GGT (impaired bile flow or obstruction).
Q: Why is creatinine alone not enough to judge kidney function?
A: Creatinine depends on muscle mass as well as kidney clearance, so eGFR is reported alongside it to give a better estimate of filtration, especially in elderly or small patients.
Q: Does one abnormal value mean disease?
A: No. Reference ranges are set so a small proportion of healthy people fall outside them; values are interpreted as patterns, with trends over time and the clinical picture.
