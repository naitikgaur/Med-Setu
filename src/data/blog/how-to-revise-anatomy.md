Students asking **how to revise anatomy** usually share the same frustration: they read a region carefully, understood it at the time, and three weeks later cannot reconstruct it. The problem is the method, not the memory. Anatomy is spatial and relational — where things run, what they cross, what lies medial to what — and text-only revision barely exercises that kind of knowledge. Revise anatomy the way it is examined: with diagrams you produce yourself, tables that force comparisons, and practice spread over time.

## Anatomy Is a Picture Subject — Revise It That Way

A paragraph describing the brachial plexus and a diagram of the brachial plexus are not two versions of the same knowledge. In the exam, questions arrive as clinical scenarios — a fracture, a nerve injury, a surgical approach — and answering them requires a mental map you can interrogate: *the fracture is here, so which structure is at risk?*

Reading builds recognition of diagrams; it does not build the map. The test is simple: close the book and draw the plexus, the inguinal canal, the relations of the kidney. If you cannot draw it roughly, you do not know it yet in the form the exam needs — however familiar the page looks. This gap between recognition and recall is where most anatomy marks are lost.

## How to Revise Anatomy With Draw-and-Label Practice

You do not need artistic skill. You need repeated, imperfect, self-corrected drawing.

- **Draw from memory first, check second.** Sketch the structure with labels, then compare against the atlas and correct in a different colour. The corrections are the learning — they show you precisely which branches, relations or boundaries you had wrong.
- **Keep drawings schematic.** Boxes, lines and arrows beat beautiful artwork. A root–trunk–division–cord diagram you can reproduce in ninety seconds is worth more than a copied plate.
- **Attach one clinical hook to every diagram.** Erb's palsy on the plexus, midshaft humerus fracture on the radial nerve diagram, the hernia on the canal diagram. FMGE anatomy is clinical anatomy; the hook is often the actual question.
- **Revisit drawings on a spacing schedule.** Redraw the same diagram after a few days, then a couple of weeks. Each redraw should be faster and more accurate; when it is not, that region goes back into rotation.

## Tables for the Facts That Are Not Pictures

Not all of anatomy is drawable. Muscle attachments, nerve root values, derivatives of germ layers and pharyngeal arches, ossification centres and the boundaries of triangles and fossae are list knowledge — and lists are best handled in comparison tables you build yourself.

Build tables that force the comparisons exams love: muscles of a compartment side by side (nerve supply and action in adjacent columns, because "which muscle is the exception?" is a standard question shape); cranial nerves in one grid (foramina, functions, the one-line clinical test); dermatomes against peripheral nerve territories, which students confuse constantly. Keep each table to one page and revise it by covering a column and recalling down it — a table you only read is a table you only recognise.

Embryology and neuroanatomy deserve a special note: both are high-yield and both punish pure memorisation. For embryology, revise as timelines and fate maps — what becomes what, and what happens when it goes wrong. For neuroanatomy, pathways drawn as flow diagrams (origin → tract → decussation → destination → lesion effect) convert the most confusing chapter in the subject into a set of reproducible sketches.

One more format deserves a place in your rotation: **clinical and imaging formats**. A growing share of anatomy questions present a scenario — a stab wound, a fracture pattern, a post-surgical deficit — or an image: a radiograph, a CT slice, a labelled diagram with one structure arrowed. Revise for the format you will face. Once a week, take a topic you know and deliberately re-ask it clinically: not "what is the course of the radial nerve?" but "a patient cannot extend the wrist after a humeral shaft fracture — what is injured, and what still works?" Then check a cross-section or radiograph of a region you have drawn, and name every structure you can before looking at the key. Students are often surprised how much "known" anatomy evaporates in an unfamiliar format; finding that out in October is a gift, in the exam hall it is not.

## Spacing and Testing: Making It Survive Until the Exam

Anatomy studied in one block in first year must survive, for FMGE, alongside eighteen other subjects competing for revision time. Two habits protect it:

- **Short, frequent redraws.** Fifteen minutes of diagram recall three times a week maintains a region better than a monthly three-hour re-read. Distribute the practice; memory research consistently favours spacing over massing.
- **Question-led gap finding.** A topic-wise set from a [question bank](/qbank/) — vessels of the lower limb, say, or the cranial nerves — will find your weak regions faster than your own judgement will. We are poor judges of what we know; questions are not.
- **Interleave regions.** Mix upper limb, thorax and neuroanatomy across a week instead of finishing one region completely. Interleaving feels harder and works better, because every session begins with genuine recall rather than warm familiarity.

Use [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) periodically to check whether the knowledge holds together at subject level and under time pressure, not just topic by topic at your desk.

Q: Is drawing diagrams really necessary, or can I just revise from an atlas?
A: Revising from an atlas builds recognition, which fails when a question describes an injury and expects you to reconstruct the anatomy yourself. Drawing from memory — then correcting against the atlas — is what converts recognition into recall. The drawings can be rough; the attempt is the point.
Q: Which anatomy topics are highest yield for FMGE?
A: Neuroanatomy, embryology, and the clinically loaded regions — brachial plexus and upper limb nerve injuries, lower limb vessels and nerves, head and neck triangles and cranial nerves, and perineum — are asked repeatedly. Confirm the pattern yourself in previous-year papers rather than trusting any list, including this one.
Q: How do I revise muscle attachments and nerve root values?
A: Build one-page comparison tables grouped by compartment, and revise by covering a column and recalling down it. Attach each muscle group to its nerve and one clinical test so the facts hang on a story instead of floating free.
Q: How often should anatomy be revised before FMGE?
A: Little and often: short diagram-recall sessions several times a week, rotating regions, plus topic-wise question sets to expose gaps. Anatomy decays like everything else; spacing the contact is what keeps first-year material alive.
