Surgery is where FMGE starts feeling like a clinical exam rather than a memory test. **Surgery for FMGE** carries **32 questions** — the second-largest block in the paper — and its allied subjects, orthopaedics (5) and anaesthesiology (5), are examined in the same spirit. Surgical questions are built around presentations: an abdomen that is rigid, a swelling that transilluminates, a trauma patient who is deteriorating. Students who study surgery as lists of operations underperform; students who study it as *presentations, investigations and management steps* find the questions surprisingly fair. This guide shows you how to organise surgery that way — the high-yield areas, the books that fit, and a revision plan that respects the subject's size.

## Surgery for FMGE: weightage and question style

The FMGE by **NBEMS** consists of 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, **no negative marking**, and a pass mark of **150 out of 300**. Surgery contributes 32 questions, typically:

- **Clinical vignettes** — presentation first, then the most likely diagnosis, the investigation of choice, or the next management step
- **Image-based questions** — clinical photographs of swellings and ulcers, X-rays, CT images, barium studies and endoscopic views
- **Trauma and emergency surgery** — ATLS-style priorities, shock, burns assessment, head injury basics
- **Classic signs and eponyms** — Murphy's, Rovsing's, Courvoisier's law, and the named signs examiners have always loved
- **Oncology basics** — staging concepts, tumour markers and management principles of common cancers (breast, colon, thyroid, stomach)

The through-line: surgery questions test whether you can move from a presentation to a decision. Prepare in that direction.

## High-yield areas

- **GI surgery** — acute abdomen causes, appendicitis, intestinal obstruction, perforation, hernias and their complications, gallstone disease and obstructive jaundice, colorectal cancer
- **Trauma** — primary survey priorities, types of shock and their recognition, burns (assessment of area and depth), head and chest injuries
- **Breast and endocrine surgery** — breast lump approach and carcinoma, thyroid swellings and thyroid cancer types, parathyroid and adrenal surgical basics
- **Hepatobiliary and pancreas** — liver abscess, hydatid cyst, portal hypertension surgical aspects, acute and chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic tumours
- **Urology basics** — BPH, renal and ureteric stones, bladder and prostate cancer essentials, testicular torsion vs epididymo-orchitis
- **Vascular and general** — varicose veins, peripheral arterial disease, DVT, wound types and healing, suture and drain one-liners, preoperative assessment basics
- **Paediatric surgery favourites** — intussusception, hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, Hirschsprung disease, common congenital anomalies

## Books at FMGE depth

- **Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery** — the classic surgical text; superb clinical photographs (directly useful for image questions) and presentation-based chapters
- **S. Das — A Manual on Clinical Surgery / Textbook of Surgery** — excellent for clinical examination methods, signs and the differential-diagnosis approach FMGE vignettes assume
- **SRB's Manual of Surgery** — popular for its exam orientation and concise coverage if Bailey & Love feels too large for your timeline

Use one core text. Surgery has the same trap as medicine: three half-read books lose to one properly read book plus questions.

## The presentation-based method

- **Build presentation grids** — for acute abdomen, dysphagia, jaundice, breast lump, neck swelling, haematuria, and leg ulcer: causes, discriminating features, first investigation, definitive investigation, management outline
- **Learn investigations in pairs** — "first test" vs "gold standard" differ often in surgery (ultrasound first, MRCP/ERCP definitive); examiners exploit exactly that gap
- **Study images deliberately** — Bailey & Love's clinical photos and your text's radiology plates: practise naming what you see before reading the caption
- **Attach the anatomy** — surgical questions quietly test anatomy (hernia contents, triangles, blood supply); revise the relevant anatomy alongside each surgical topic, which also feeds your anatomy marks
- **Solve vignettes early** — work topic-wise from a [question bank](/qbank/) after each system; surgical stems reward students who have practised moving from story to decision

## Revision plan

- **First pass:** system-wise reading with presentation grids; image practice alongside; question set after each system
- **Second pass:** grids, classic signs list, staging and marker tables; timed surgery tests from the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/)
- **Final phase:** integrated clinical revision with medicine and paediatrics, trauma one-liners, and full 300-question stamina work in the [grand test series](/tests/) and [previous-year papers](/papers/)

## Common mistakes that cost surgery marks

- **Memorising operations instead of presentations** — the paper rarely asks you to describe a procedure step by step; it asks what this patient has, what test comes first, and what must be done now. Students whose notes are operation lists are preparing for a different exam
- **Confusing "first investigation" with "best investigation"** — this single distinction accounts for a steady stream of lost marks across GI, urology and trauma questions. For every major presentation, fix both answers separately and note where they differ
- **Ignoring trauma because it feels like a small chapter** — shock recognition, burns assessment and primary-survey priorities are compact, algorithmic and repeatedly examined; they are among the cheapest marks in the subject
- **Skipping the images** — surgery is the most photograph-driven subject in the paper. If your preparation never included looking at clinical pictures and naming the lesion, the image questions become coin flips
