If you are a foreign medical graduate wondering **how to prepare for FMGE**, start with one clear fact: this is a screening test, not a ranking exam. You do not need to beat other candidates — you need to score **150 out of 300**. Everything in your preparation should be built around that single target. This guide covers the exact pattern, the subject weightage, a phased plan, the right resources, and the revision method that turns studied material into marks.

## Know the exam before you open a book

The Foreign Medical Graduate Examination (FMGE) is conducted by the **National Board of Examinations in Medical Sciences (NBEMS)** twice a year for Indian citizens and OCIs who earned their medical qualification abroad and want to practise in India. The paper pattern is fixed and worth memorising:

- **300 multiple-choice questions**, each with four options and one correct answer
- Two parts — **Part A (150 questions)** and **Part B (150 questions)** — taken the same day with a break in between
- **+1 mark** for every correct answer and **no negative marking**
- **Pass mark: 150 out of 300** — exactly 50%

No negative marking changes your strategy completely. An unanswered question is a guaranteed zero, while a guessed question has a real chance of scoring. In the exam hall, **attempt all 300 questions** — this is not optional advice, it is arithmetic.

## How to prepare for FMGE: follow the subject weightage

FMGE does not draw equally from all 19 subjects. The official distribution per 300 questions is the single most useful planning document you have:

- **Medicine — 33** | **Surgery — 32** | **Obstetrics & Gynaecology — 30** | **Community Medicine — 30**
- **Anatomy, Physiology, Biochemistry — 17 each** | **Paediatrics, Ophthalmology, ENT — 15 each**
- **Pathology, Microbiology, Pharmacology — 13 each** | **Forensic Medicine — 10** | **Radiology — 10**
- **Psychiatry, Dermatology, Anaesthesia, Orthopaedics — 5 each**

Add it up and a pattern emerges: the four big clinical subjects alone give you **125 questions — nearly 42% of the paper**. A candidate who is genuinely strong in Medicine, Surgery, OBG and Community Medicine, and average everywhere else, is already close to the pass mark. Weightage does not mean you skip small subjects — 5-mark subjects like Psychiatry are cheap marks because the syllabus is small — but it does decide where your hours go first.

## A phased plan that fits real life

Most candidates have somewhere between three and six months. Whatever your window, the phases stay the same; only their length changes.

### Phase 1: First reading with questions alongside

Go subject by subject, in weightage order. For every subject, read the theory and solve topic-wise questions the same week — never "theory first, questions later", because questions teach you what the theory was actually for. Standard textbooks you already own from your MBBS years (Harrison for Medicine, Robbins for Pathology, Ganong for Physiology, Park for Community Medicine) are for reference and depth, not for cover-to-cover reading at this stage.

### Phase 2: Previous-year papers and grand tests

Once the major subjects are done, start sitting full papers under exam conditions. Working through [previous-year papers](/papers/) shows you the real difficulty level and the repeated themes — FMGE recycles concepts heavily even when the exact wording changes. Alongside papers, a structured [grand test series](/tests/) trains the two skills a textbook cannot: stamina across 300 questions and time judgement across two sittings.

### Phase 3: Revision only

The last three to four weeks are not for new material. Cycle back through your notes, your marked questions and your mistakes. A topic you have revised three times beats a new topic read once — in a 50%-to-pass exam, depth in known areas is worth more than shallow coverage of everything.

## Mock tests: the part most candidates underuse

NBEMS publishes results every session, and historically a minority of candidates pass. The gap is rarely knowledge alone — it is usually exam behaviour. Mock tests fix behaviour. Sit them at the same time of day as the real exam, in one sitting, without pausing. Afterwards, spend as long analysing as you spent writing: classify every wrong answer as a **knowledge gap**, a **silly mistake**, or a **guess that failed**. Knowledge gaps go back into revision; silly mistakes are fixed by slowing down on easy questions; failed guesses are simply the cost of the no-negative-marking system and nothing to mourn.

Use [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) during Phase 1 and 2 to check each subject as you finish it, and reserve full mocks for measuring overall readiness. Your mock scores will tell you the truth your feelings won't: if you are consistently near or above 150 in realistic conditions, you are ready.

## Common mistakes that cost the pass mark

- **Reading without solving.** FMGE is an MCQ exam; recognition memory built by solving questions is what the hall rewards.
- **Ignoring small subjects.** Psychiatry, Dermatology, Anaesthesia and Orthopaedics together are 20 questions from compact syllabi — often the cheapest 20 marks in the paper.
- **Leaving questions blank.** With no negative marking, a blank answer is the only guaranteed loss.
- **One giant revision at the end.** Spaced, repeated revision beats a single heroic week.
- **Comparing yourself to rankers.** There is no rank here. There is a line at 150, and your only job is to cross it.

## Exam-day essentials

Reach the centre early, carry exactly the documents the admit card specifies, and use the break between Part A and Part B to reset — do not spend it discussing questions with other candidates. In each part, do a first pass answering what you know, flag the doubtful ones, and return to them; finish by making an educated attempt on everything remaining. Manage the clock per block of 50 questions rather than per question.

FMGE preparation is a long, unglamorous project, but it is a winnable one: a fixed pattern, a known weightage, and a pass mark of exactly half.
