Ask a room of medical students about **group study vs self study** and you will start an argument, because both sides are defending a real experience: the group session that finally made renal physiology click, and the group session that produced four hours of tea and twelve pages. The honest answer is that group study and self study are not rivals for the same job. They do different work. Self-study is where knowledge is built and tested; group study is where it is explained, challenged and repaired. Marks come from using each for what it is actually good at.

## What Self-Study Does Better

The core activities that produce exam marks are solitary by nature. **Active recall** — closing the book and retrieving — happens inside one head. So does honest self-testing: a timed set of questions cannot be shared, and neither can the discovery of what *you*, specifically, do not know.

Self-study also controls pace and targeting. Your weak area might be cranial nerves while your friend's is antimicrobials; alone, each of you spends the hour on your own gap. In a group, the hour goes to the group's average gap, which is nobody's. And self-study is where the unglamorous volume gets done — the spaced repetitions, the question sets, the mistake-logging — on a schedule the forgetting curve demands, not a schedule five people's calendars permit.

Its weaknesses are equally real. Alone, you cannot hear your own wrong explanations. A misconception rehearsed privately for weeks becomes fluent, confident error. And motivation, for most humans, is harder to sustain in isolation across a preparation that lasts a year or more.

## What Group Study Does Better

A good group does three things self-study cannot:

- **It forces explanation.** Teaching a topic to peers — the so-called protégé effect — exposes every soft spot in your understanding within minutes. If you cannot explain the coagulation cascade simply, you do not own it, and no amount of private re-reading would have told you so bluntly.
- **It pools blind spots.** Different students notice different high-yield angles, remember different exceptions, and bring questions the others would never have thought to ask. In clinical subjects especially, discussing how a vignette could be twisted is genuine exam training.
- **It stress-tests under social pressure.** Answering aloud, on the spot, with peers listening, is closer to the psychology of an exam hall than silent reading — and it is very good practice for vivas.

Its failure modes are just as consistent: sessions drift to the group's favourite topics rather than anyone's weak ones; confident wrong answers go uncorrected and get absorbed by everyone; the pace is set by the slowest explainer; and "studying together" quietly becomes socialising with books open — pleasant, restorative even, but not revision, and dishonest to log as such.

## Group Study vs Self Study: A Workable Division of Labour

Treat the two modes as shifts in a factory, each with assigned work:

- **Self-study (the large majority of hours):** first learning of topics, spaced recall, question sets from a [question bank](/qbank/), timed papers, mistake notebook upkeep. This is where your personal syllabus gets covered and your personal gaps get found.
- **Group study (a few scheduled hours a week):** only topics already studied alone. Each member teaches one assigned topic, brings five self-made questions, and the group works through disagreements. Nothing is *first-learned* in the group — first learning is too pace-sensitive to share.
- **Ground rules that keep groups honest:** fixed start and end times, phones away, a written agenda of topics agreed the day before, and a rule that any factual dispute is settled by opening a book or testing it against questions — never by seniority or confidence.

A useful test for any session, either mode: afterwards, can you list what you can now do that you could not do before? If the honest answer is "nothing specific", the session was social or passive, whatever it was called.

One modern variant deserves a mention: the online study group — a small circle on a call or a chat thread, often of friends preparing from different cities. Everything above applies unchanged, plus one advantage and one risk. The advantage is accountability at a distance: a fixed daily check-in ("sets done, scores posted") sustains solo work remarkably well, because your self-study is being witnessed. The risk is that an always-open chat becomes always-open distraction, and that shared documents quietly replace personal notes — a folder of other people's summaries is not your revision. Keep the group small, keep the check-ins brief and factual, and keep the actual studying stubbornly your own. Used that way, an online group is not a compromise between the two modes; it is the discipline of group study wrapped around the substance of self-study.

## What This Means for FMGE Preparation

FMGE is a single-candidate, 300-question, computer-based exam — the performance itself is solo, and the bulk of preparation should be too. But a preparation spanning months benefits from one or two structured group sessions a week: teach-backs of the week's hardest topics, and joint review of a [grand test series](/tests/) paper everyone has already attempted alone. Attempting alone first is non-negotiable — it is what gives the group real errors to work on instead of shared impressions.

Q: Is group study or self-study better for medical exams?
A: Self-study is better for the core work — first learning, recall practice, timed testing and fixing your personal gaps. Group study is better for explaining, catching misconceptions and pooling questions. Most toppers use both, with self-study taking the large majority of hours.
Q: How many hours of group study per week is ideal?
A: For most students, two to four structured hours a week is enough — one or two sessions with an agenda, on topics everyone has already studied alone. Beyond that, group time usually starts replacing the solo practice that produces marks.
Q: Why does my group study session feel productive but my test scores don't improve?
A: Probably because the session ran on recognition and discussion rather than retrieval. Listening to a good explanation feels like learning; only answering questions proves it. End every group session with a short test, and attempt papers alone before discussing them.
Q: What is the best group size for studying medicine?
A: Three to five members. Smaller groups drift into conversation; larger ones let members hide. Everyone should teach and everyone should be questioned in every session — if someone can stay silent for two hours, the group is too big or unstructured.
