**Orthopaedics for FMGE** carries just **5 questions out of 300** in the NBEMS paper — two parts of 150 MCQs, no negative marking, pass mark 150. Five questions does not justify a week of study, but it does justify a sharp half-day: the subject's examinable list is short and remarkably stable. The same named fractures, the same classic deformities and the same complications recur across sessions. This guide tells you exactly where those 5 questions come from and how little you need to cover to take most of them.

## Orthopaedics for FMGE: fractures first — the centre of the subject

Most orthopaedics questions are fracture questions, and most fracture questions test one of four things — the classic presentation, the named complication, the investigation or the first management step. Learn each major fracture as a four-point card:

- **Colles' fracture** — fall on an outstretched hand, dinner-fork deformity, distal radius
- **Supracondylar fracture of the humerus** — the child with a fall, and the feared complications: brachial artery injury and Volkmann's ischaemic contracture
- **Fracture neck of femur** — the elderly patient, shortened and externally rotated limb, Garden classification principles
- **Monteggia and Galeazzi fractures** — the fracture-dislocation pairs of the forearm; examiners test which bone and which joint together
- **Spine and pelvis injuries** — recognition and the priority of immobilisation and associated injuries

Add the general fracture principles: classification by pattern, open versus closed, the complications timeline (immediate, early, late), compartment syndrome and its cardinal signs, and fat embolism syndrome after long-bone fractures.

## Dislocations and soft-tissue injuries

- **Shoulder dislocation** — anterior dislocation as the commonest, its presentation and complications such as axillary nerve injury
- **Hip dislocation** — posterior dislocation with the flexed, adducted, internally rotated limb and sciatic nerve risk
- **Recurrent dislocation and common sports injuries** — the structures damaged in anterior cruciate and meniscal injuries of the knee

These are image- and description-friendly topics: a limb position described in one line is often the whole question.

## Bone tumours, infection and the classic signs

- **Osteosarcoma and Ewing's sarcoma** — age groups, metaphyseal location, sunburst appearance and Codman's triangle versus onion-peel periosteal reaction
- **Osteomyelitis** — the acute haematogenous picture in a child, the metaphysis, and chronic disease with sequestrum and involucrum
- **Skeletal tuberculosis** — Pott's spine as the commonest site and its presentation
- **Named signs and tests** — and the common metabolic and congenital conditions in outline: rickets, osteogenesis imperfecta, congenital hip dislocation screening

## How much study is enough?

**Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics** is the standard concise text and is genuinely sufficient for this subject at FMGE level. The efficient method is a single pass through fractures, dislocations and tumours with a card for each — presentation, complication, first step — followed by question practice. Do not let orthopaedics expand: beyond the classic list, returns fall quickly, and those hours earn more in Pharmacology or Community Medicine. But do not skip it either — 5 predictable questions are worth roughly a full percentage point of the paper, and the pass mark is a hard line at 150. For a candidate sitting near that line, this small subject can be the difference between clearing the examination and repeating the entire cycle, which makes its short list some of the cheapest insurance in the whole syllabus.

## Paediatric orthopaedics and joint disease

Two smaller areas complete the classic list. **Paediatric orthopaedics** contributes developmental dysplasia of the hip and its screening, Perthes disease and slipped capital femoral epiphysis — separated by age and body habitus in the stem — and clubfoot (CTEV) with its component deformities. **Joint disease** contributes the orthopaedic face of osteoarthritis versus rheumatoid arthritis at outline level, septic arthritis as the emergency (the hot joint in a child is aspirated, not observed), and gout's classic presentation. Add **osteoporosis and osteomalacia** in a few lines each, shared with Medicine, and the amputation and traction vocabulary that appears inside Surgery questions — skin versus skeletal traction and their classic indications.

## Reading orthopaedics questions well

Orthopaedic stems reward a fixed reading order: age, mechanism of injury, limb position or deformity, then neurovascular status. The mechanism alone often names the fracture — fall on an outstretched hand, dashboard injury, twisting injury in sport. The deformity is frequently the diagnosis in disguise: dinner-fork, garden-spade, mallet finger. And when a stem mentions a nerve deficit after a fracture, the question has usually become a complication question, so switch your thinking from "which fracture" to "which structure". Practising twenty or thirty stems with this reading order builds the reflex quickly, which is why question practice, not re-reading, is the right finishing step for this subject.

## What to skip without guilt

Lean also means knowing what to leave. Detailed classifications beyond the headline systems, implant and prosthesis details, operative technique steps, and rare congenital syndromes sit below the FMGE waterline for this subject — a passing familiarity is enough. The same applies to elaborate physiotherapy and rehabilitation protocols. If a topic cannot be summarised as a presentation, a complication or a first step, it is probably not where the 5 questions live. Spend the saved time where the blueprint pays more, and let orthopaedics be what it is: a small, well-mapped territory you can hold completely rather than a large one you hold partially.
