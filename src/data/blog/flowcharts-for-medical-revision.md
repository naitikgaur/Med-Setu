Open any medical textbook chapter and you will find paragraphs. Sit any medical exam and you will find something else: a patient, a decision, a next step. Flowcharts for medical revision exist to close that gap. A flowchart stores a topic the way the exam asks it — as a pathway with decision points — while a table stores it the way differentials actually work: side by side, feature by feature. Converting your revision into these two formats is slower than re-reading a chapter once, and dramatically faster every time after that.

## Flowcharts for Medical Revision: Why the Format Fits the Subject

A large part of medicine is genuinely algorithmic. A patient presents with chest pain; certain findings push you towards one pathway, other findings down another; investigations confirm or redirect; management follows in steps. A paragraph hides this structure inside prose. A **flowchart** makes it the whole point: boxes for states and findings, arrows for "leads to" and "do next", and clear branch points where one result changes everything downstream.

Tables do the complementary job. Whenever the exam's real task is *"which of these two is it?"* — and a remarkable share of questions reduce to exactly that — the knowledge is comparative. Two diseases share most features and differ in three. A table puts the three differences in neighbouring cells, where your eye and memory can work on them directly, instead of in two chapters forty pages apart.

There is also a memory argument. Material you have **organised** is easier to retrieve than material you have merely seen, because retrieval follows structure: remember the branch point and the branch follows. A one-page map of a topic gives memory a skeleton to hang the details on.

## Building a Diagnostic Flowchart

Start from the presentation, not from the disease. Exams begin with a patient, so your chart should too.

1. **Top box: the presenting problem.** "Breathlessness", "Jaundice", "Fever in a child" — the way a question opens.
2. **First split: the questions that divide the field.** Usually a small number of findings — history points, signs, one key investigation — separate the major causes. These are your branch points, and identifying them is the real learning.
3. **Follow each branch to its conditions.** Keep each box to a name and, at most, the one feature that clinches it.
4. **End with the confirmatory test or the first management step.** A branch that ends in a diagnosis but no action is half a chart.

Build the chart *after* studying the topic once, from memory where you can, and correct it against the book afterwards. The building is a study session in itself — you cannot draw a branch point you do not understand, which is precisely the point.

## Building Tables That Earn Their Space

Not everything deserves a table. Reserve them for genuine comparisons:

- **Differential tables:** conditions in columns, discriminating features in rows — age group, onset, pain character, the one investigation that separates them
- **Drug tables:** class, example, mechanism in four words, the adverse effect that gets asked
- **Criteria and staging tables:** the components of a score or stage, laid out so they can be covered and recalled row by row

Two disciplines make tables work. First, **only discriminating rows**: a feature that is identical across all columns wastes a row — delete it. Second, **fill them from questions as well as books**: every time a question catches you confusing two conditions, that pair gains a row in your table. Your tables, like your notes, should partly be built from your mistakes.

## Revising With Flowcharts and Tables

The powerful way to use a flowchart is to **redraw it blank**. Look at the topic title, draw the pathway from memory on a fresh sheet, then compare with your original and mark what was missing or misplaced. This is active recall in its purest form — retrieval, structure and feedback in one exercise — and the testing effect research is clear that this kind of effortful retrieval, checked against the source, is what durable memory is made of. Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing among the highest-utility techniques; redrawing a chart *is* a practice test you set yourself.

Tables revise the same way: cover one column and produce it from the other, or cover a row and name the feature across all conditions. Ten minutes of this beats an hour of looking at the page — looking feels fluent because the answers are visible, which is exactly the illusion that collapses in the exam hall.

One habit multiplies the value of every chart you own: keep them **alive**. A flowchart drawn in the first month and never touched again fossilises your early, partial understanding. After every test or question session, take two minutes to update the relevant chart — add the branch a question caught you on, correct the step you had in the wrong order, annotate the box where the examiner's favourite trap sits. Over a season, your charts quietly become records of your own error patterns, which is the most exam-relevant document you can own.

Q: What topics suit flowcharts best?
A: Anything with a pathway: diagnostic approaches to a presentation, management algorithms, physiological cascades, and stepwise procedures. If the topic has decision points — "if this, then that" — it will flowchart well.
Q: Are tables or flowcharts better for revision?
A: They do different jobs. Flowcharts store sequences and decisions; tables store comparisons. Most large topics need one of each — a pathway chart for the approach, and a differential table for the conditions along it.
Q: Should I copy flowcharts from textbooks or draw my own?
A: Draw your own, after one proper study of the topic, and correct it against the book. Building the chart forces you to find the branch points, which is where the learning happens; a copied chart gives you the picture without the understanding.
Q: How do I revise a flowchart effectively?
A: Redraw it from memory on a blank sheet, then compare and mark the gaps. Cover-and-recall beats looking at the finished chart, because retrieval with feedback is what fixes the pathway in memory.
