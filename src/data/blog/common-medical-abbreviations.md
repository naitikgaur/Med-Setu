Case sheets, discharge summaries and ward orders are written in shorthand, so fluency in **common medical abbreviations** is a survival skill for students: you cannot follow a round if "SOB, BD, IV, stat, NBM" reads like a foreign language. This article organises about a hundred of the abbreviations you will meet daily into four learnable groups. One warning runs through it: some traditional abbreviations are **error-prone and should not be used when you write** — recognising them is essential, copying them is not. When you prescribe, prefer full words; when you read, use this list to decode confidently.

## Common Medical Abbreviations for Frequency and Timing

These appear on almost every drug chart:

- **OD** — once daily (from Latin *omne in die*); many safety guides prefer writing "once daily" in full
- **BD / BID** — twice daily
- **TDS / TID** — three times daily
- **QID** — four times daily
- **HS** — at bedtime (error-prone; write "at night" instead)
- **stat** — immediately, as a single urgent dose
- **SOS** — when required / if needed (similar in use to PRN)
- **PRN** — as needed (*pro re nata*)
- **AC** — before meals (*ante cibum*)
- **PC** — after meals (*post cibum*)
- **Q4H, Q6H, Q8H** — every 4, 6 or 8 hours
- **alternate day** — often written QOD (error-prone; avoid, write it out)

Notice how many of these Latin forms look alike when handwritten. That is exactly why modern safety practice — including the ISMP list of error-prone abbreviations — pushes prescribers towards plain English. Decoding them is a reading skill; writing them is increasingly discouraged. The related safety habits are covered in depth alongside our [resources](/resources/) on prescription writing.

## Routes and Forms of Administration

- **PO** — by mouth (*per os*)
- **IV** — intravenous
- **IM** — intramuscular
- **SC / SQ** — subcutaneous
- **ID** — intradermal
- **PR** — per rectum
- **PV** — per vaginam
- **SL** — sublingual
- **NG** — nasogastric (tube feeding route)
- **inh** — by inhalation
- **top** — topical, applied to skin
- **gtt** — drops (*guttae*)
- **tab, cap, syr, inj** — tablet, capsule, syrup, injection
- **U** — units: **avoid writing this**; a handwritten U is misread as 0 and has caused tenfold insulin errors. Always write "units" in full.

## Clinical Shorthand You Will Hear on Rounds

- **SOB** — shortness of breath; **DOE** — dyspnoea on exertion
- **PND** — paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (also used for postnasal drip — context decides, which is why clarity matters)
- **CCF / HF** — congestive cardiac failure / heart failure
- **COPD, BA** — chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchial asthma
- **MI, ACS, NSTEMI, STEMI** — myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome, and its non-ST-elevation and ST-elevation forms
- **CVA** — cerebrovascular accident (stroke); **TIA** — transient ischaemic attack
- **DM, HTN** — diabetes mellitus, hypertension
- **CKD, AKI, ESRD** — chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, end-stage renal disease
- **UTI, LRTI, URTI** — urinary tract infection, lower and upper respiratory tract infections
- **PID** — pelvic inflammatory disease; **DUB / AUB** — (abnormal) uterine bleeding
- **NBM / NPO** — nil by mouth; **RTA** — road traffic accident
- **LOC** — loss of consciousness; **GCS** — Glasgow Coma Scale
- **DNR** — do not resuscitate (a formal documented decision, never casual shorthand)
- **LAMA** — left against medical advice; **OPD / IPD** — outpatient / inpatient department
- **H/O, C/O, K/C/O** — history of, complaining of, known case of
- **O/E** — on examination; **NAD** — no abnormality detected
- **BP, HR, RR, SpO2, Temp** — blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, temperature
- **JVP, CRT** — jugular venous pressure, capillary refill time

Build the habit of expanding any abbreviation the first time you write it in your own notes — "shortness of breath (SOB)" — until the meaning is automatic. Examiners in the [question bank](/qbank/) and ward teachers alike reward students who can translate shorthand into clear clinical language.

## Investigations and Reports

- **CBC** — complete blood count; **Hb, TLC, DLC, ESR** — haemoglobin, total and differential leucocyte counts, erythrocyte sedimentation rate
- **LFT, RFT / KFT** — liver and renal (kidney) function tests
- **ABG** — arterial blood gas; **VBG** — venous blood gas
- **ECG** — electrocardiogram; **ECHO** — echocardiography
- **CXR** — chest X-ray; **USG** — ultrasonography
- **CT, MRI, PET** — computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, positron emission tomography
- **FNAC** — fine-needle aspiration cytology; **LP** — lumbar puncture
- **RBS / FBS / PPBS** — random, fasting and postprandial blood sugar; **HbA1c** — glycated haemoglobin
- **PT / INR, APTT** — prothrombin time/international normalised ratio, activated partial thromboplastin time
- **CRP** — C-reactive protein; **WBC, RBC, MCV** — white/red cell counts and mean corpuscular volume
- **HIV, HBsAg, HCV** — common viral screening tests; **VDRL** — syphilis serology test
- **D/W, NS, RL** — dextrose in water, normal saline, Ringer's lactate (IV fluids)

## How to Learn Them — and When Not to Use Them

Learn abbreviations in context, not from a wall chart alone: keep a small notebook during postings, write down every new abbreviation you meet that day, expand it in full, and review the list weekly — spaced repetition will fix about a hundred terms within a single posting. Test yourself by reading a full case sheet and translating it aloud into plain language; if you can present the case without shorthand, you truly know it.

The professional rule is asymmetric: **read everything, write carefully.** In prescriptions and drug orders, avoid the flagged forms — U for units, QD/QOD, HS, handwritten µg — and write instructions in full. In your own study notes, shorthand is fine. In anything another human will act on at 2 a.m., clarity wins.

Q: What does BD mean in a prescription?
A: BD (also written BID) means the medicine is to be taken twice daily. TDS/TID means three times daily and QID four times daily.
Q: Which medical abbreviations are dangerous and should be avoided?
A: U for units (misread as 0), QD and QOD (confused with each other and with QID), HS, and handwritten µg for micrograms. Safety bodies such as the ISMP advise writing these instructions in full.
Q: What do stat and SOS mean?
A: Stat means give the dose immediately, once. SOS (similar to PRN) means give the medicine only when needed, within any limits prescribed.
Q: How can students learn medical abbreviations quickly?
A: Learn them in context during postings: note every new abbreviation daily, write its expansion, and review weekly; then practise translating whole case sheets into plain language.
