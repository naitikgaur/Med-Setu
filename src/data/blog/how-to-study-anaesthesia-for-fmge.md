**Anaesthesia for FMGE** carries **5 questions out of 300** in the NBEMS paper — two parts of 150 MCQs, no negative marking, pass mark 150. It is the smallest-feeling subject in the syllabus for many students because it is rarely taught as a long undergraduate block, yet its question list is short and heavily pharmacological. If your Pharmacology is in decent shape, anaesthesia is close to free marks: most of its core is the same drugs viewed from the operating-theatre side. This guide sets out the defined list those 5 questions come from.

## Anaesthetic agents: the drug core of anaesthesia for FMGE

- **Intravenous induction agents** — propofol, thiopentone, etomidate and ketamine: the agent of choice in classic situations, and each one's signature advantage or danger (ketamine and raised intracranial pressure debates aside, learn the standard textbook positions: etomidate's cardiovascular stability, propofol's day-case popularity, thiopentone's cerebral protection association)
- **Inhalational agents** — the concept of MAC (minimum alveolar concentration), blood–gas solubility and speed of induction, and the classic association of halothane with hepatitis and malignant hyperthermia triggers
- **Neuromuscular blockers** — depolarising versus non-depolarising agents, succinylcholine's rapid onset and its cautions, and reversal with neostigmine
- **Malignant hyperthermia** — triggers, the role of dantrolene, and its genetics in outline; a perennial one-question topic

Frame each agent as: class, one advantage, one danger, one classic use. That four-point frame answers most agent questions ever written. It also protects you against the commonest distractor pattern in this subject, where three options describe real properties of the named drug but belong to a different member of its class — the frame tells you which property actually belongs to the agent in the stem.

## Local anaesthesia and regional techniques

- **Local anaesthetic agents** — lidocaine and bupivacaine, the amide–ester split, maximum-dose concepts in qualitative terms, and local anaesthetic toxicity and its recognition
- **Spinal and epidural anaesthesia** — levels, complications such as post-dural-puncture headache and total spinal, and contraindications
- **Peripheral blocks** in outline only

Local anaesthetic questions overlap with Pharmacology's autonomic and local-anaesthesia teaching, so revise them together.

## Airway, monitoring and resuscitation

- **Airway management** — the airway devices ladder (oral airway, laryngeal mask airway, endotracheal tube), indications for intubation, and the difficult-airway mindset
- **Preoperative assessment** — ASA physical status grading and fasting guidelines in principle
- **Monitoring** — what pulse oximetry, capnography and ECG monitoring each tell you; capnography as the standard for confirming tube placement
- **Cardiopulmonary resuscitation** — the BLS sequence and priorities as taught in standard guidelines, and perioperative emergencies such as anaphylaxis recognition
- **Fluids and pain** — crystalloids versus colloids in outline, and the WHO analgesic ladder as the framework for postoperative pain

## Study method

Anaesthesia does not need a textbook of its own at FMGE level. Work from a concise set of notes or the anaesthesia chapters of a standard pharmacology text — **KDT (Tripathi's Essentials of Medical Pharmacology)** covers the agents thoroughly — and add the airway, ASA grading and monitoring facts from any undergraduate surgery or anaesthesia summary. One focused pass, a set of drug cards, and question practice will cover the subject. Because the overlap with Pharmacology is so large, schedule anaesthesia immediately after a pharmacology revision block and let the two consolidate each other.

## Perioperative medicine: the questions around the edges

The remaining questions come from the perioperative period rather than the agents themselves. Know the **preoperative** frame: history points that change anaesthetic risk, the significance of a difficult-airway history, and why recent food intake matters (aspiration risk). Know the **intraoperative** complications at recognition level: laryngospasm, bronchospasm, hypotension under spinal anaesthesia from sympathetic blockade, and the signs of inadequate depth. Know the **postoperative** list: nausea and vomiting and its risk factors, shivering, delayed recovery and its common causes, and postoperative pain assessment. Oxygen therapy deserves its own lines — delivery devices in outline, what a Venturi mask does differently, and the caution with high-flow oxygen in chronic CO2 retainers, shared with Medicine. Blood transfusion basics — cross-matching, the major transfusion reactions and their first response — sit on the border with Pathology and Surgery and appear steadily.

## Anaesthesia one-liners worth a page

Finally, keep a page of pure associations, the form in which this subject is most often asked: the gas in the blue cylinder and the colour codes of medical gas cylinders as taught in Indian examinations, the fastest and shortest-acting agents in each class, the agent avoided in porphyria or epilepsy debates at textbook level, the antidotes and reversal agents (naloxone for opioids, flumazenil for benzodiazepines, sugammadex in outline for non-depolarising blockade), and the day-care anaesthesia criteria in principle. One page, revised three times, is a fair price for a visible share of this subject's five questions.

## The last-week page

Condense the subject onto one page: the induction agents as a four-row table (agent, advantage, danger, classic use), the inhalational facts around MAC, the two muscle-relaxant classes with their reversal, malignant hyperthermia in three lines, local anaesthetic toxicity, spinal complications, the airway ladder, and ASA grades. Anaesthesia questions are drawn from a visibly small pool, so this page genuinely covers a large fraction of what can be asked. Revise it alongside your pharmacology last-week pages — the two subjects share so much that a combined sitting costs barely more than either alone, and the cross-links make both sets of facts stick better.
