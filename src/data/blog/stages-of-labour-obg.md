If there is one obstetrics topic you can revise the night before the exam and almost certainly meet again, it is the **stages of labour fmge** questions built around it. Examiners return to it because it is simple to state, easy to test in a clinical vignette, and closely linked to the partograph, prolonged labour, and postpartum haemorrhage. A stem may describe a primigravida pushing for a long time, a cervix that has stopped dilating, or bleeding after delivery of the placenta, and expect you to identify the stage first. Once the stage is clear, the rest of the question usually becomes straightforward. This guide follows standard textbooks such as Dutta's Obstetrics, with the durations and definitions FMGE expects.

## Why Stages of Labour FMGE Questions Repeat Every Year

Labour connects normal physiology with emergencies. The same definitions that describe a normal delivery also define **prolonged labour** and the timing of interventions, which makes the topic ideal for single-best-answer questions.

Examiners commonly test:

- The exact start and end point of each stage
- Normal duration in a **primigravida** and a **multigravida**
- Which finding belongs to which stage in a clinical stem
- Partograph interpretation, especially the **alert line** and **action line**
- The components of **active management of the third stage of labour**
- Complications of a prolonged second or third stage

Learn the boundaries precisely. Many wrong options are simply the correct fact placed in the wrong stage.

## The Four Stages at a Glance

**Labour** is the process by which the fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled through the birth canal. True labour is characterised by regular, painful uterine contractions that increase in frequency and intensity, with progressive dilatation and effacement of the cervix, often with a show.

- **First stage:** From the onset of true labour pains to full dilatation of the cervix, that is, 10 cm. This is the stage of cervical dilatation.
- **Second stage:** From full dilatation of the cervix to expulsion of the fetus. This is the stage of expulsion.
- **Third stage:** From expulsion of the fetus to expulsion of the placenta and membranes. This is the placental stage.
- **Fourth stage:** The period of observation, conventionally the first two hours after delivery of the placenta, during which the mother is watched closely for bleeding and uterine contraction.

A frequent trap is to count the third stage from delivery of the head or crowning. It does not. It begins only after the baby is completely expelled and ends when the placenta and membranes are delivered.

The first stage is divided into a **latent phase**, the early slower phase during which the cervix effaces and dilates gradually, and an **active phase**, when dilatation becomes faster — conventionally from about 3 to 4 cm in classic textbook teaching — until full dilatation. Partograph questions are based on the active phase, so remember that distinction.

## Durations and What Is Considered Normal

Durations differ between a woman delivering for the first time and one who has delivered before. Standard textbook ranges taught for FMGE revision are:

- **First stage:** About 12 hours in a primigravida and about 6 hours in a multigravida
- **Latent phase:** About 8 hours in a primigravida and about 4 hours in a multigravida
- **Second stage:** About 2 hours in a primigravida and about 30 minutes in a multigravida
- **Third stage:** About 15 minutes in both, and it should not exceed 30 minutes
- **Fourth stage:** About 2 hours of close observation

These are average textbook figures, not rigid limits for every labour. Individual labours vary, and factors such as epidural analgesia are also considered in contemporary practice. For the exam, however, the classic ranges above are what the options are built on.

Definitions follow directly from them. A placenta not delivered within 30 minutes of the baby is a **retained placenta**. In the active phase, cervical dilatation of about 1 cm per hour in a primigravida is the classic rate linked to the partograph. Slower progress is a warning sign requiring assessment of contractions, fetal position, and the pelvis, not a finding to observe indefinitely.

## Partograph Basics Every Aspirant Must Know

The **partograph** is a graphical record of the progress of labour and of maternal and fetal condition during the first stage. Its purpose is early recognition of abnormal progress so that timely referral or intervention can happen.

Key points to know:

- It is started in the **active phase** of the first stage, not in the latent phase.
- **Cervical dilatation** is plotted with an X, and descent of the presenting part with an O, against time.
- The **alert line** represents expected dilatation of about 1 cm per hour in the active phase.
- The **action line** is parallel to the alert line and 4 hours to its right. If the curve crosses to the right of it, action is required.
- A curve to the left of the alert line indicates satisfactory progress; a curve moving right of it is a warning of slow progress.
- Fetal heart rate, uterine contractions in ten minutes, maternal pulse, blood pressure, temperature, and urine are also recorded regularly.

In an image-based question, first look at where the dilatation plot lies in relation to the two lines. That often answers whether labour is progressing normally, is prolonged, or needs intervention. Do not confuse the alert line, a warning, with the action line, the point for action.

## Active Management of the Third Stage of Labour

The third stage is short, but it is closely linked to **postpartum haemorrhage**, a leading cause of maternal mortality. **Active management of the third stage of labour**, or AMTSL, is a set of interventions to facilitate delivery of the placenta and reduce blood loss, and a favourite FMGE topic.

The three classic components are:

- **Administration of a uterotonic**, with oxytocin as the preferred agent, around the time of delivery of the baby, to contract the uterus and help separate and expel the placenta
- **Controlled cord traction**, with gentle traction on the cord and counter-pressure on the uterus, once the uterus is contracted and signs of placental separation are present
- **Assessment of uterine tone** after delivery of the placenta, with uterine massage if the uterus is not well contracted

Older descriptions listed sustained uterine massage as a routine component. Current teaching emphasises assessment of tone and massaging only when needed, so read options carefully: the principle tested is a contracted, retracted uterus.

Also know the signs of **placental separation**: the uterus becomes globular, firm, and rises in the abdomen, there is a sudden gush of blood, and the length of cord outside the vulva increases. Traction before separation, and before the uterus contracts, is incorrect and a common distractor. Management without uterotonics and controlled cord traction is called physiological or expectant management, and is associated with a longer third stage and more blood loss.

## How FMGE Tests This Topic and How to Revise

FMGE is conducted by NBEMS as 300 MCQs in two parts of 150 questions each, with no negative marking, and the pass mark is 150 out of 300. Obstetrics contributes a steady set of questions in that paper, and labour is among its most predictable areas.

A practical revision method is:

- Write the four stages with start point, end point, and duration on one page
- Draw a blank partograph once by hand, marking the alert and action lines
- Learn AMTSL as three components in order, and what is *not* part of it
- Link each stage to its main complication: abnormal progress in the first, distress in the second, haemorrhage and retained placenta in the third
- Attempt questions soon after reading, because stage-boundary traps appear only when you must choose

Q: What are the four stages of labour?
A: The first stage runs from the onset of true labour to full cervical dilatation, the second from full dilatation to expulsion of the fetus, the third from expulsion of the fetus to delivery of the placenta and membranes, and the fourth is the two hours of observation after that.
Q: What is the normal duration of the second stage of labour?
A: In standard textbook teaching, the second stage lasts about 2 hours in a primigravida and about 30 minutes in a multigravida.
Q: What is the difference between the alert line and the action line on a partograph?
A: The alert line represents expected progress of about 1 cm of cervical dilatation per hour in the active phase. The action line lies 4 hours to the right of the alert line, and crossing it means action is required.
Q: What are the components of active management of the third stage of labour?
A: Administration of a uterotonic such as oxytocin, controlled cord traction with counter-pressure on the contracted uterus, and assessment of uterine tone after placental delivery, with massage if the uterus is not well contracted.
