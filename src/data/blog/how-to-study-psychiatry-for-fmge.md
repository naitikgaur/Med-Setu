**Psychiatry for FMGE** carries **5 questions out of 300** in the NBEMS examination — two parts of 150 MCQs, no negative marking, pass mark 150. Like the other 5-question subjects, psychiatry is worth a short, deliberate effort rather than a long one: its examinable core is well defined, built on diagnostic criteria, a handful of drug classes and a set of classic presentations that recur across sessions. This guide maps that core so a day of focused study can reasonably secure most of the subject's marks.

## Psychiatry for FMGE: the diagnostic core — learn the patterns

Psychiatry questions are pattern-recognition questions. Each major disorder has a signature cluster, and stems are written so that one or two details identify it:

- **Schizophrenia** — delusions, hallucinations (classically auditory), disorganised thought, negative symptoms, and the first-rank symptoms traditionally associated with it
- **Mood disorders** — major depression and mania: the symptom clusters, duration logic, and the features that separate unipolar from bipolar illness
- **Anxiety and related disorders** — panic attacks, phobias, generalised anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorder with its obsession–compulsion cycle
- **Somatoform and dissociative presentations**, and **delirium versus dementia** — the acute fluctuating disturbance of attention against the chronic cognitive decline; a comparison examiners return to often
- **Substance use disorders** — alcohol withdrawal and delirium tremens, opioid and other substance pictures, and the stages of change in outline

Learn each disorder as a card: core features, the one distinguishing feature, first-line treatment class. Duration criteria matter — know the standard time thresholds that separate brief reactions from established disorders.

## Psychopharmacology: small list, many questions

A large share of psychiatry questions are really drug questions:

- **Antipsychotics** — typical versus atypical agents, and the side-effect signatures: extrapyramidal symptoms, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, metabolic effects
- **Antidepressants** — the SSRI class as first line for most depressive and anxiety disorders, and the classic cautions of the older classes
- **Mood stabilisers** — lithium above all: its narrow therapeutic range, monitoring, and toxicity features; plus the main alternatives
- **Anxiolytics and sedatives** — benzodiazepine uses and dependence, in outline
- **ECT** — its principal indications, especially severe depression with certain features, as a standard factual topic

This material overlaps directly with Pharmacology, so revise the two together and let each reinforce the other.

## Emergencies and the child–special topics tail

Know the recognition-level management of psychiatric emergencies: the suicidal patient and risk assessment principles, acute agitation, neuroleptic malignant syndrome versus serotonin syndrome (a favourite comparison), and alcohol withdrawal. In child psychiatry, learn the outlines of autism spectrum disorder, ADHD and intellectual disability. Sleep disorders and sexual disorders appear occasionally at one-line level; a single reading is enough.

## Study method

Psychiatry rewards tables and comparisons more than prose: delirium versus dementia, typical versus atypical antipsychotics, NMS versus serotonin syndrome, mania versus schizophrenia. Use a concise undergraduate psychiatry text to build those tables, then test yourself with questions — the stems are short, and practising them teaches you which single detail the examiner planted as the key. Keep the whole subject to a day or so; beyond the defined core, returns diminish fast.

## Defence mechanisms, personality and the factual tail

A thin tail of theory topics supplies the remaining questions. **Defence mechanisms** — denial, projection, reaction formation, rationalisation, sublimation as the mature example — are asked as short scenario matches. **Personality disorders** reduce to their clusters and one-line signatures: the odd-eccentric cluster, the dramatic-erratic cluster (antisocial and borderline patterns are the favourites), and the anxious-fearful cluster. **Psychological tests and scales** appear at recognition level, as do the stages of grief and the components of the mental status examination. Learn each as a matching exercise — term to example — because that is the form the questions take, and a single revision page holds the whole tail.

## How psychiatry stems are constructed

Psychiatry stems are usually the longest in the small subjects, because the diagnosis lives in the story: a timeline, a change from baseline, and one planted discriminator. Read for three things — **duration** (days versus weeks versus months separates delirium, brief psychosis, schizophrenia and the mood episodes), **awareness and attention** (clouded consciousness points away from primary psychiatric illness toward delirium), and **the discriminator detail**: the guilt and early-morning waking of depression, the grandiosity and reduced need for sleep of mania, the ego-dystonic quality of obsessions. If you practise extracting those three elements from every stem, psychiatry questions become some of the most mechanical in the paper — which is exactly what you want from a 5-question subject.

## The last-week page

Condense psychiatry onto one page before the exam: the five disorder cards in two lines each, the drug-class table with side-effect signatures, lithium's monitoring and toxicity in three lines, NMS versus serotonin syndrome as a two-column strip, and the defence-mechanism matches. Psychiatry's facts are stable and few; the risk is not ignorance but blurring — depression into adjustment reaction, delirium into dementia — under time pressure. A single sharp page, revised twice in the last week, keeps the boundaries clean. Build it during your main pass, while the distinctions are fresh, rather than trying to reconstruct it from a textbook in the final days.
