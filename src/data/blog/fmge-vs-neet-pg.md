Students preparing abroad often plan their whole future around a confusion between two exams, so a clear **fmge vs neet pg** comparison is worth doing carefully. Both are national, computer-based, MCQ examinations covering the MBBS subjects. Both are conducted within the NBEMS ecosystem of national medical examinations. And there the resemblance largely ends. The **FMGE** is a **screening test**: it certifies that a foreign medical graduate meets the minimum standard to be licensed to practise in India. **NEET PG** is a **competitive entrance examination**: it ranks Indian-system graduates — and foreign graduates who have completed the FMGE pathway and registration — for admission to postgraduate seats, where the number of aspirants vastly exceeds the number of seats. One exam asks, "Are you above the line?" The other asks, "Where do you stand in the queue?" Almost every difference between them — pattern, marking, difficulty, preparation — flows from that single difference of purpose. This article sets the two side by side so you can plan each stage of your career with the right exam in mind.

## FMGE vs NEET PG on Purpose: Licence Versus Admission

The FMGE exists because India cannot directly supervise medical teaching in every country its students travel to. The screening test is the quality gate: pass it, complete the **compulsory rotating internship (CRMI) in India**, obtain permanent registration, and you are licensed to practise. NEET PG exists because postgraduate seats — MD, MS and diploma seats — are scarce and must be allocated by merit. It does not certify a minimum standard; it produces a rank order. A candidate can be an excellent doctor and still not get a desired PG seat, because ranking is relative. Conversely, the FMGE has no ranking at all: **150 out of 300 is a pass**, whether you are the only candidate or one of thousands, and whether you score 151 or 251. Understanding this removes a common anxiety — in the FMGE, other candidates are not your competition. The paper is.

## Pattern and Marking, Side by Side

The structural facts, stated plainly:

- **FMGE:** 300 MCQs, in two parts — Part A 150 questions and Part B 150 questions, taken the same day with a break between parts. **+1 per correct answer, no negative marking.** Pass mark fixed at **150/300**.
- **NEET PG:** 200 questions in a single sitting of **3.5 hours**. Marking is **+4 for a correct answer and −1 for a wrong one** — negative marking is very much present.

These numbers produce different exams in feel. The FMGE is a test of **breadth and endurance**: more questions, every subject given blueprint weight, two sittings in one day, and no penalty for attempting — so attempt strategy means attempting everything. NEET PG is a test of **precision under penalty**: fewer questions, each worth more, and every guess carries a downside that must be weighed. A preparation habit that is correct for one — attempt all 300 without fear — would be reckless if carried unchanged into the other.

## Difficulty: A Question That Needs Unpacking

"Which is harder?" is the question students ask most and the one with the least useful short answer. The FMGE's questions are set at the standard expected of a competent graduate — the difficulty lies in its **breadth across 19 subjects**, the unfamiliarity of the Indian exam style for students trained abroad, and the fact that a fixed pass line offers no partial credit for a strong subject profile: 150 is 150, however it is composed. NEET PG's difficulty is **competitive**: the paper may be individually manageable, but the rank you need for a clinical seat in a preferred institution can demand a score far above what mere competence produces, because thousands of well-prepared candidates are ranked on the same scale. In short, the FMGE is hard to pass without broad preparation; NEET PG is hard to top without deep, repeated, rank-oriented preparation. They are difficult in different currencies.

## Preparation Differences That Follow

For the **FMGE**, preparation should be blueprint-led: cover all 19 subjects to a solid standard, train with [previous-year papers](/papers/) and full two-part mocks, master the no-negative-marking attempt strategy, and use the [question bank](/qbank/) to close topic gaps. The target is a stable score comfortably above 150, not brilliance in three subjects and absence in five.

For **NEET PG**, preparation is rank-led: deeper coverage of high-yield topics, far more questions practised under negative-marking discipline, and strategic guessing — where an unattempted question is often correct strategy. Timelines stretch longer, and revision cycles multiply, because the margin between ranks is thin.

For the foreign graduate, the two preparations are **sequential, not alternatives**. The pathway runs: FMGE → internship (CRMI) → permanent registration → NEET PG eligibility for postgraduate admission. Preparing for the FMGE with NEET PG also in mind is sensible — the subjects overlap almost entirely, and FMGE preparation done conceptually, rather than by rote, becomes the foundation of later PG preparation. But the immediate target must stay clear: first cross the licensing line. A brilliant PG-style preparation that neglects FMGE breadth can fail the screening test; a sound FMGE preparation, by contrast, is never wasted on the road to PG.

## Which Exam Should Be on Your Desk Right Now?

If you hold a foreign primary medical qualification and are not yet registered in India, the answer is the FMGE — it is the gate in front of every other door, including NEET PG. Organise your months around it: blueprint coverage, [grand test series](/tests/) practice, honest analysis. Once you are through it and registered, the NEET PG preparation style takes over, with its own pattern and its own discipline. Confusing the two wastes effort; sequencing them builds a career.
