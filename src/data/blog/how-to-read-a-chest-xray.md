Knowing **how to read chest X-ray** films is a core skill because the chest radiograph is the most requested imaging test in medicine — and one of the easiest to misread when you look at it randomly. Beginners look straight at the obvious shadow. Systematic readers first check that the film is worth reading, then sweep through the same structures in the same order every time, including the corners where pathology hides. The **ABCDE approach** below is the standard teaching framework used for exactly that discipline.

## Before ABCDE: Check the Film Itself

A wrong conclusion from a bad film is still a wrong conclusion. Run through these checks first:

- **Patient and date:** confirm the name, side marker and when the film was taken.
- **Projection:** most routine films are **PA** (posteroanterior, patient upright, chest against the plate); portable films are usually **AP**, which magnifies the heart and makes cardiac size unreliable.
- **Rotation:** the medial ends of the clavicles should be equidistant from the spinous processes; a rotated film fakes mediastinal shift.
- **Inspiration:** on a good inspiratory film you can count about 9–10 posterior ribs above the diaphragm; a poorly inspired film crowds the lung markings and enlarges the heart shadow.
- **Penetration:** the vertebral bodies should be just visible behind the heart; too dark or too pale a film hides real disease.

Say these out loud in a viva before describing any abnormality — examiners award the habit as much as the findings. Build the same check-first discipline for other investigations with the guides in our [resources](/resources/) and imaging questions in the [question bank](/qbank/).

## How to Read Chest Xray Films: A Is for Airway

**A — Airway.** Follow the trachea down the midline. Is it central, or pushed or pulled to one side? The trachea is **pushed** away by increased pressure or volume on one side — a large pleural effusion or a tension pneumothorax — and **pulled** towards loss of volume, such as collapse or fibrosis. Continue to the carina and main bronchi; note any narrowing, displacement or obvious mass around the hila, remembering that the left hilum normally sits slightly higher than the right.

## B Is for Breathing: The Lungs and Pleura

**B — Breathing.** Compare the two lung fields zone by zone — upper, middle and lower — left against right, because the eye catches asymmetry better than absolute abnormality. Assess:

- **Lung markings and opacities:** consolidation appears as air-space shadowing, often with air bronchograms; collapse shows volume loss with shift of fissures and structures towards it.
- **The pleura:** a pleural effusion blunts the costophrenic angle and forms a meniscus; a pneumothorax shows a pleural line with no lung markings beyond it.
- **The edges:** trace the border of each lung out to the chest wall and down to the angles — small effusions and small pneumothoraces hide at the margins.
- **Tubes and lines:** note the position of any endotracheal tube, central line, chest drain or nasogastric tube on the film.

## C Is for Circulation: Heart and Mediastinum

**C — Circulation.** Judge the cardiac shadow on a PA film: the cardiothoracic ratio should be **less than 0.5** — the widest heart diameter under half the widest chest diameter. Trace the heart borders: the right border is formed mainly by the right atrium, the left by the left ventricle. Loss of a border localises disease — loss of the right heart border points to right middle lobe pathology, loss of the left border to lingular disease; this is the **silhouette sign**. Then assess the mediastinum: a widened mediastinum, an abnormal aortic knuckle or shifted structures each carry their own differential. Look at the hila again for enlargement, which may be vascular, nodal or a mass.

## D and E: Diaphragm, and Everything Else

**D — Diaphragm.** The right hemidiaphragm normally sits slightly higher than the left because of the liver. Check its shape and clarity, look for flattening in hyperinflation, and — critically — look **under** it: free air under the diaphragm on an erect film suggests a perforated hollow viscus and is a surgical emergency until proved otherwise.

**E — Everything else.** Finish with the review areas students forget: the **bones** (ribs, clavicles, spine and shoulders for fractures, lytic or sclerotic lesions), the **soft tissues** (surgical emphysema, breast shadows, missing breast after mastectomy), the **lung apices** above the clavicles, and the corners behind the heart and below the diaphragms. Many a viva case is decided by the lesion sitting quietly in an apex or a review area.

## Common Patterns Worth Recognising Early

A few appearances recur constantly in examinations and on the wards. **Consolidation** from pneumonia fills a lobe or segment with shadowing while the volume stays normal and the heart border beside it may vanish. **Collapse** does the opposite: shadowing with clear volume loss, structures pulled towards it. A **pleural effusion** produces a dense white base with a curved upper edge, erasing the costophrenic angle, while **pulmonary oedema** classically spreads symmetrically from both hila in a "bat's wing" pattern with an enlarged heart. Naming the pattern first, then listing its causes, is how clinicians reason — and how examiners expect you to answer.

Finally, describe the film before diagnosing: projection and quality, then each ABCDE element, then a summary and the most likely diagnosis with two or three alternatives. Compare with previous films whenever they exist — change over time is often the most valuable finding on the page.

Q: What does ABCDE stand for in chest X-ray reading?
A: Airway, Breathing (lungs and pleura), Circulation (heart and mediastinum), Diaphragm, and Everything else (bones, soft tissues, apices and other review areas).
Q: What is a normal cardiothoracic ratio?
A: On a properly taken PA film, the widest diameter of the heart should be less than half the widest internal diameter of the chest — a ratio under 0.5.
Q: Why does it matter whether a chest X-ray is PA or AP?
A: An AP (usually portable) film magnifies the heart shadow, so cardiac size can look falsely enlarged; the cardiothoracic ratio is only reliable on a PA film.
Q: What does free air under the diaphragm mean?
A: On an erect film it suggests perforation of a hollow abdominal organ such as the stomach or bowel, and needs urgent surgical assessment.
