Every park psm book guide starts with the same honest warning: this park textbook is big, fact-heavy and easy to read badly. Used well, the psm book becomes the single most reliable source for Community Medicine in both MBBS and FMGE, and this guide shows you how to turn a dense book into marks instead of reading it cover to cover and retaining almost nothing.

## Park PSM Book Guide: What the Textbook Actually Covers

**K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine** is the standard text for **Community Medicine**, also called Preventive and Social Medicine or PSM. Its scope is wider than most students expect. It moves from the concepts of health and disease, through **epidemiology** and biostatistics, into communicable and non-communicable diseases, and then into the practical machinery of public health in India: national health programmes, demography and family welfare, nutrition, environmental health, occupational health, health planning and management, and communication for health education.

That breadth is exactly why the book feels overwhelming. Park is not a book you read like a novel. It is a reference-organised textbook in which some chapters are concept chapters you must understand deeply, some are fact chapters you must revise repeatedly, and some are programme chapters you mainly need in outline, with names, target groups and recent changes held clearly.

A useful way to see the book is in four blocks:

- **Concepts and tools** — health, disease causation, epidemiology, screening and statistics
- **Disease control** — communicable diseases, non-communicable diseases and their prevention levels
- **Health systems** — programmes, policies, planning, management and health care delivery in India
- **Applied topics** — nutrition, environment, occupation, demography, genetics and mental health

## The Chapters That Carry the Most Weight

Not all chapters are equal, and treating them as equal is the commonest mistake. **Epidemiology** is the heart of the subject: study designs, measures of morbidity and mortality, association and causation, and screening tests reward genuine understanding and are tested again and again. Learn to calculate and interpret rates, ratios, sensitivity, specificity and predictive values rather than merely recognising definitions.

**National health programmes** are the second heavyweight. Examiners love programme names, implementing agencies, target beneficiaries and recent revisions. Because programmes change, always prefer the latest teaching and official updates over older notes for this block, and make a one-page summary per programme instead of re-reading full chapters.

Other high-yield areas include:

- **Demography and family welfare** — demographic cycle, fertility indicators, contraceptive methods and their Pearl index values
- **Nutrition** — nutritional requirements, deficiency disorders, food fortification and nutritional programmes
- **Communicable diseases** — epidemiology, prevention and control of major infections, with incubation periods and vaccine details held precisely
- **Health planning and management** — planning cycle, management techniques and health committee recommendations

## How to Read Park Without Drowning

Read in **layers**. On the first pass through a chapter, read only for structure: headings, definitions, classifications and tables. Park's tables are the book in compressed form — if you understand a table, you understand most of what an examiner can ask from that section. On the second pass, read the text around the tables and mark numbers, criteria and named recommendations that are classically tested.

Then convert, do not just highlight. For each chapter, produce a short set of notes in your own words: one page for concepts, one page for numbers and criteria, one page for programmes. Highlighting feels productive but survives badly; written recall material is what your second and third revisions will actually use.

Practical habits that work:

- **Study epidemiology actively** — solve small numericals on rates and screening until the formulas feel boring
- **Keep a numbers notebook** — cut-offs, indices, periods and doses of public-health importance in one place, revised weekly
- **Update programme facts separately** — programmes change faster than textbooks, so keep a dated, single list you can correct
- **Link chapters together** — vaccine-preventable diseases, the immunisation schedule and the relevant programme form one story, not three

## Common Mistakes Students Make With Park

The first mistake is **reading once, late, and in page order**. Community Medicine rewards spaced repetition far more than heroic single readings, because so much of it is factual. Start early in the academic year, even at two or three chapters a week, and let revision do the heavy lifting.

The second mistake is ignoring the subject because it feels less clinical. In FMGE, Community Medicine carries a substantial share of the 300 questions, and its questions are often more predictable than clinical ones — a prepared student collects these marks reliably. The third mistake is memorising programme details from outdated sources; when a programme is renamed, merged or re-targeted, old notes quietly become wrong answers.

Finally, do not skip the "boring" foundations. Concepts of health, levels of prevention and epidemiological methods appear simple, yet they frame a large number of questions, including ones that look clinical at first glance.

## Turning Park Into Exam Marks

Close the loop with questions. After finishing a topic in Park, attempt topic-wise questions the same week, while the structure is fresh, and note which facts you missed — those missed facts go back into your numbers notebook. Spaced testing converts Park's density from a burden into an advantage, because the subject's facts are finite and heavily repeated across years.

Q: Is Park enough for Community Medicine in FMGE?
A: Park covers the subject comprehensively and is the standard reference for Community Medicine. Combined with topic-wise practice and updated programme facts, it is sufficient for most students.
Q: Which Park chapters are most important?
A: Epidemiology, screening, national health programmes, demography and family welfare, nutrition and communicable diseases are consistently high-yield. Concepts of health and levels of prevention are small but frequently tested.
Q: How do I remember so many numbers from Park?
A: Keep one dedicated notebook for cut-offs, indices, incubation periods and criteria, and revise it weekly. Numbers are retained through spaced recall and question practice, not through repeated reading.
Q: Should I read Park cover to cover?
A: No. Read concept chapters deeply, convert tables and facts into short notes, and keep programme details in updated one-page summaries. Layered reading with revision beats a single cover-to-cover pass.
