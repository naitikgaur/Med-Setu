**Ophthalmology for FMGE** carries **15 questions out of 300**, the same weightage as Paediatrics and ENT, in the NBEMS-conducted paper of two parts of 150 MCQs with no negative marking and a pass mark of 150. It is a self-contained subject with a finite syllabus, which makes it attractive: the list of conditions examiners draw from is short, and many questions are built around a fundus picture, a slit-lamp description or one classic sign. If you study ophthalmology visually and by condition clusters, 15 questions is a very achievable scoring block.

## Ophthalmology for FMGE: how the questions are framed

Eye questions in FMGE usually present in one of three ways: a **clinical photograph or fundus image** to identify, a **classic presentation** described in one or two lines, or a **management priority** — what to do first, or what never to do. This means your preparation should pair every condition with (a) what it looks like, (b) its one-line identifying feature, and (c) its first-line management. Reading long theory without the images leaves the most common question type unprepared.

## The lens and glaucoma: the guaranteed core

- **Cataract** — types and causes, senile cataract maturation stages, congenital cataract causes, and surgical principles including phacoemulsification and intraocular lens power calculation concepts
- **Glaucoma** — open-angle versus angle-closure: the painful red eye with a fixed mid-dilated pupil and raised intraocular pressure in acute angle closure, its emergency management, and the drug classes used in chronic open-angle glaucoma
- **Refractive errors and the phorias** — myopia, hypermetropia, astigmatism and their corrections, a small but recurring factual area

Glaucoma pharmacology overlaps with Pharmacology teaching — prostaglandin analogues as first-line for chronic open-angle glaucoma is the kind of cross-subject fact that appears in both.

## Retina, neuro-ophthalmology and the red eye

- **Diabetic and hypertensive retinopathy** — fundus findings in sequence: microaneurysms, dot-blot haemorrhages, hard exudates, neovascularisation; and the grading logic
- **Retinal vascular occlusions** — the cherry-red spot of central retinal artery occlusion and the blood-and-thunder fundus of central retinal vein occlusion
- **Retinal detachment and retinitis pigmentosa** — flashes, floaters and a curtain over vision; night blindness with bone-spicule pigmentation
- **The red eye differential** — conjunctivitis, keratitis, uveitis, acute glaucoma and subconjunctival haemorrhage separated by pain, vision change, pupil and discharge
- **Vitamin A deficiency and xerophthalmia** — night blindness, Bitot's spots and keratomalacia, a favourite in the Indian context
- **Trachoma and common infections**, plus **ocular manifestations of systemic disease** — from thyroid eye disease to the eye in diabetes and hypertension

Learn the red-eye differential as a comparison table; it converts a whole family of questions into a single revision page.

## Books and study method

**Khurana's Comprehensive Ophthalmology** is the standard undergraduate text and is well matched to the FMGE level. Use it with an image-focused approach: for every condition, look at the clinical photograph in the book until you can name the finding in one line. Build two summary tables — the red-eye differential and the fundus findings of the major retinal diseases — and revise those repeatedly. Because the subject is small, two complete passes are realistic: one for concepts, one purely for images and tables in the final weeks.

## Optics, squint and the one-liner topics

Beyond the big three blocks, keep a page for the short factual topics that appear as single questions. **Optics basics** — the power of the eye, the lens and common aberrations in outline — support the refractive-error questions. **Squint (strabismus)** contributes the cover test, the difference between concomitant and paralytic squint, and amblyopia as the time-critical complication in children. **Ocular trauma** asks about chemical injuries — irrigation as the immediate step before everything else — and the sympathetic ophthalmia risk after penetrating injury. **Tumours** reduce largely to retinoblastoma in a child with leukocoria and its genetics in outline. None of these needs a chapter; each needs ten accurate lines, and together they are worth several questions across a few sessions.

## How to use images in revision

Because so many questions are photographs, revise actively: cover the caption, name the finding and the diagnosis aloud, then check. Mix normal and abnormal images — several questions hinge on recognising that an eye or fundus is normal, or on spotting the one abnormal quadrant. Pay special attention to the disc: its colour, cup and margins distinguish papilloedema, optic atrophy and glaucomatous cupping, three conditions students confuse under pressure. If you build the habit of describing every image in a fixed order — media, disc, vessels, macula, periphery for fundus pictures — you will extract the diagnostic feature even from an unfamiliar photograph.

## A realistic study allocation

Give ophthalmology two to three focused days inside a full revision cycle. Day one: the lens, glaucoma and refractive errors, with their pharmacology. Day two: retina and neuro-ophthalmology, working through fundus images until the major patterns are instant. Day three: the red-eye table, systemic disease manifestations, trauma and the one-liner topics, finished with a full topic-wise question run and a review of every mistake. After that, the subject needs only maintenance — ten minutes with your two summary tables whenever you revise a neighbouring subject. That allocation matches the subject's size: large enough to matter at 15 questions, small enough to finish properly.
