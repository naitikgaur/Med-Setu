Most diagnoses are made from the story, not the scan. These **history taking basics medical students** learn early decide almost everything that follows: what you examine, which tests you order and which emergencies you must not miss. A good history is not a long list of questions asked in any order. It is a structured conversation that lets the patient tell their story while you quietly test hypotheses in your mind. Master the structure now, in the wards and in the outpatient department, and every case presentation, viva and clinical examination becomes easier.

## History Taking Basics Medical Students Should Follow Every Time

Use the same order for every patient until it becomes automatic. Consistency is what protects you when you are tired, rushed or facing an unfamiliar presentation.

- **Identification and introduction:** name, age, sex, occupation and address in brief; introduce yourself, confirm identity and take consent to proceed.
- **Presenting complaint:** the main problem, in the patient's own words, with its duration — "chest pain for two hours", not your interpretation of it.
- **History of presenting illness:** the full story of that complaint, from onset to today, in chronological order.
- **Past medical and surgical history:** previous illnesses, operations, admissions and injuries.
- **Drug history and allergies:** every medicine, including over-the-counter and traditional remedies, dose and duration; allergies with the exact reaction.
- **Family history:** similar illness, major diseases and cause of death in close relatives where relevant.
- **Social and personal history:** occupation, smoking, alcohol, diet, sleep, and the impact of the illness on daily life.
- **Systems review:** a short screening sweep of the other systems so nothing important is missed.

Practise structuring histories the way you practise questions — topic by topic in our [question bank](/qbank/) and full cases in the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) — so the framework is fixed before you meet real patients.

## Expanding the Presenting Complaint

For any symptom, ask about onset, duration, progression and associated features. Was the onset sudden or gradual? Is it constant or episodic? What makes it better or worse? What has the patient already tried? Then ask directly about the features that separate the serious causes from the simple ones. A cough history, for example, changes completely once you ask about haemoptysis, fever, weight loss, breathlessness, chest pain and exposure to tuberculosis. Learn to ask the **red-flag questions** for each common presentation and ask them every time, even when the story sounds benign.

Close this section by summarising back: "So the pain began three days ago, it comes in waves, and nothing has helped so far — is that right?" Summarising catches errors early and tells the patient they have been heard.

## SOCRATES: The Pain Framework

Pain is the commonest presenting complaint, and **SOCRATES** is the standard framework for characterising it:

- **S — Site:** where exactly is the pain? Ask the patient to point with one finger.
- **O — Onset:** when did it start, and was it sudden or gradual? What were they doing?
- **C — Character:** what does it feel like — sharp, dull, burning, colicky, crushing?
- **R — Radiation:** does it spread anywhere, such as arm, back, jaw or leg?
- **A — Associated symptoms:** nausea, sweating, breathlessness, fever, vomiting?
- **T — Timing:** how long does it last, how often, and is the pattern changing?
- **E — Exacerbating and relieving factors:** movement, food, posture, medicines, rest?
- **S — Severity:** rate it out of ten, and ask what it stops them doing.

SOCRATES works for headache, abdominal pain, chest pain and joint pain alike. Adapt it, do not abandon it: for breathlessness you will emphasise orthopnoea and exercise tolerance; for a swelling you will ask about growth, discharge and similar lumps. The principle is identical — no symptom is fully described until its dimensions are known.

## Past History, Drugs and Allergies: Where Marks and Patients Are Lost

Examiners and real clinicians both judge you here. Ask specifically about **diabetes, hypertension, asthma, tuberculosis, jaundice, epilepsy and thyroid disease** rather than "any past illness?", because patients forget diagnoses they consider unimportant. Record operations with approximate year and any complications.

The drug history must include prescribed medicines, self-medication, inhalers, injections, oral contraceptives and herbal or Ayurvedic preparations. For allergies, never write "allergic to penicillin" alone — record what happened: rash, swelling or collapse. A vague allergy label can deny a patient a first-line drug for life, while a missed true allergy can be dangerous. If a patient takes no medicines, write that too; a blank space looks like a forgotten question.

## The Systems Review, Done Briefly and Well

The **systems review** (review of systems) is a rapid screen, not a second full history. Group your questions:

- **General:** fever, appetite, weight change, sleep, fatigue.
- **Cardiorespiratory:** cough, sputum, breathlessness, chest pain, palpitations, ankle swelling.
- **Gastrointestinal:** pain, vomiting, bowel habit, bleeding, jaundice.
- **Genitourinary:** urine output, burning, frequency, discharge, menstrual history where relevant.
- **Neurological:** headache, fits, weakness, numbness, vision or hearing change.
- **Musculoskeletal and skin:** joint pain, swelling, rash, ulcers.

Keep it to the questions that matter for the case in front of you, but never skip it entirely — the systems review is where the second diagnosis hides: the diabetic's foot ulcer, the breathless patient's long-forgotten tuberculosis.

## Presenting the History

A history is only useful if you can present it in ninety seconds without reading a page. Practise this format: identification in one line, presenting complaint with duration, the story in chronological order, the important negatives, past history and drugs in one breath, then your summary and the diagnoses you are considering. Stating **important negatives** — "no fever, no weight loss, no haemoptysis" — shows the examiner you asked the discriminating questions. End with what you would examine and investigate next, and why.

History taking improves only with repetition and feedback. Present to seniors, ask what they would have asked, and notice which single question changed the case. Q: What is the correct order of history taking?
A: Introduce yourself and confirm identity, record the presenting complaint, take the history of the presenting illness, then past history, drug history and allergies, family history, social history, and finish with a brief systems review before summarising.
Q: What does SOCRATES stand for?
A: Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associated symptoms, Timing, Exacerbating and relieving factors, and Severity — the standard framework for characterising pain.
Q: Why are important negatives mentioned in a presentation?
A: Because they show you asked the questions that rule out serious alternatives — for example, no fever, no weight loss and no haemoptysis in a cough history — which is how clinicians narrow a differential diagnosis.
Q: How long should a case presentation take?
A: Aim for about ninety seconds to two minutes: identification, complaint, chronological story, key negatives, past history and drugs, then your summary and likely diagnoses.
