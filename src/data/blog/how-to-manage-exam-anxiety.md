If you are searching for how to manage exam anxiety in the weeks before the FMGE, start with a reassuring fact: a racing heart before a big test is a normal stress response, not proof that you will fail. Anxiety becomes a problem only when it starts stealing sleep, blocking revision, or freezing you in the exam hall. The good news is that most exam anxiety is driven by **uncertainty** — not knowing the pattern, not knowing your timing, not knowing how a bad first section feels. Uncertainty can be reduced, step by step, and that is exactly what this guide does.

## How to Manage Exam Anxiety Before and During FMGE

Anxiety feeds on the unknown, so the most reliable calming technique is not a slogan — it is **familiarity**. The FMGE is a computer-based test conducted by **NBEMS**: 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, with a break between Part A and Part B, +1 for every correct answer, **no negative marking**, and a pass mark of 150 out of 300. When you know this structure cold, and have sat through it many times in practice, exam day stops feeling like a cliff edge and starts feeling like a routine you have rehearsed.

Work through the levers below in order: preparation first, body second, mind third, and exam-hall tactics last.

## Reduce Uncertainty With Simulation, Not Just Study

Reading notes reduces knowledge gaps, but it does not rehearse the exam itself. What lowers anxiety measurably is **simulation** — sitting a full paper, in the real two-part manner, with the timer running and no pausing to check answers. Each simulation teaches your nervous system that 150 questions in a part is survivable, that difficult patches end, and that the break between parts is a genuine reset.

Build simulation into your week:

- Attempt full papers from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) under timed conditions, not as casual reading.
- Review every attempt honestly — which topics leaked marks, which questions ate your time.
- Repeat the exact exam-day sequence: same wake-up time, same meal, same start time where possible.
- Practise recovering from a bad patch: ten hard questions in a row is a normal event in a 300-question paper, not a verdict.

After a few full simulations, most students find their baseline anxiety drops simply because there is less left to imagine. Your imagination is almost always harsher than the real hall.

## Look After the Body That Carries the Brain

Anxiety is physical before it is mental. A tired, dehydrated, caffeine-loaded body produces symptoms — palpitations, restlessness, poor concentration — that the mind then interprets as panic. You cannot fully control exam nerves, but you can stop manufacturing extra ones.

- **Sleep:** protect a regular sleep window in the final weeks. Sleep supports memory consolidation, and a rested brain retrieves faster under pressure.
- **Caffeine:** keep it moderate and familiar. Exam week is not the time to double your coffee or try an energy drink for the first time.
- **Movement:** a daily walk or light exercise supports mood and helps discharge physical tension. It does not need to be intense to help.
- **Food:** eat normally and simply, especially on exam day. Heavy experimentation with supplements or diets belongs nowhere near a high-stakes week.

Think of these as removing noise. The calmer your baseline physiology, the less material your anxious thoughts have to work with.

## Reframe the Thoughts, Not Just the Feelings

**Cognitive reframing** means examining an anxious thought and replacing it with an accurate one — not a falsely cheerful one. "I will fail" becomes "I have prepared these topics, my mock scores are around this range, and one paper decides nothing about my worth." Accuracy is calming because it is believable.

Three reframes that hold up under pressure:

- **"Everyone else is more prepared."** You cannot see anyone else's preparation; you can only see their confidence, which is not the same thing.
- **"This one question matters."** In a 300-question paper with no negative marking, no single question matters very much. Attempt it, mark it if unsure, move on.
- **"Anxiety means I am not ready."** Anxiety means the exam matters to you. Many well-prepared students feel it; performance depends on your method, not on feeling perfectly calm.

Write your reframes down before exam day. In the hall, a tired brain believes what it has rehearsed, not what it invents on the spot.

## A Simple Plan for the Exam Hall

Decide your hall tactics in advance so anxiety has fewer decisions to hijack. Use a **two-pass method**: answer the questions you know first, mark the doubtful ones, and return to them with your remaining time. With no negative marking, **never leave a question blank** — an educated guess costs nothing and can only help. If panic spikes mid-part, use slow breathing: breathe in gently, and make the exhale longer than the inhale for a minute or so, while resting your eyes on a fixed point. Then pick the next question — any question — and restart the routine.

Use the break between Part A and Part B properly: leave Part A behind completely, eat something light, move around, and do not dissect questions with other candidates. Whatever happened in the first 150 is already fixed; the second 150 is a fresh paper worth a full 150 marks.

## When Anxiety Needs More Than Self-Help

Self-help has limits, and recognising them is a strength, not a failure. If anxiety is severe or persistent — if you cannot sleep for many nights, cannot study at all, have panic attacks, or feel persistently low or hopeless — please speak to a doctor, counsellor, or mental-health professional. Many colleges have support services for exactly this. Getting proper help early protects both your health and your exam far better than struggling alone.

For most students, though, anxiety sits at the manageable end: a loud but trainable response. Prepare the pattern, rehearse the day, steady the body, reframe the thoughts — and let your preparation do the talking.

Q: Is some exam anxiety normal before the FMGE?
A: Yes. A stress response before a high-stakes exam is normal and does not predict failure. It becomes a concern when it persistently blocks sleep, study, or functioning — then professional support is the right step.
Q: What is the fastest way to reduce exam anxiety?
A: Reduce uncertainty. Learn the exact pattern — 300 MCQs, two parts of 150, no negative marking, pass mark 150 — and rehearse it in full timed mock tests until the format feels routine.
Q: Should I leave questions blank if I am anxious and unsure?
A: No. The FMGE has no negative marking, so an educated guess can only help. Attempt every question, using elimination to improve your odds on the doubtful ones.
Q: When should I seek professional help for exam anxiety?
A: If symptoms are severe or persistent — repeated sleepless nights, panic attacks, inability to study, or persistent low mood — consult a doctor, counsellor, or mental-health professional rather than relying on self-help alone.
