Neuroanatomy frightens students mainly because it is taught as diagrams instead of as a localisation method. Sound **neuroanatomy basics medical students** can actually use come down to three questions: which tract is involved, where does it cross, and on which side will the signs appear? Answer those for the ascending sensory tracts, the descending motor tract, and the classic cord and brainstem syndromes, and most clinical stems — a patient, a set of deficits, and a "where is the lesion?" — become systematic rather than guesswork.

## Neuroanatomy basics medical students need: the tracts that matter

Three long tracts carry almost all the exam weight. Learn each one's modality, crossing point and cord location.

**Ascending (sensory) tracts:**

- **Dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway** — carries fine touch, vibration, proprioception and two-point discrimination. Fibres ascend **uncrossed** in the dorsal columns of the cord (fasciculus gracilis medially for the lower limb, fasciculus cuneatus laterally for the upper limb), synapse in the nucleus gracilis and cuneatus of the medulla, cross there as internal arcuate fibres, and ascend as the medial lemniscus to the thalamus (VPL nucleus) and then the sensory cortex
- **Spinothalamic tract** — carries pain and temperature (lateral tract) and crude touch and pressure (anterior tract). Fibres synapse in the dorsal horn, cross in the **anterior white commissure of the cord within one or two segments**, and ascend on the opposite side to the thalamus. This low crossing point is the single most tested fact in cord anatomy
- **Spinocerebellar tracts** — carry unconscious proprioception to the cerebellum for coordination; the dorsal tract is mainly ipsilateral, which is why cerebellar signs appear on the **same side** as the lesion

**Descending (motor) tract:**

- **Corticospinal (pyramidal) tract** — fibres from the motor cortex pass through the posterior limb of the **internal capsule**, the cerebral peduncle of the midbrain and the pons, and about 90% cross at the **pyramidal decussation of the lower medulla** to form the lateral corticospinal tract; the rest descend uncrossed as the anterior corticospinal tract and cross near their level of termination
- The practical rule follows directly: a motor lesion **above** the medullary decussation causes weakness on the **opposite** side of the body; a lesion in the cord **below** it causes weakness on the **same** side

## Decussations: the crossings that decide the side of signs

If you memorise only one table in neuroanatomy, make it this one:

- **Pain and temperature** cross in the cord, near the level of entry — so cord lesions affect pain sensation on the opposite side, starting a segment or two below the lesion
- **Fine touch and proprioception** cross in the medulla — so in the cord they are lost on the same side as the lesion
- **Motor fibres** cross in the lower medulla — so cortical and capsular lesions weaken the opposite side, cord lesions weaken the same side
- **Cerebellar pathways** are effectively uncrossed or double-crossed — cerebellar hemisphere lesions cause ataxia on the same side

This asymmetry — sensory modalities crossing at different levels — is exactly what produces the **dissociated sensory loss** the exam loves: loss of one modality with preservation of another tells you where the lesion sits. Practising stems built on this logic in a [question bank](/qbank/) is the fastest way to make it automatic.

## Brown-Séquard syndrome and other cord lesions

**Brown-Séquard syndrome** is hemisection of the spinal cord, classically from trauma, and it is the perfect test of everything above. A lesion of the right half of the cord produces:

- **Ipsilateral (same side, below the lesion):** upper motor neuron weakness, and loss of fine touch, vibration and proprioception — because the corticospinal tract and dorsal columns have not yet crossed at cord level
- **Contralateral (opposite side, starting 1–2 segments below):** loss of pain and temperature — because spinothalamic fibres cross near their entry level
- **At the level of the lesion:** a band of ipsilateral sensory loss and lower motor neuron signs from damage to the entering roots and anterior horn cells

Contrast it with the other cord patterns the exam rotates:

- **Syringomyelia** — a central cord cavity interrupts crossing pain and temperature fibres in the anterior white commissure, giving a **cape-like dissociated sensory loss** over the shoulders and arms with touch preserved; expansion later involves anterior horn cells (wasted hand muscles) and the corticospinal tracts
- **Anterior spinal artery syndrome** — infarction of the anterior two-thirds of the cord causes bilateral motor loss and loss of pain and temperature below the lesion, with **dorsal column sensations spared** because the posterior columns have a separate blood supply
- **Subacute combined degeneration** (vitamin B12 deficiency) — damages dorsal and lateral columns, so proprioception and vibration loss combine with upper motor neuron signs, typically with a peripheral neuropathy alongside
- **Tabes dorsalis** — dorsal column and root involvement causes loss of proprioception, sensory ataxia, a positive Romberg sign and lightning pains

## Lesion localisation: cortex, capsule, brainstem and cord

Localisation questions follow a ladder. Work down it in order:

- **Cortex** — deficits are partial and specific: weakness of one part (face or a limb) rather than the whole side, cortical sensory loss, aphasia if the dominant hemisphere is involved, seizures, and visual field defects such as homonymous hemianopia. The face and hand, with their large cortical representation, are affected prominently
- **Internal capsule (posterior limb)** — a small lesion causes a dense, complete **contralateral hemiplegia affecting face, arm and leg equally**, because all the motor fibres are packed tightly together; sensory loss on the same side may accompany it. This "equal weakness" pattern distinguishes capsular from cortical lesions
- **Brainstem — crossed signs** — the signature of a brainstem lesion is an **ipsilateral cranial nerve palsy with contralateral body signs**, because cranial nerve nuclei sit at the level of the lesion while the long tracts passing through have not yet crossed (or have). Examples: a midbrain lesion causing ipsilateral third nerve palsy with contralateral hemiplegia (**Weber syndrome**); a pontine lesion causing ipsilateral sixth and seventh nerve palsies with contralateral hemiplegia; a lateral medullary lesion (**Wallenberg syndrome**, from posterior inferior cerebellar artery territory infarction) causing ipsilateral Horner syndrome, ataxia and loss of pain and temperature over the face, with contralateral loss of pain and temperature over the body, plus dysphagia and vertigo — and notably **no weakness**, because the pyramids lie medially
- **Spinal cord** — look for a **sensory level** on the trunk, bilateral signs below it, sphincter involvement, and upper motor neuron signs in the legs (or all four limbs if the cervical cord is involved). A sensory level is the strongest pointer to a cord lesion

A simple exam habit: first decide whether the signs are crossed (brainstem), one-sided below a level (cord), or face-arm-leg equal (capsule). That single decision usually leaves only one option standing. Timed [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) help you practise making it in seconds rather than minutes.

## How the exam tests neuroanatomy — and how to revise it

FMGE neuroanatomy questions, within the 300 MCQs in two parts of 150 set by NBEMS (no negative marking, pass mark 150 out of 300), are usually short clinical stems: a trauma patient with dissociated sensory loss, a stroke patient with crossed signs, a B12-deficient patient with ataxia. To revise for them:

- **Draw the three tracts once** — origin, cord position, crossing, destination — and redraw from memory weekly
- **Revise syndromes as contrasts** — Brown-Séquard against syringomyelia against anterior cord syndrome, side by side
- **Attach one artery to each brainstem syndrome** — PICA for lateral medullary syndrome is the classic pairing
- **Solve stems, not notes** — work through [previous-year papers](/papers/) and note which localisation step each question was really testing

Q: At what level do pain and temperature fibres cross in the spinal cord?
A: Pain and temperature fibres synapse in the dorsal horn and cross in the anterior white commissure within one or two segments of entry, then ascend in the contralateral spinothalamic tract.
Q: What are the features of Brown-Séquard syndrome?
A: Cord hemisection causes ipsilateral upper motor neuron weakness and loss of proprioception and vibration below the lesion, with contralateral loss of pain and temperature starting one or two segments below the lesion.
Q: What is the classic sign of a brainstem lesion?
A: Crossed signs — an ipsilateral cranial nerve palsy at the level of the lesion with contralateral motor or sensory signs in the body, as in Weber syndrome or lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome.
Q: Which cord syndrome spares the dorsal columns?
A: Anterior spinal artery syndrome. Infarction of the anterior two-thirds of the cord causes bilateral weakness and loss of pain and temperature below the lesion, while vibration and proprioception are preserved.
