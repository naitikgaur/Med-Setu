If you are deciding where your next study week should go, **obg for fmge** deserves to be near the top of the list. Obstetrics & Gynaecology carries **30 questions out of 300** in the FMGE, conducted by the National Board of Examinations in Medical Sciences (NBEMS). That is the joint-largest subject weightage in the paper, shared only with Community Medicine and close behind Medicine and Surgery. The paper has 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, there is no negative marking, and you need 150 marks to pass. A subject worth ten per cent of the paper, built largely on well-defined protocols and repeated clinical themes, is one of the most efficient places to convert study hours into marks.

## Why OBG for FMGE rewards structured study

Unlike some subjects where questions range across an enormous syllabus, OBG questions cluster around a manageable set of clinical situations: a pregnant woman with a problem, a labour that is not progressing, a gynaecological condition with a classic presentation. The examiners return to the same decision points — when to intervene, which investigation first, which drug is safe in pregnancy. If you organise your study around these decision points rather than reading a textbook cover to cover, the subject becomes far more predictable.

## The high-yield obstetrics core

Obstetrics usually contributes the larger share of the 30 questions. Prioritise these areas:

- **Antenatal care and normal pregnancy** — dating, investigations schedule, supplements, and screening for anaemia, diabetes and pre-eclampsia
- **Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy** — pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, magnesium sulphate as the drug of choice for seizure prophylaxis, and delivery as the definitive treatment
- **Antepartum haemorrhage** — placenta praevia versus abruptio placentae: painless versus painful bleeding, and the rule against per-vaginal examination in suspected praevia
- **Labour and its management** — the partograph, stages of labour, active management of the third stage, and postpartum haemorrhage with its causes (tone, trauma, tissue, thrombin)
- **Medical disorders in pregnancy** — diabetes and its screening, anaemia, heart disease, thyroid disorders and infections such as HIV and hepatitis B
- **Obstetric emergencies** — shoulder dystocia, cord prolapse, uterine rupture and amniotic fluid embolism, where the first step of management is what is tested

Learn the normal values and timelines cold: duration of pregnancy, fetal growth milestones, and the sequence of routine antenatal visits form the background against which abnormal cases are framed.

## The gynaecology half

- **Menstrual disorders and AUB** — classification of abnormal uterine bleeding and the first-line workup
- **Fibroid uterus and endometriosis** — presentations, imaging findings and management options
- **Infertility** — the systematic evaluation of both partners and common causes such as PCOS and tubal factors
- **Contraception** — methods, eligibility, failure rates in qualitative terms, and emergency contraception; a reliably repeated area
- **Gynae oncology** — cervical cancer screening with Pap smear and VIA, HPV vaccination, FIGO staging basics for cervix, endometrium and ovary
- **Prolapse and fistulae** — types of pelvic organ prolapse and vesicovaginal fistula causes

For oncology, focus on screening, staging principles and the single best investigation or treatment at each stage rather than memorising entire staging tables in one sitting.

## Which books and resources to use

**DC Dutta's Obstetrics** is the standard undergraduate anchor for the obstetrics half, and **Shaw's Textbook of Gynaecology** serves the same role for gynae. For FMGE preparation you do not need to read either cover to cover. Use them to clarify concepts your notes compress too far — the mechanism of labour, the physiology of lactation, the steps of a caesarean section. Build one concise set of notes or tables for drug safety in pregnancy, contraceptive choices and emergency protocols, and revise those repeatedly. Pair reading with question practice early: OBG is a subject where practising clinical vignettes teaches you how the facts are actually asked.

## A revision method that fits the subject

Study OBG in two passes. In the first pass, go system by system — pregnancy, labour, then gynae topics — and finish each block with a set of questions before moving on. In the second pass, work only from your tables and your mistakes: the contraindicated drugs, the "except" questions, the staging cut-offs. Because there is no negative marking in FMGE, also train yourself to attempt every question; a protocol you half-remember still earns its mark when you commit to an answer.

## Common traps in OBG questions

A few patterns catch students every session. **Drug safety in pregnancy** is the first: know the classic teratogens and the drugs to avoid — ACE inhibitors, warfarin, isotretinoin, certain antiepileptics — and equally, the drugs considered safe, because "all EXCEPT" questions work both ways. The second trap is **timing**: gestational-age cut-offs decide management, from when screening tests are offered to when delivery is planned in pre-eclampsia or a post-term pregnancy, so anchor every protocol to its week. Third, **the first step versus the definitive step**: resuscitation before delivery in eclampsia, stabilisation before caesarean in a shocked patient — stems deliberately offer the definitive treatment as a distractor when the first step is what is asked. Finally, in labour questions, read whether the stem describes the first or second stage and whether the cervix is fully dilated; the correct intervention changes completely on those two facts. Training yourself to underline the gestational age, the stage and the word "first" in every stem removes most of these traps.

## Putting the two halves together

Obstetrics and gynaecology share more than a name. Contraception links to population health, infertility links to endocrinology, cervical screening links to oncology and microbiology (HPV), and pregnancy physiology reaches into Medicine and Pharmacology. When you revise OBG, revise it alongside those neighbours: the same magnesium sulphate appears in pharmacology, the same HPV in microbiology. Students who build these links find that OBG revision quietly strengthens three other subjects — another reason its 30 questions understate its real value in the paper.
