Sit with a difficult chapter for three unbroken hours and you will notice something honest: hour three is not worth what hour one was worth. Your eyes move, the pages turn, and very little lands. That experience is exactly what the research on **study breaks, Pomodoro** timing and the **science** of attention tries to explain — sustained focus is a resource that depletes with use and recovers with rest, and the students who manage that cycle deliberately get more from the same hours than the students who simply endure them. This guide separates what is genuinely supported from what is folklore, and turns both into a workable rhythm for FMGE preparation.

## Study Breaks, Pomodoro and the Science of Attention

The best-established finding in this area is the **vigilance decrement**: on tasks demanding continuous attention, performance declines steadily the longer you go without a break — errors rise, mind-wandering increases, and reading becomes shallow. Short breaks interrupt that decline; after a genuine rest, attention resets close to its starting level. Breaks are therefore not time stolen from studying. They are the mechanism that keeps the remaining study time worth having.

A second, related point: what you do in the break matters. Rest that changes your posture, light and sensory input — standing, walking, looking into the distance, water — restores attention. "Breaks" spent on stimulating feeds usually do not; they swap one demanding input for another and add new threads for your mind to chew on during the next block.

## The Pomodoro Technique: A Useful Tool, Not a Law of Nature

The **Pomodoro technique** is a popular time-boxing method: focus for a set block — classically **25 minutes** — then take a short break of about five minutes, with a longer break after four blocks. It deserves its popularity for two practical reasons: a 25-minute commitment is small enough that starting feels easy even on bad days, and the timer externalises discipline, so you stop negotiating with yourself mid-block.

But treat it as a **technique, not science law**. The 25-minute figure is a convention from the method's creator, not a measured constant of the human brain. Many medical students find 25 minutes too short for deep topics — just as a Pathology mechanism finally clicks, the timer rings. Common adaptations:

- **50/10:** fifty minutes of focus, ten minutes of break. Suits long theory and question-review sessions.
- **90-minute blocks** with a 15–20 minute break, matching the length of a full paper sitting and useful in the mock-heavy final phase.
- **Classic 25/5** for low-energy days, revision sprints and getting started on a dreaded topic.

The right block length is the longest one you can repeat with quality, all day, for months. Experiment for a week each; your error rates and evening recall will tell you more than any article can.

## How Long, How Often, and When to Break

Practical guidance that fits the evidence:

- **Break before you are empty.** If you wait until you are rereading the same line three times, you have already spent twenty minutes at half value. Scheduled breaks beat collapse-driven ones.
- **Short breaks: 5–10 minutes.** Enough to stand, move, hydrate and look far away. Set a return alarm so the break has a wall at the end of it.
- **Long breaks: 20–30 minutes** after three or four blocks — a walk, a meal, daylight. This is also where a daily walk fits naturally into the timetable.
- **Never let the phone define the break's end.** A break that ends "when I finish scrolling" ends late or never. Timers are unglamorous and undefeated.

## The Break Menu: What to Actually Do in Five Minutes

A break fails when it has no plan — you stand up, drift to the phone, and return ten minutes later more scattered than before. Keep a short menu of break activities that genuinely restore, and pick from it without thinking:

- **Move.** Walk to the gate and back, climb a flight of stairs, stretch the neck, shoulders and back that have been holding your study posture.
- **Look far.** Rest your eyes on the most distant point available — a rooftop, a tree, the end of the corridor. Hours of near focus tire the eye muscles; distance relaxes them.
- **Water and light.** Drink a glass of water, and get daylight on your face if the hour allows. Both signal wakefulness to the brain more honestly than another coffee.
- **Breathe and idle.** A few slow breaths by an open window, letting your mind wander without input, is not wasted time — unfocused minutes are when the brain quietly sorts what the last block fed it.

What is not on the menu: feeds, videos and chats. They feel like rest because they are pleasant, but they are new information competing for the same attention you are trying to restore, and they are engineered to overrun the five minutes you gave them. Keep the phone parked until the long break, and even then, end by timer.

## Breaks Inside Practice: Training for the Real Exam

One caution specific to exam preparation: FMGE itself offers no Pomodoro. Each part is **150 questions in one sitting**, so your training must include unbroken sittings that build exam stamina. Use break-structured study for learning and revision days — and keep at least one or two full-length, uninterrupted mocks a week from the [previous-year papers](/papers/) or the [grand test series](/tests/) where you sit the whole part without pausing, exactly as the exam demands. Breaks are how you learn; unbroken mocks are how you prove the learning survives exam conditions. Both belong in the same week.
