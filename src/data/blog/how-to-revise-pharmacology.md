If you are trying to learn **how to revise pharmacology**, the first thing to accept is that the drug-by-drug approach is the problem. Pharmacology has hundreds of drugs, but it does not have hundreds of *ideas*. Almost every drug belongs to a class, every class has a shared mechanism, and the exam mostly tests whether you know the class story plus the handful of members that break it. Revise the classes properly and most individual drugs take care of themselves.

## Why Drug Lists Fail

A list of forty antihypertensives, memorised as names, doses and random facts, has no structure for memory to hold onto. Within weeks the names blur — which one causes a dry cough, which one is safe in pregnancy, which one must never be combined with what. This is predictable: isolated facts decay fastest, and similar facts interfere with each other.

Classes solve both problems. Learn that ACE inhibitors share a mechanism, and you inherit most of their uses, adverse effects and contraindications in one move. Your revision unit becomes "about fifty class stories plus exceptions" instead of "five hundred drug profiles". That is a volume of material a human can actually keep alive with spaced review.

## How to Revise Pharmacology by Class

For every class, work through the same five questions, in the same order, every time:

- **What is the mechanism, in one line?** If you cannot say it in a sentence, you do not know the class yet. Mechanism predicts uses and most adverse effects.
- **Who is the prototype?** Learn one representative drug deeply — usually the oldest or most-used member (propranolol for beta blockers, furosemide for loop diuretics). Other members are then stored as *differences from the prototype*, which is far cheaper than storing them whole.
- **What does the whole class do?** Indications, common adverse effects, common contraindications — the shared profile.
- **Which members are exceptions?** This is where the marks are. The beta blocker with intrinsic sympathomimetic activity, the cephalosporin that does not cover Pseudomonas, the statin interaction that matters. Exams disproportionately test the member that breaks the class rule.
- **What is the one-line exam hook?** Drug of choice for a condition, antidote pairs (naloxone–opioids, flumazenil–benzodiazepines), and the adverse effect so classic it is practically a diagnosis.

## Adverse Effects Are Your Memory Hooks

Students treat adverse effects as a separate list to cram. Used properly, they are the strongest memory anchors in the subject, because they connect to mechanism and to clinical stories. A dry cough points to bradykinin and therefore to ACE inhibitors; ankle swelling points to arteriolar dilatation and therefore to dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers. When a vignette mentions the adverse effect, the class identifies itself.

Build small adverse-effect tables for the classes where confusion is common — antimicrobials and anticancer drugs especially — and revise them as "which drug causes X?" drills rather than paragraphs. Also keep a running list of **contraindication pairs**: drug plus the patient in whom it is dangerous (methotrexate in pregnancy, metformin in severe renal failure, NSAIDs in peptic ulcer disease). These pairs are asked constantly and are cheap to store because each is a small story, not a fact.

A word on mnemonics, because pharmacology attracts them. They have a genuine but narrow use: purely arbitrary lists that no mechanism can generate — the enzyme inducers and inhibitors, for instance, or the drugs excreted unchanged. For those, a mnemonic is a legitimate storage device. The mistake is building your whole revision on them. A mnemonic tells you *which* drugs are on a list and nothing about what they do, when they are dangerous, or how a vignette will disguise them; it also collapses the moment two mnemonics share letters. Treat mnemonics as labels on folders, not as the contents. If you know the class story, most lists shorten themselves — and the few that do not are exactly where a mnemonic earns its place.

## A Revision Rhythm That Keeps Drugs Alive

Pharmacology decays faster than most subjects because so much of it is arbitrary — names and pairings with no logic to reconstruct them from. It therefore needs *more frequent, shorter* contact than a conceptual subject:

- Rotate one class per day in a short recall session — fifteen minutes, closed book, writing the five answers from the framework above.
- Mix old classes into every session. Two new classes plus three old ones beats five new ones, because the old ones are the ones quietly decaying.
- Test with questions early and often. A set on antihypertensives from a [question bank](/qbank/) will expose in ten minutes which exceptions you only *recognised* rather than knew. Recognition feels like knowledge until an exam charges you for it.
- Keep a personal "always confused" list — the pairs you personally mix up (loop vs thiazide electrolyte effects is a common one) — and drill that list weekly. Your confusions are specific to you; generic high-yield lists will not fix them.

When a full paper is on the schedule, [previous-year papers](/papers/) show you the real distribution: which classes FMGE actually asks about, year after year. Let that distribution decide where your revision hours go — antimicrobials, cardiovascular, autonomic and endocrine pharmacology consistently earn their keep.

Q: How many drugs do I actually need to know for FMGE?
A: Far fewer as individual profiles than the textbook suggests. Master roughly fifty class stories with their prototypes, then the exceptions and drug-of-choice facts within each class. That covers the large majority of pharmacology questions asked.
Q: Should I revise pharmacology from a textbook or from notes?
A: Revise from your own class-wise notes or tables, and return to the textbook only for a class whose mechanism you cannot explain in one line. Re-reading whole chapters is the slowest possible use of revision time.
Q: What is the fastest way to learn adverse effects?
A: Attach each classic adverse effect to its mechanism and turn it into a question: "which drug causes this?" Adverse effects learned as diagnostic clues are remembered far better than adverse effects memorised as lists.
Q: How often should pharmacology be revised?
A: More often, in smaller doses, than conceptual subjects — a short recall session most days, rotating classes, with question sets mixed in. Arbitrary names and pairings decay quickly, so frequency beats session length.
