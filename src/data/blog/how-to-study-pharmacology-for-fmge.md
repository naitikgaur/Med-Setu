Ask students which subject feels endless and most will say pharmacology. The drug list genuinely is long — but **pharmacology for FMGE** is far more systematic than it looks. It carries **13 questions** in the paper, and the examiners draw from a predictable pool: mechanisms of action, drugs of choice, adverse effects, contraindications and antidotes. Students who memorise drug-by-drug drown; students who study *classifications and mechanisms* find that one concept answers five questions. This guide shows you how to organise pharmacology so it becomes a scoring subject instead of a burden — including the books, the high-yield systems, and a revision method designed for a subject that evaporates quickly.

## Pharmacology for FMGE: weightage and question style

FMGE is conducted by **NBEMS** — 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, **no negative marking**, and a pass mark of **150 out of 300**. Pharmacology contributes 13 questions, typically:

- **Mechanism of action** — the single most common question type; if you know the mechanism, the uses and adverse effects follow logically
- **Drug of choice** — for infections, emergencies and common conditions
- **Adverse effects and toxicity** — classic side effects, teratogenicity, and the antidote or management
- **Contraindications and interactions** — especially in pregnancy, renal failure and with common drug pairs
- **Newer and named drugs** — monoclonal antibodies and targeted therapies appear as one-liners with their targets

Notice the pattern: almost everything hangs off mechanism and class. That is your study strategy in one line.

## High-yield systems and drug groups

- **Autonomic pharmacology** — cholinergic and adrenergic drugs, organophosphorus poisoning and its management, myasthenia gravis
- **Cardiovascular drugs** — antihypertensives, antianginals, heart failure drugs (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, beta-blockers, newer agents), antiarrhythmics, hypolipidaemics
- **Antimicrobials** — the biggest cluster: beta-lactams, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, antitubercular drugs, antifungals, antivirals, antimalarials — mechanisms, resistance and drug of choice for classic infections
- **Endocrine drugs** — antidiabetics (oral agents and insulins), thyroid and antithyroid drugs, corticosteroids
- **CNS drugs** — antiepileptics, antidepressants, antipsychotics, opioids, sedative-hypnotics, drugs for Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease
- **Autacoids and chemotherapy basics** — NSAIDs, gout drugs, anticancer drug mechanisms and their signature toxicities
- **General pharmacology** — pharmacokinetics (first-pass, half-life, clearance), pharmacodynamics, teratogenicity categories, essential one-liners

## Books that work

- **K.D. Tripathi's Essentials of Medical Pharmacology** — the standard Indian text; its classifications and flowcharts are exactly the format your notes should take
- **Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics** — the reference giant; use it to clarify mechanisms you find confusing, not for a full read
- **Lippincott's Illustrated Reviews: Pharmacology** — outstanding for fast, visual revision of drug classes once your first pass is done

Whichever book you use, your *notes* matter more than the book: one classification chart per system, with mechanism, prototype drug, key uses and signature adverse effects.

## The classification method

- **Never study a drug alone** — always place it in its class, learn the class mechanism once, then learn only what makes each member different
- **Build "DOC tables"** — a running list of *drug of choice for…* entries; revise it weekly, because these questions are direct marks
- **Build an adverse-effect bank** — classic pairings (drug → signature toxicity) and (toxicity → antidote) as flashcards or a two-column table
- **Link to physiology** — if a drug's mechanism feels arbitrary, go back to the receptor physiology; pharmacology is applied physiology, and the link makes it stick
- **Revise in short, frequent cycles** — pharmacology fades faster than pathology; 20 minutes daily on charts beats a 6-hour monthly marathon

Then convert knowledge into exam skill with topic-wise practice from a [question bank](/qbank/) — pharmacology stems often hide the answer in one phrase ("patient on lithium develops…"), and pattern recognition only comes from solving questions.

## Revision plan

- **First pass:** system-wise classifications with mechanisms; DOC and adverse-effect tables started from day one
- **Second pass:** charts and tables only, plus wrong questions; a timed pharmacology test from the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/)
- **Final phase:** daily 20-minute table revision, antidote list, teratogenic drugs list, and full mocks from [previous-year papers](/papers/) to integrate with medicine and microbiology

## Common mistakes that cost pharmacology marks

- **Making notes drug by drug** — a notebook organised as 400 isolated drug entries cannot be revised and cannot be reasoned from. Notes organised by class and mechanism compress the same material into a fraction of the pages and let you derive answers for drugs you barely remember
- **Ignoring general pharmacology** — pharmacokinetics feels abstract, so students rush it; then half-life, loading dose, clearance and bioavailability questions — pure, learnable marks — are lost across every paper
- **Learning uses but not adverse effects** — for many drugs, the tested point is the toxicity, not the indication. Every drug you learn should come with its signature harm and, where relevant, its antidote
- **Never revisiting antimicrobials** — this is the largest and most factual cluster, and it decays quickly. It needs scheduled returns, not one heroic reading
- **Skipping the named newer drugs** — monoclonal antibodies and targeted agents appear as short one-liners asking for the target or the class; a single maintained list of these drugs, revised monthly, converts them from guaranteed losses into easy marks
