Every FMGE aspirant should have one subject they can count on completely, and for most students that subject should be forensic medicine. **Forensic medicine in FMGE** carries only **10 questions**, but few subjects offer such a high return per hour of study: the syllabus is finite, the questions are factual and repetitive in style, and the toxicology half overlaps with pharmacology and medicine. Students lose these marks not because the subject is hard but because they leave it for the last week and read it passively. Give forensic medicine a short, structured pass early, revise it twice, and it will quietly hand you marks that many better-prepared-looking competitors drop. Here is the plan.

## Forensic medicine FMGE: weightage and question style

FMGE is conducted by **NBEMS** as 300 MCQs in two parts of 150, with **no negative marking** and a pass mark of **150 out of 300**. Forensic medicine contributes 10 questions, usually split between:

- **Medical jurisprudence** — Indian legal system basics, consent, medical negligence, professional misconduct, the Indian Evidence Act and courts, death certificates and legal procedures
- **Thanatology and postmortem changes** — signs of death, rigor mortis, algor mortis, livor mortis, decomposition and their medico-legal importance
- **Injuries and wounds** — classification, blunt vs sharp injuries, firearm injuries, and differentiating features examiners love
- **Toxicology** — poisons, their signs, antidotes and medico-legal aspects: organophosphorus, opioids, alcohol, heavy metals, snake bite, corrosives
- **Identification and sexual jurisprudence** — age estimation, dactylography, sexual offences and relevant law, abortion law basics

The questions are direct. If you have revised the fact once recently, you will answer it; there is very little "application" to hide behind.

## High-yield topics to lock first

- **Postmortem changes** — the timeline of rigor, lividity and decomposition is asked in some form in almost every cycle of papers
- **Consent and negligence** — types of consent, age thresholds, criminal negligence (Section 304A IPC context), Bolam test basics, informed consent requirements
- **IPC/BNS sections and acts** — key sections related to medical practice, the POCSO Act basics, the MTP Act conditions, the Transplantation of Human Organs Act essentials
- **Injuries** — abrasion vs contusion vs laceration, incised vs lacerated wounds, firearm entry vs exit, defence wounds, hesitation cuts
- **Toxicology favourites** — organophosphorus compounds (signs and management), opioid poisoning (the classic triad), methanol (blindness, antidotes), copper sulphate, aluminium phosphide, snake envenomation features, alcohol stages and withdrawal
- **Thanatology extras** — mummification, adipocere, cadaveric spasm, suspended animation — one-liner territory
- **Identification** — Gustafson's method, ossification centres for age, fingerprint patterns and their permanence

## The one book you need

- **K.S. Narayan Reddy's The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology** — the standard text for Indian students; its medico-legal chapters and toxicology sections map directly onto the FMGE question style
- Supplement with your **own one-liner notebook** — forensic medicine is a one-liner subject; every fact you extract in question–answer form is a future mark

Do not read Reddy cover to cover at novel speed. Read chapter, extract one-liners, move on. The notebook is the asset.

## Study method: short, sharp, repeated

- **Finish the subject in days, not weeks** — a focused 4–6 day pass is realistic for the full syllabus at FMGE depth
- **Extract, don't highlight** — write every testable fact as a one-line question and answer; passive highlighting fails badly in this subject
- **Toxicology as a table** — poison | source | key signs | fatal dose concept | antidote/management: one table for the whole of toxicology, revised weekly
- **Law as a list** — acts, sections and age thresholds in a single list; these are pure-recall marks
- **Solve the whole topic set early** — work through forensic questions from a [question bank](/qbank/) right after your first pass; the question style repeats so closely that practice quickly reveals the finite fact pool

Because the subject is small, it is also the perfect "confidence subject" to revise the day before a full mock — quick wins that put you in a scoring mindset.

## Revision plan

- **First pass (4–6 days):** Reddy chapter-wise with one-liner extraction; toxicology table and law list built alongside
- **Second pass (1–2 days):** notebook and tables only; full forensic set from the [subject-wise tests](/subjects/) under time
- **Final phase:** 30-minute refreshers of the notebook before each mock in the [grand test series](/tests/); the whole subject should take under two hours to revise completely by the end

## Common mistakes that cost forensic medicine marks

- **Leaving it for the last week** — the subject is short, but ten questions absorbed in a panic are worth half of ten questions absorbed calmly with two revisions. Forensic medicine should be one of the first subjects you *finish*, precisely because finishing it is so achievable
- **Reading without extracting** — a highlighted Reddy chapter is not knowledge. This subject pays only for facts you can produce on demand, which is why the one-liner notebook, not the textbook, is what toppers revise from
- **Mixing up timelines and thresholds** — rigor mortis stages, age limits for consent, and legal time limits are asked precisely because students blur them. Keep them in separate, labelled lists and revise them as lists
- **Skipping toxicology because it overlaps pharmacology** — the overlap is the opportunity: the same poisons appear in both subjects, so one good toxicology table earns marks twice
